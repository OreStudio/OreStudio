* Goal

An instrument is identified by its trade, so the model carries ORE's two identities — trade and security — and no third.

* Acceptance

- instrument_id is gone from instrument_identity_field_group and from every entity that inherits or references it.
- trade.instrument_id is gone, so the two directions of a one-to-one relationship cannot disagree.
- Instrument tables are keyed by trade_id, not null, with a real foreign key to the trade rather than a soft link.
- An instrument with no real trade behind it is represented by a trade of a reserved type, so there is one shape rather than two.
- The ORE round trip stays at zero loss and the import planner still links every instrument to its trade.

* Plan

** What ORE has

=componentTrade= is a =TradeType=, an =Envelope=, =TradeActions= and
the trade-data group, with one identity: the =id= attribute. No
instrument concept is named anywhere in =instruments.xsd=. The
instrument /is/ the trade data. The only other identity is
=SecurityId=, and that names a security in reference data.

So ORE has two identities, trade and security. We have three.

** What we have instead

=trade.instrument_id= is a nullable uuid pointing at the instrument,
and the instrument's =trade_id= is a nullable uuid pointing back.
Neither is a foreign key: the trades table carries an index and the
instrument's own comment calls its side a soft link.

A one-to-one relationship is therefore modelled as two nullable
pointers facing each other, with nothing able to guarantee they agree.
Trade A can name instrument X while instrument X names trade B, and
the database will accept it.

The trade-less case the nullability exists for is never exercised. The
import planner always sets =trade_id=, and =planner_import_planner_tests=
requires it.

** Why this blocks the security split

Three identities is why fields drift. With trade, instrument and
security there is no obvious home for a field, and =instrument= sits
in the middle collecting things that belong at either end — which is
how a bond future's contract terms and a bond's coupon schedule ended
up stored once per trade. With two, the question answers itself: a
field is keyed by the trade or by the security.

Doing the security split first would mean reshaping the same tables
twice.

** The change

=instrument_identity_field_group= is the leverage point: it declares
=instrument_id=, =trade_type_code=, =party_id= and =trade_id=, and 27
models inherit it. Removing =instrument_id= there and making
=trade_id= the non-nullable key carries most of the change. The
remaining entities keyed on =instrument_id= — the per-product and
child tables, 47 in all — take the renamed key.

=trade.instrument_id= goes with it. The link is the child's
=trade_id=, and a real foreign key replaces the soft one.

An instrument with no real trade gets a trade of a reserved type
rather than a null link, so every instrument is reached the same way.

** Sequence

Model first, then regenerate, then the hand-written callers, then the
SQL migration. Nothing removes a column before its replacement exists.
The component audit runs before the task closes.

** A finding to carry separately

The generated C++ in =ores.trading= carries no generated marker, where
the generated SQL says so plainly. Nothing tells a reader that
=domain/ascot.hpp= is codegen output, which is how a generated file
gets hand-edited. It also made this change hard to size: of 815 files
referencing =instrument_id=, the split between generated and
hand-written cannot be read off the files.
