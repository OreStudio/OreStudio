#+title: Precedence among the levels of reference data
#+description: How a datum is stated at more than one level, which statement a read prefers, and why a copy is never the answer.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :knowledge:architecture:reference_data:tenancy:precedence:
#+created: 2026-11-25
#+updated: 2026-11-25

* Summary

A reference datum may be stated at the installation, at the system tenant, at a
tenant or at a party, and where one datum is stated at two levels the system
decides between the statements at read rather than by copying one into the
other. The precedence is fixed. A read acts for a level, takes the statement of
the deepest level that holds the key, and falls back to the next wider level;
the wider statement stays where it lies, and no level edits it. Four shapes put
that rule to work, and each answers a different question about who owns the
difference between the levels. A copy is the one shape that ends the precedence,
and it is chosen only when the narrower level owns the value outright.

* Detail

** Why a datum is stated once

Two copies of one datum diverge as soon as a person changes the first, and no
reader can then say which of the two is authoritative. The architecture
therefore permits one copy of a shared row and holds it in one tenant; the rule
and the levels are stated in
[[id:7287B58F-964C-4D42-8EC4-6C5DA53EEE2D][Reference data classification by publication level]],
and this page takes them as given. What that page does not decide is the case
this page does: a narrower level needs a different value for one key of a datum
the wider level states.

The answer to that need is not a second copy of the datum. A narrower level
states the difference and only the difference. The read resolves the two
statements, and the wider statement remains the statement of every key the
narrower level does not name. A copy, by contrast, ends the connection: a tenant
provisioned with the system tenant's tenors keeps those rows for good, and a
later change to the system tenant's rows reaches a new tenant and no existing
one ([[id:99481EA8-1D2B-426D-A4E6-5E834FF5FFCE][Publish conventions from data quality]], step 9).

** The levels

The level is the scope a statement is made for, and it is one of four. The
first three are the ownership classes of
[[id:5A5D6490-AF64-4AAF-8B3D-48EF3EDAF3D2][Tenant ownership and the information flow]];
the fourth subdivides a tenant, and a row names it in =party_id=.

| Level          | What it states                                          | Who may write it                          | An example |
|----------------+---------------------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------+------------|
| The installation | Facts about the deployment itself                     | The installation's own processes          | The grid and node samples, the telemetry logs |
| The system tenant | The platform and world catalogues, and the derived sets every tenant reads | The platform's publish functions | The provider catalogue, the countries, the calendars |
| A tenant       | Its own business data                                   | The tenant's people and the services acting for it | Its parties, books and trades |
| A party        | Its own departure from a tenant-wide statement          | The party's people                        | Its calendar exceptions |

A statement at a level does not carry the value of the level above it. The
system tenant's calendar holds the rules of a calendar; a party's exception
holds one day that departs from those rules. The two are not the same statement
written twice, and the read is what joins them.

** The four shapes

| Shape                  | What the narrower level writes              | What the read does                                    | Where it is used |
|------------------------+---------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+-----------------|
| Read where the row lies | Nothing                                    | Takes the wider row as it stands                      | The countries, the currencies, the calendars, the calendar dates |
| The override           | A row under the same key                    | Prefers the narrower row and leaves the wider one     | A tenant's script of a world script's name |
| The overlay            | Only the difference, at a coarser grain     | Reads both and applies the narrower over the wider    | A party's calendar exceptions |
| The copy at publication| A row of its own, published from one artefact | Reads its own copy and consults no other level      | A party's instrument conventions |

*** Read where the row lies

The widest shape holds when no narrower level owns a different value. The model
states the shape in two places: =:rls_own_or_system_tenant_rows:= on the SQL side emits
a read policy of =tenant_id = current or tenant_id = system= with writes
confined to the caller's tenant, and =:system_tenant_visible:= in the model's
=* Repository= drawer makes every generated read carry the same comparison and
adds the own-tenant clause to the delete rule. A table whose rows are all shared
states =:tenant_read_scope: shared= and its read carries no tenant predicate at
all. [[id:0EAAEDFD-7954-4649-A463-9EE377B023F7][Data read across tenants]]
states the six building blocks this shape uses, and the checklist a new shared
table follows.

The calendar dates table is the case this shape is being applied to. Its days
are derived from the installation's calendar templates and exceptions, so they
belong once under the system tenant, and a tenant is to read that copy where it
lies. The read is in place; moving the materialisation into the system tenant is
the next step of the task that applies this page.

*** The override

The narrower level states a row under the key the wider level already states,
and the read prefers it. The trading script library is the case. A script of the
world library is held by the system tenant; a tenant that publishes a script of
the same name holds its own row, and the trade that names the script resolves to
the tenant's row. The resolution is a precedence and not a replacement: the world
library keeps its row, a later release of ORE that adds the name leaves the
tenant's script in force, and the tenant's row is read first
([[id:CCA8090C-26E0-4571-84BA-9115C110376C][Versioning of the script library]]).
The repository states the order in one place, and the read returns one row.

The same rule at the grain of a value is the manual correction of a derived
market data set. A hand value holds its coordinate against every later automatic
write, and a read at an as-of prefers the hand value to a value an automatic
writer produced later. The correction is not a lock, because the feed keeps
publishing; the read decides which of the two it shows, and clearing the
correction restores the automatic value
([[id:C1C37A35-7DE8-4FD2-8A2F-E7E2CF08EED4][Manual correction of a derived market data set]]).

*** The overlay

The narrower level states only the difference, at a coarser grain than the wider
statement. The installation states one row for a calendar day and every party
follows it; a party that closes the day, or opens a day the rules close, states
one exception row for that date, and states no rule
([[id:8B6E1C53-871E-4B6D-BD20-CB3F551B5C46][ores.refdata.calendar_exception]]).
A read for the party takes the installation's exceptions beside the party's, and
the materialisation applies the party's row over the installation's on the date
the two name.

The overlay requires that both statements exist, so the natural key of the table
carries the level: one row for the installation and one for each party, for the
same calendar and date. A key that leaves the level out permits one statement
for all parties, which is the opposite of the shape.

*** The copy at publication

The narrower level is given a row of its own, and it owns that row outright. The
instrument conventions are published this way: =system_core= publishes the system
party's copy and =party_essentials= publishes each other party's, from one
artefact dataset
([[id:7287B58F-964C-4D42-8EC4-6C5DA53EEE2D][Reference data classification by publication level]]).
The copy is the right shape when the narrower level must be able to change the
value and the wider level must not reach it afterwards. Its limit is the one this
page opened with: a change at the wide level does not reach the copy, and the
reader cannot tell from the row that a newer statement exists.

** The order a read applies

A read names the datum and the level it acts for. It takes the statement of that
level when the level holds one, and otherwise the statement of the next wider
level that does. In the overlay shape both statements apply, and the narrower is
applied over the wider; the wider is still read, because it is the statement of
every key the narrower level does not name.

** What the shapes forbid

- *A level edits another level's row.* A tenant writes its own rows and never the system tenant's, and the policy of a shared table confines every write to the caller's tenant.
- *A copy stands in for a precedence.* A second copy of a shared row is the defect the publication levels exist to prevent.
- *A read returns two statements for one key.* The caller would have to choose between them, and no two callers choose alike. The read states the precedence, or the table holds one statement per key and level.
- *The order the store returns rows in decides the precedence.* The exceptions fold that applies a party's row over the installation's must state the order it applies them in, or be given them in that order by the read. Row order is not a decision the store makes.
- *A level states a value that another level derives.* The derived set is materialised once, and the narrower level states only its departure from it.

** Applying the rule: the calendar dates

The calendar dates show the four shapes working together, and the case is the one
the rule was written for. A holiday is not the same on every desk: a party in
Hong Kong closes on days a party in the United Kingdom opens, and both parties
sit in one installation.

The dates are derived, so they belong to the installation and are held once in
the system tenant, which is the first shape. The exceptions are the party's
departure, which is the overlay. The rule that joins them is a read for a party:
the installation's exceptions apply to every party, the party's own apply beside
them, and the party's row decides the date the two name. Two parties therefore
read different answers on one date, from one copy of the days and two rows of
difference.

The task that moves the dates into the system tenant records one lesson about the
mechanism. The read the shape needs is not a custom read. The two model flags
above generate it, and a table that declares them is readable across the levels
without any hand-written query. The hand-written read is needed only where the
read names an axis the generator does not have, as the party axis of the
exceptions' read.
