This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A tenant administrator verifies that a newly provisioned party has the conventions its runs will read.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I have just provisioned a party. Its runs need market terms from the first
minute. Do they have them, and how would I know if they did not?"

This journey is a verification and not a fill. A party's conventions are not
something a person assembles: the provisioning run publishes the
=party_essentials= bundle once per party, and that bundle's first member is the
conventions dataset. So the correct answer to "how do I give the new party its
conventions" is "you do not — the run did it". The journey exists because a
person still needs to prove it, and because the one state that goes wrong here
goes wrong silently.

The journey is done when the person can state, for the party they just created,
how many documents of each kind it holds, which of those belong to the tenant
rather than the party, and what a run of that party would write today.

* Actor and trigger

The actor is a tenant administrator, or the person operating provisioning for
the tenant. They hold the tenant's permissions in full and they act inside their
own tenant. The trigger is a party that has just been created — by a starting
point's =provision_party= operation, by the Acme demo profile, or by an
administrator creating one outside a plan — and a run that either has not been
made yet or has produced figures nobody has checked.

The journey starts when the party exists and ends when the person has read its
conventions back, or has found that it has none and knows what would fill them.

This is a verification journey. In the normal case it writes nothing: the party
provisioning already wrote the rows. It reaches one write only in the abnormal
case, and only by handing the work to
[[id:99481EA8-1D2B-426D-A4E6-5E834FF5FFCE][Publish conventions from data
quality]], which is the same publish the provisioning run made.

The journey keeps no server state. The provisioning run does: it is a workflow
instance, and its steps are where the person reads what the run actually did.

* Steps

1. *Read that the conventions are part of provisioning, and not a step after it.*
   The person opens the provisioning run and reads the party plan's step 30,
   =provision_party=, whose argument is ={"bundles": ["party_essentials"]}=. The
   bundle states what it carries: "The reference data a single party needs: its
   instrument conventions and its CRM topology. Published once per party with a
   ={"party_id": "..."}= param." The person reads that the conventions and the CRM
   topology are the same operation, and that neither is a manual fill. In the
   Acme demo profile the four operational parties reach this step through the
   same plan, after the LEI hierarchy import and before staff are loaded.
2. *Read the publish the plan's step performs, and in what order.* The bundle
   publishes its members in dependency order, one workflow step per dataset. The
   conventions dataset, =ore.conventions=, is the bundle's member at display
   order 5, before the CRM topology at 20. The step's command subject is
   =refdata.v1.ops.publish_conventions_from_dq= and its parameters carry the
   party's id. The person reads the step's counts, not a summary: the first
   party's publish writes 2,188 rows, and every later party's publish writes
   2,155 and skips 33.
3. *Read the 33 as already-present and not as a failure.* 2,188 is 2,155 plus 33.
   The 33 are the tenant-scoped kinds that the first party's publish wrote:
   =ibor_index= with 10 and =overnight_index= with 23. They carry no =party_id=,
   so they are the tenant's rows and every later party's publish correctly finds
   them present. A person who reads 33 without this account reads a defect. The
   screen states the two kinds and the two counts beside the skip.
4. *Read the party's own total against what it should be.* The person reads the
   party's conventions back, kind by kind. A party published from the bundle
   holds 2,155 rows across the 22 party kinds, and reads a further 216 across the
   four tenant kinds — =currency_pair= 179, =overnight_index= 23, =ibor_index= 10
   and =tenor= 4 — which belong to the tenant and are shared with every other
   party. So the party's own count and the number of documents a run of that
   party reads are different numbers, and the screen shows both.
5. *Read why the tenant kinds were there before the party was.* Of the four,
   two arrived differently from the rest and the person reads why.
   - =currency_pair= is published into the tenant by the =base= bundle, which is
     a tenant publication and not a party one, so its 179 rows exist before any
     party is provisioned.
   - =tenor='s 4 rows are not published at all. They are copied from the system
     tenant by =ores_iam_provision_tenant_fn= when the tenant is created, with
     =system.new_record= and the commentary "Copied from the system tenant during
     provisioning", and they are never refreshed afterwards.
   The screen states which of the four came from where, because "the run did it"
   is true of 2,155 of the rows and of none of the 33's second and later parties.
6. *Read what a run of this party would write today.* The person asks the
   question the party was provisioned to answer: what file does a run of this
   party carry? The export reads the party's whole conventions document, kind by
   kind, and writes it as the run's =conventionsFile=. For a party published from
   the bundle that file holds the party's 2,155 rows and the tenant's 216. The
   person reads that the four conventions configurations ACME's definitions bind
   do not enter into it, and that every definition of the party writes the same
   file.
7. *Read the one party that has nothing, and what that means.* The person creates
   a party outside the plan, or reads a party whose =provision_party= step failed.
   It holds nothing for the 22 party kinds, because the bundle's publish never
   ran for it. It still reads the tenant's 216 for the four tenant kinds, so the
   party is not empty and the emptiness is easy to miss. Its runs write a
   =conventionsFile= with no rows for 22 kinds. Nothing in the tree refuses such
   a run and nothing says the party was skipped. This is the state the journey
   exists to catch, and the screen marks it plainly rather than drawing an empty
   table with no explanation.
8. *Fill the gap with the operation provisioning already used, not with a
   hand-written set.* The person who has to fix a party nobody published for
   sends the same publish provisioning sends:
   =dq.v1.ops.publish_bundle= for =party_essentials= with the party's id, or the
   dataset alone with =dq.v1.ops.publish_datasets=. That publish writes the
   2,155 rows and reads the 33 as skips, exactly as it does inside provisioning.
   The person does not author 2,155 rows and does not choose documents:
   [[id:99481EA8-1D2B-426D-A4E6-5E834FF5FFCE][Publish conventions from data
   quality]] owns that act and this journey hands to it.
9. *Leave, or go on.* The party is verified. The person either reads its
   conventions in detail
   ([[id:0DC107D4-170D-478B-9476-570343806888][See the conventions a party
   uses]]), or corrects a term the party needs differently from the curated set
   ([[id:FA1CC25B-9745-4172-BAE8-4B6413958077][Change the convention a party
   uses for one kind]]).

* Screens and wireframes

One screen: the party and the provisioning run in the header, the publish step
with its four counts, and the per-kind read of what the party holds and what it
shares with the tenant. The state that matters is drawn once and drawn plainly:
a party with no party-scoped conventions is marked as unpublished, with what a
run would write and which operation would fill it.

The screen does not offer a hand-entry path for the 2,155 rows. A screen that
offered one would be teaching a person to do by hand what the plan does, which
is how a party ends up with 2,155 rows that match no dataset and no provenance.

* Prototype

No prototype is written for this journey in this pass. The journey is a
verification and its open questions are all about reads the server does not
offer — a per-party expected count, a record that a party's conventions were
published, and an attribution of the skip count. A prototype would draw three
panels and fake all three numbers.

What a prototype must draw when the reads exist is stated here: the publish
step's four counts, the party's own total beside the tenant's shared total, the
per-kind table with the owning scope marked, and the unpublished party as its own
state with the operation that fills it.

The provisioning engine dispatches every plan step to one subject,
=iam.v1.ops.provision_tenant_step=, and the publish operation is one of the
operations that subject performs. The publish step's own command subject is
=refdata.v1.ops.publish_conventions_from_dq=, which is what the workflow step
records and what the person reads.

No BFF route serves this screen. This journey adds one, for example
=GET /api/refdata/parties/<party-id>/conventions/verification=, which returns
the party's per-kind counts, the tenant's counts for the four shared kinds, the
expected total for a party published from =party_essentials=, and the last
publish step's four counts.

* What is missing

1. *Nothing records that a party's conventions were published.* The publish's
   only trace is the workflow step's =result_json=, and the conventions rows
   themselves say =system.external_data_import= whoever wrote them. So the
   question "did this party's conventions arrive" is answered by counting rows and
   comparing against a number the person has to know. A per-party marker, or a
   publication record keyed by party and dataset, would answer it directly.
2. *No read states a party's expected convention count.* The 2,155 is a fact
   about the curated dataset, derivable only by counting the artefact tables. A
   party is verified against a number the person computed elsewhere. No operation
   returns "this dataset should place these rows for this party".
3. *A party the plan never reached is indistinguishable from a healthy one at a
   glance.* It holds 216 tenant rows and no party rows, and every per-kind list
   answers successfully with no rows. Nothing in the server reports the party as
   unpublished, so the screen must derive "unpublished" from an empty per-kind
   list and an expected count it does not have.
4. *A run for an unpublished party is not refused.* The export writes a
   conventions file with no rows for the 22 party kinds and the run proceeds. What
   ORE does with such a file is ORE's affair, and nothing in the tree stops the
   run or warns the person. A precondition on the run, or a validation at export,
   is the missing guard.
5. *The 33 are unattributed on the receipt.* The publish returns one
   =records_skipped= and the person must know that two tenant-scoped kinds account
   for it. The same gap
   [[id:99481EA8-1D2B-426D-A4E6-5E834FF5FFCE][Publish conventions from data
   quality]] records, seen from the party end.
6. *`refdata::conventions:read` is held by services and not by the person
   verifying.* It is held by =OreService= and =ReportRun=, so the one read that
   answers "what does this party's document hold" is closed to the tenant
   administrator who is verifying it, and they read 26 lists instead.
7. *`tenor` is refreshed for no tenant after the first.* Its 4 rows come from a
   one-time copy at tenant creation. A tenant provisioned before a tenor
   correction keeps the old rows forever and nothing says so; the only way to
   refresh them is to write them with the per-kind =put=.
8. *A starting point other than =acme_demo= may not name the bundle.*
   =party_essentials= is named by the plans that have one, and a party created
   outside a plan is published for by nobody. Nothing checks that a party has been
   through a plan that names the bundle, and nothing reports a party that has not.
