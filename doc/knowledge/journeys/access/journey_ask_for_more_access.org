This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A member asks for a role beyond the one registration gave them, and a tenant administrator answers.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I cannot do my job with the access I have." The member asks for a role they do
not hold. The journey is done when the request has an answer the member can
read: the role is now theirs, or the request was refused and the refusal says
why.

* Actor and trigger

Two people act on one request, at different times. The requester is a member: an
account of type =user=, signed in inside one tenant, reading their own record.
The trigger is a refusal — a screen answers "permission denied" — or a task the
member cannot finish. It starts at *My access*, which already lists the roles
they hold, and it ends when they read the answer.

The answerer is a tenant administrator: an account inside the same tenant that
holds =iam::roles:assign=. The trigger is a request that is waiting. It starts
at the tenant's request queue and it ends when the role is granted, or when the
request is refused.

This is one journey with two actors, because it is one request with one
lifecycle and each actor owns one end of it. The screens stay apart: the member
never sees the queue, and the administrator never sees the ask. The
administrator's unprompted version of the same write is
[[id:3C753578-BC0E-433C-92B5-38031F050ADC][Change someone's access]]; here the
write answers a request instead of starting one.

A request waits on somebody, so this is the case the
[[id:CA94C762-B0C8-40E7-85CE-D4C6355023CC][user_journey contract]] calls
server-side state. The design first put that state in
[[id:FDF3C079-4E18-4DB7-911F-7691AD1F5665][ores.workflow]], as an instance that
suspends until somebody answers and resumes when they do. It was landed a
different way, and the difference is worth stating because it is the design
this document now describes.

A request is a record of its own in =ores.inbox=, not a step of a run. It has a
kind, which says who may decide it and how long it waits; a state; the person
who asked; and the reason. Answering it writes a decision beside it, and the
two together are its history. Nothing suspends and nothing resumes, because
there is no run to suspend: the request is the thing that waits.

Two things followed from that. The engine needed no waiting state, no terminal
state per answer and no "a person decides" step kind, because the waiting is a
row. And the detail of what was asked for stayed with the component that owns
it: an =iam.role_grant= request is an inbox request plus an IAM row naming the
account and the roles, so IAM keeps its own record of a grant and the inbox
keeps the queue every kind shares.

The member's rail is not resumable across a reload — only the request is.

* Steps

** The member asks

1. *Open my access.* The member opens *My access*. The system reads the roles
   they hold, and beside them the roles the tenant offers that they do not hold.
   The first list is =iam.v1.ops.get_my_roles= and the second is
   =iam.v1.roles.list=, filtered to the roles the tenant says may be asked for.
2. *Read what a role grants.* The member opens a role and reads the permissions
   it bundles, so the ask is an informed one. The read is
   =iam.v1.ops.get_role_permissions=.
3. *Ask for the role.* The member picks a role and writes why they need it. The
   ask is =iam.v1.ops.ask_for_roles=, which is IAM's because the roles asked for
   are IAM's to validate; it records the account and the roles and raises the
   inbox request with =inbox.v1.ops.raise_approval=. IAM refuses a role the
   tenant has not put on offer. This is the step that leaves somebody waiting,
   so the request is written down rather than kept in the browser.
4. *Wait.* The member leaves. *My access* shows the request as waiting, with what
   was asked for and when. The roles they hold do not change: a request is not a
   grant. The member reads what they have asked for with
   =inbox.v1.ops.list_my_approval_requests=, and may withdraw a request with
   =inbox.v1.ops.withdraw_approval=, which closes it.
5. *Read the answer.* The member returns to *My access*, or follows a notice, and
   reads the answer. What they have been told is
   =inbox.v1.ops.list_my_notifications=. An approval shows the role among the
   roles they hold. A refusal shows the reason the administrator wrote.
6. *Leave.* The request is closed, and it stays in the member's history. A
   request nobody answers is not the member's to close: the tenant's deployment
   closes it on a schedule, and the member is told the same way.

** The administrator answers

7. *Read the queue.* The administrator opens *Requests* and reads what the
   tenant's members have asked for: who, which role, why, and how long it has
   waited. The system lists the requests waiting in the tenant, oldest first,
   with =inbox.v1.ops.list_approval_queue=.
8. *Weigh the request.* The administrator opens one request and reads the member,
   the role, the reason, and the permissions the role bundles. The last of these
   is what turns a role name into a decision.
9. *Decide.* The administrator approves or refuses, with
   =inbox.v1.ops.decide_approval=. An approval grants the role to the member
   through =iam.v1.ops.assign_role=, the ordinary assignment path, so the
   =account_role= row carries the same audit tail every grant carries, and the
   request closes as approved. A refusal writes the reason the member will read,
   and the request closes as refused. An administrator who is the requester is
   refused: four eyes means the answer comes from somebody else.
10. *Leave.* The request leaves the waiting list. The grant takes effect at the
    member's next sign-in, as every assignment does. The request does not
    disappear: it stays in the queue's answered tail for as long as the
    installation says, so the administrator sees what they answered, and the
    notice that links back to it opens.

The ask and the decision are final steps: there is no Back past a request that
has been made, or past a decision that has been given. A member who needs the
same role again asks again, and the second request is a new one.

* Screens and wireframes

Two screens, one per actor. The member's screen is *My access*, extended by two
panels: the ask, which pairs a role picker with the reason, and the requests,
which lists what the member has asked for and how each request ended. The four
panels answer one question — what may I do, and what have I asked for — so they
belong on one screen rather than on a screen of their own.

#+caption: Ask for more access: the roles the member holds, the ask, and the requests the member has made.
#+attr_html: :width 100%
[[proj:doc/knowledge/journeys/access/wireframe_ask_for_more_access.png]]

The administrator's screen is the queue. It shows the requests waiting in the
tenant, the selected request in full, and the decision. The decision is one
panel because the administrator reads the reason and answers at once; a request
that needs research is left in the queue rather than half-answered.

An answered request leaves the waiting list, but it does not leave the screen.
The notice that says what happened links back to the request, and a request
that vanished would give the administrator nothing to look at, so the queue
carries the requests answered within a window below the waiting ones. How long
that window is, is the installation's, as the variability system setting
=inbox.approval_queue.answered_window_seconds=.

#+caption: Answer a request: the tenant's waiting requests, the selected request with what the role grants, and the decision.
#+attr_html: :width 100%
[[proj:doc/knowledge/journeys/access/wireframe_answer_a_request.png]]

The queue is the Access group's screen, beside the roster rather than inside it.
[[id:99ABD4E6-A69E-4D3F-902D-A69EBE2A565F][See who has access]] answers who
is in the tenant; this screen answers what they have asked for.

What was built is these two screens. The member's is =MyAccessPage=, with the
ask in =AskForRoleDialog= and the member's own list in =MyRequests=. The
administrator's is =RequestsPage=, which opens a request in =RequestDetailPage=
and shows where it stands with =RequestStateChip=. The panels are where the
design put them; what the design did not settle, and the build did, is that a
request is a record the inbox reads rather than an instance both screens watch.

The request is not an IAM record, and it is not a step of a run either. A
request waits on a person, so it carries the waiting itself: =kind_code= says
what it is and therefore who may decide it, =requested_by= is the member,
=reason= is why, =state_code= is where it has got to — waiting, approved,
refused, withdrawn or expired — and =expires_at= is when it stops waiting.

The kind is what makes the queue one queue. It names the permission a decider
must hold and the deadline a request takes, so the inbox can offer the whole
tenant's requests without knowing what a role grant is. The detail of *this*
kind is IAM's: =role_grant_request= names the account and one row per role
asked for, keyed by the request. IAM validates the ask against the roles the
tenant has put on offer, and that is a rule about roles, which is IAM's to
state.

No credential appears on either screen, and nothing either screen carries is a
secret: a password, the material a password is checked against, a one-time-secret
seed and a service's own secret all stay on the server. The member's ask shows
the roles on offer, and the administrator's decision shows the reason — neither
needs any of them.

The ask is two subjects because two components own a part of it. IAM owns the
question "may this role be asked for", so =iam.v1.ops.ask_for_roles= is IAM's;
the inbox owns waiting, so the request it raises is the inbox's. Nothing
suspends, so nothing follows a run's steps.

The story of a request is read through an operation of its own for the same
reason the roles are. The entity read of approval requests and the generic
history read both gate on the administrator's permission, so the person who
raised a request cannot read their own request's versions through either; and
the only place a granted role is tied to the request that asked for it is the
junction row IAM marks applied. =inbox.v1.ops.get_approval_history= is entitled
to the person who raised the request, so a member reads their own trail and a
request they did not raise is answered as absent.

The roles a request asks for are read through an operation rather than through
the generated read of =role_grant_request_role=. That read is gated on
=iam::role_grant_request_roles:read= and IAM isolates those rows by tenant
alone, so granting it would let any member read what every other member asked
for. =iam.v1.ops.get_request_roles= is entitled to the person who raised the
request and to whoever may read the roles of any, so one read serves the
member's own screen and the administrator's queue, and a caller entitled to
neither is told the request does not exist.

=iam.v1.ops.assign_role= checks =iam::roles:assign= before it writes, so the
administrator's decision carries the check the member's ask must not.
=inbox.v1.ops.expire_overdue_approvals= is the one subject here no person calls:
the scheduler fires it, so it presents no session, and it closes only what is
already past its deadline, which is why that is affordable. The reads a person
makes of their own requests and notices are the inbox's, and each is scoped to
the person the session names.

* The seven gaps, and where each stands

This document was written before anything was built, so it listed seven things
the journey needed and did not have. All seven are closed. The
list is kept because a reader arriving from the older text will have it in mind,
and because two of the seven were closed by changing the design rather than by
building what the gap asked for.

| # | Gap | State | What answered it |
|---+-----+-------+------------------|
| 1 | No request subject exists | closed | =iam.v1.ops.ask_for_roles= asks, and the inbox's operations raise, list, withdraw and decide. The approval needed no new write, so =iam.v1.ops.assign_role= is still the grant. |
| 2 | The workflow engine cannot wait on a person | closed, by design | Nothing had to suspend. An approval request is its own record with a state, so =ores.workflow= was never the holder and needed no waiting state. |
| 3 | The requester may answer their own request | closed | The four-eyes guard refuses a decision by the person who asked, so an administrator cannot escalate through a request of their own. |
| 4 | Nothing says which roles may be asked for | closed | =is_requestable= on the role row, in the shape =is_registration_default= already had. IAM refuses a role the tenant has not put on offer. |
| 5 | Nothing tells either person that something happened | closed, and larger than this journey | Notifications are a record of their own in =ores.inbox=, with a kind per kind of event, raised to a person or to whoever holds a permission. |
| 6 | No query returns a request as a row | closed, by design | The request became a table, so the queue is a read of it rather than a projection over an instance's payload. |
| 7 | A request that nobody answers waits forever | closed | The kind carries a deadline, the request takes it, and a scheduled sweep closes what nobody answered. A second sweep, on its own schedule and its own window, warns the people who may answer a request that is close to running out. |

The deadline and its warning are two behaviours, and they are built as two: the
sweep that closes a queue that stopped moving is housekeeping, and the sweep
that warns somebody it is about to stop moving is a nudge with its own timing
and its own audience. Both are the installation's to schedule, and both are
described in [[id:F06175E8-CA73-4A21-BEC5-898D0A388E17][Approval request
expiry]]. The warning is raised once per request, and
[[id:0FE6BDD9-EB24-4B07-8B0C-D816AAF1BEBF][the notice]] is what says so.
