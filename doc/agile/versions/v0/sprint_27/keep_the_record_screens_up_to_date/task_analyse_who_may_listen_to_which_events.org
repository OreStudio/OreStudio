* Goal

A session must hear only the changes of the tenant and the parties it may see. A published entity event names no tenant or party, in its subject or its payload, and ores.qt does not filter either. Analyse how events reach a session today, what each option costs, and recommend one: a tenant and party header from the generated registrar, the tenant in the subject, a NATS account or permission per tenant, or a check in the BFF before it relays. State what the options mean for a party-scoped reader.

* Notes

** How an event travels today

1. A trigger on the table sends =pg_notify= with =event_id=, =entity=, =key=,
   =action=, =version=, =occurred_at= and =tenant_id=. The tenant is there.
2. =postgres_event_source= reads the notification. It gives the mapping the
   notification, and the mapping publishes the typed event on the bus. It also
   publishes an =entity_change_event= that keeps the tenant, but only in process.
3. The registrar's bus handler receives the typed event. The typed event has no
   tenant. =publish_entity_event= sends it to
   =component.v1.collection_events.action= with no headers.
4. The BFF has one connection for listening (=eventClient=). It is not signed in.
   It subscribes to =component.v1.collection_events.>=.
5. =ChangeEventRegistry= keys its subscriptions by tenant. The subject does not
   change with the tenant. Two tenants therefore open two subscriptions to the same
   subject, and each receives the events of every tenant. The key shares work. It
   filters nothing.
6. The browser receives =entity-changed {component, entity, at}=.

Tenant is lost in step 3. Party is never in the notification, even for the three
party-owned tables (=party_identifiers=, =counterparty_identifiers=,
=party_id_schemes=). Its triggers send =tenant_id= only.

** What leaks today

- A session hears that something changed in another tenant, and when.
- The browser learns the entity name and the time. It learns no key and no value.
- The next read is filtered by the database (row level security by tenant and
  party), so no data leaks. The leak is the fact and the time of a change.
- NATS has one account and mTLS only. Any holder of a service certificate can
  subscribe to any subject. Nothing at the broker separates tenants.

** The options

| Option | What it protects | Cost | Change to generated code |
|--------+------------------+------+--------------------------|
| A. Check in the BFF only | The browser. A bug in the check leaks. | Small | None, but the check needs the tenant, so it needs B or C first |
| B. Tenant and party as headers from the registrar | The browser, with a check in the BFF | Medium | The mapping hands the notification's tenant to the registrar. =publish_entity_event= sets =Ores-Tenant-Id= and =Ores-Party-Id=. The trigger template adds the party where the table has one. The BFF reads headers in =subscribeToEvents= |
| C. Tenant in the subject | The browser and every subscriber. The broker does the filtering, so a BFF routing bug cannot leak another tenant | Medium | =event_subject= takes the tenant: =component.v1.collection_events.tenant.action=. All registrars change. Each subscriber chooses its tenant |
| D. A NATS account or permission per tenant | Every client, even one with a certificate | High | None in the registrar, but the services need cross-account exports and imports, a key per tenant, and a change to provisioning |

*** Party

A party is not a stable property of a session. A person who may see three parties
can switch between them, and an administrator sees all of them. A subject cannot
say "these three parties" without one subscription for each party and entity. That
is why party belongs in a header that the BFF checks against the session's current
party list, and not in the subject. A tenant is fixed for a session, so it can
stand in the subject.

*** The system tenant

Some rows belong to the system tenant and are visible to every tenant, such as
change reasons and classification lists. A change to them is news to everybody. The
BFF therefore listens to its own tenant and to the system tenant, and to no other.

*** Qt

ores.qt listens on the wildcard and does not filter. It must subscribe to its own
tenant and to the system tenant, the same as the BFF, when the subject changes.

** Recommendation

Use C, and add the party as a header (the party half of B).

- The tenant goes in the subject. The broker then delivers only the tenants a
  client asked for, and the BFF can ask for no more than its sessions hold.
- The party goes in a header on the events of party-owned tables. The BFF drops an
  event whose party the session may not see. An event with no party header is
  tenant-wide and passes.
- Defence in depth (D) waits. It protects against a client with a certificate.
  That is a broker hardening task, and it is not needed for the web.
- A alone is rejected. It has nothing to check against.

Cost of C to know before accepting: the subject changes for every entity, and all
three kinds of subscriber change with it (BFF, Qt, any service that listens).
Codegen does the producer side in one template change.

** Tasks that would build it

1. Carry the tenant to the registrar. The mapping gives the notification's tenant
   to the bus handler. =event_subject= takes the tenant. Codegen regenerates all
   registrars.
2. Carry the party. The notify trigger template adds =party_id= where the table
   has the column. The registrar sets =Ores-Party-Id=.
3. Listen by tenant in the BFF. The registry subscribes to
   =component.v1.collection_events.tenant.>= for the session's tenant and for the
   system tenant. It drops an event whose party the session may not see, and it
   checks again when the session switches party.
4. Listen by tenant in ores.qt.
5. Update the specification and the pipeline page. Remove the "known limit".
6. Later, and apart from this story: a NATS permission per client (option D).
