* Goal

Provisioning completes on a busy host. A step reports its progress as a heartbeat and fails only when that heartbeat stalls, never on elapsed time. No request does work that grows with the data. Each step runs in the service that owns its work, so iam carries only identity work and no workflow that is not about identity waits on it.

* Acceptance

- A step that keeps reporting progress is never failed for taking long, and a step whose heartbeat stops fails within a bounded time.
- The shell shows each running step's progress from its heartbeat.
- No request handler does work that grows with the tenant's data inside one request.
- Every provisioning step that is not identity work runs outside ores.iam, and ores.iam links no synthetic, marketdata or reporting core library.
- Acme provisioning completes on a loaded host, and a check books every generated trade type through the database.

* Decisions

- *Iam stays off the critical path of non-identity work, 2026-11-12.* The
  user's rule: a key service like iam must not be anywhere near the critical
  path of a workflow whose activity is not identity. Today every provisioning
  step is dispatched to =iam.v1.ops.provision_tenant_step=, so iam generates
  trades, publishes curves, attaches photos and schedules reports, and links
  the synthetic, marketdata and reporting libraries to do so. Its step
  consumer runs one step at a time, so the parallel steps queue: a run on
  this branch waited two minutes before iam took =set_staff_passwords=.
- *A step fails when it stalls, not when it is slow, 2026-11-12.* The user's
  rule: a fixed timeout makes no sense while a step is working; the step
  reports progress as a heartbeat, and only a stalled heartbeat times out.
  The claim lease a step holder renews is already a heartbeat, but the
  engine also applies a fixed per-step deadline, and a step's own requests
  run under the fixed 30 second request deadline with no progress channel.
- *The evidence, 2026-11-12.* Acme provisioning failed with "NATS request
  timed out" because =set_staff_passwords= reset 74 passwords in one request,
  about 30 seconds of hashing on a busy host. A page of eight unblocked it
  (PR 4648); that is the bounded-request rule applied where it failed, not
  the redesign. The same run showed =compass db recreate -k= refusing while
  the stopped services' connections still drained, for about 15 seconds.
- *Builds on running steps concurrently, 2026-11-12.* The story
  [[id:1D550641-953B-4F17-BFA4-B1DB1EFBE62C][Run a service's steps concurrently]] gave a service a pool of step
  workers. That shortens the queue inside one service; this story removes
  the reason the queue forms in iam.

- *A service serves one family of step commands per stream, 2026-11-13.* The
  first end-to-end run of the per-service dispatch failed because ores.refdata
  declared a second step family on the consumer its data-quality publish steps
  already used: the second family replaced the first one's filter, the countries
  publish was never delivered, and the run that nested it stood still for twenty
  minutes with nothing in the log to say why. A family now names a group of its
  own, and =serve_step_commands= refuses a second family on one stream rather
  than let a service silently stop serving one.
- *A moved step writes as its own service, 2026-11-13.* The calendar step wrote
  =modified_by = "system"=, which the account trigger refuses now that the tenant
  has accounts. A step that acts for the tenant being provisioned writes as the
  service that runs it, which is an account of the system tenant, so the trigger
  accepts it and the row says which service did the work.

* Out of scope

- The account read that the password and picture steps make with
  =limit = 10'000= is the one request left that grows with the tenant's data. It
  is captured as
  [[id:DC63BCCB-0967-4A2D-B978-2033DF7C943D][Provisioning asks for every account in one request, and drops what is past ten thousand]],
  because bounding it means giving the step a page to advance through, which is
  the shape of the step that moves to its owning service.

- Changing what provisioning provisions: the steps, the profile and the data
  stay as they are; only where and how a step runs changes.
