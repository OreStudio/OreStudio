#+title: Tenant ownership and the information flow
#+description: Who owns each row: the installation, the system tenant, or a tenant. The three ownership classes, the mechanism each deserves, and the holes that come from not naming them.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :architecture:tenancy:security:rls:information_flow:
#+created: 2026-10-07
#+updated: 2026-10-26

* Summary

Every row belongs to one of three owners, and the schema names only two. A
*tenant* owns its own business data; *the system tenant* owns the
installation's registers and the shared datasets every tenant reads; *the
installation* owns the facts about the deployment itself — which services
run, which nodes report, what the bus is doing. Because the third owner has
no name, its rows are stored under the system tenant and read through
policies written for tenant data, so every read of them is a workaround.
This page names the three owners, states the mechanism each deserves, and
lists where the missing third class already bites.

* Detail

** The three owners

| Owner              | What it owns                                          | Examples |
|--------------------+-------------------------------------------------------+----------|
| A tenant           | Its own business data                                  | parties, portfolios, books, trades, workunits, batches, results, a party's quote series, routes and subscriptions, synthetic configuration |
| The system tenant  | The installation's registers, and the datasets every tenant reads | the tenants registry, service accounts, permissions, roles, seed profiles; countries, currencies, coding schemes, asset classes, avatars, calendars |
| The installation   | Facts about the deployment itself, owned by nobody in particular | the services roster and its instances, hosts and wrapper nodes, grid and node samples, telemetry logs, message-bus samples, the database and build row, the expected-services registry, the tick streams and their points |

The third row is the one the schema does not name. Its rows have no tenant
in any meaningful sense: a node sample describes a machine, and the machine
runs whatever work the installation gives it.

A tick point is the same kind of row: an observation of a source, not a
tenant's data. It carries no tenant and no row-level security, only the
marketdata service's roles reach it, and a party reads it through its own
series and entitlement. See [[id:D71F9446-C62E-48A6-A87B-136EF598A59E][A source tick is stored once and entitled when it is read]].

** What the schema does today

Measured across =projects/ores.sql/create= on 2026-10-07, by a throwaway
script that matched every =create policy= statement and classified its body
by the tenant functions it names: a widening compares the session to the
system tenant, a system-owned policy compares =tenant_id= to the system
tenant, an own-tenant policy names the current tenant and no system
function, and a policy that names neither is scoped by party, sandbox or
role. The same run reproduced the guard's count of
=check_rls_system_tenant_widenings.py=:

| Policy shape                                        | Count | Meaning |
|-----------------------------------------------------+-------+---------|
| Own tenant only                                     |   353 | =tenant_id = ores_iam_current_tenant_id_fn()= |
| Party or sandbox scoping                            |   152 | keyed on =party_id= or a sandbox, with no tenant predicate |
| A system-tenant session sees every tenant           |    10 | =current = system=; every one is listed as under audit by the guard |
| System-owned rows readable by every tenant          |    14 | =tenant_id = system=; the shared-reference idiom |
| A role-scoped cross-tenant read                     |     3 | =using (true)= granted to the market-data service role, keyed on neither tenant nor party |
| *Total*                                             |   532 | every policy in =projects/ores.sql/create= |

The count does not include the policy on accounts that limits a read to the
parties the caller's account works in. *That policy is a special case and
belongs to accounts alone.* It is hand-written, it names no system tenant
function, and it only narrows a read the own-tenant policy already bounds. See
[[id:0FF2B4E7-473D-A754-A893-C4C70E636C76][Multi-Tenancy Architecture]], "The
accounts exception".

The guarded shape is the third. =check_rls_system_tenant_widenings.py=
fails any new one, and its message is the rule: *"A service that needs a
tenant's data acts inside that tenant, with a token for it (Token Exchange),
instead."*

** The four mechanisms, and what each is for

| Mechanism | What it does | Correct use |
|-----------+--------------+-------------|
| Own-tenant policy | Scopes every read to the session's tenant. | A tenant's data. |
| System-owned policy (=tenant_id = system=) | Every session sees the system's rows. | Shared reference data. |
| =:tenant_read_scope: shared= | The read states no tenant predicate; the policy decides. | A read whose rows the policy already admits. |
| =tenant_context::with_tenant=, or internal impersonation | Acts inside a tenant. | Reaching a tenant's data from outside it. |

[[id:0EAAEDFD-7954-4649-A463-9EE377B023F7][Data read across tenants]] states the
building blocks of the second and third rows together, and the checklist for a new
shared table.

The last row has two forms because it serves two layers: =with_tenant= sets
=app.current_tenant_id= for SQL, and
[[id:AAC60D20-8696-4455-9292-E8FAE1389CBC][Internal Actor Impersonation]]
mints a JWT so a service can drive another tenant's NATS handlers. Both are
the [[id:96460BBE-D7BF-4538-B620-AC5FD9F0C9FE][Token Exchange]] pattern.

** The hole

Installation-scoped data is stored in tenant-scoped tables. Every read of it
must therefore answer "which tenant?", and there is no right answer — only
four workarounds: write it under the system tenant and hope the policy
admits it; widen the policy, which the guard exists to stop; state no
predicate and let a policy written for tenant data decide; or loop over
tenants and impersonate each.

** Where it bites today

1. *The grid summary counts the wrong thing.*
   =ores_compute_grid_stats_fn= takes a tenant, and the poller that calls it
   holds the system tenant, so the stored summary is that one tenant's
   slice. Measured on the running fleet: the stored sample reported
   =total_workunits = 0= while the installation held one workunit and two
   batches under another tenant.
2. *The node and grid samples carry a constant, not attribution.* Every
   sample is written under the system tenant, because the launcher bakes the
   system tenant into every wrapper's =--tenant-id=. The =tenant_id= on
   those rows says nothing about the node or the work; it holds the same
   value in every row.
3. *The roster works by accident.* The services roster reads every expected
   instance and every instance's last report. It works only because every
   process that reports is a service account, and service accounts live in
   the system tenant — so no policy had to be widened. The same read over a
   tenant-owned subject would not work.
4. *The compute service cannot enumerate tenants.* The tenants registry is
   owned by the system tenant, so a system-tenant session may read it — but
   the only C++ that reads that table is =ores.iam.core=, and
   =ores.compute.service= has no dependency on it. The one cross-tenant read
   the design blesses is unreachable from a component that needs it.

** The design

Name the third owner, and give it the mechanism it has earned.

- *Class I — the installation.* Rows that describe the deployment. Services
  write them, and they carry the system tenant as a *constant* rather than
  as an owner. Their policy is the shared-reference shape —
  =tenant_id = current or tenant_id = system= — which the guard does not
  flag, because it admits no tenant's data to anyone: the rows it exposes
  are the installation's own. The *permission* decides who may call the
  read, exactly as =compute::grid_samples:read= does today.
- *Class R — shared reference.* Already right: system-owned rows every
  tenant reads, with writes tenant-scoped.
- *Class T — a tenant.* Already right: an own-tenant policy, with
  cross-tenant access only by acting inside the tenant.
- *A cross-tenant aggregate is Class T, not Class I.* A count of every
  tenant's workunits is not a fact about the installation; it is a fact
  about several tenants at once, and no single tenant owns it. The
  sanctioned way to compute one is to act inside each tenant. If the
  Operations area needs installation-wide work totals, the aggregate needs
  an owner allowed to enter tenants — the mechanism
  [[id:6C930659-6548-4371-B9EB-1AACE597D0E9][Read each tenant's cache
  partition inside its tenant]] is building, and the decision belongs with
  [[id:577FE7CA-F7BC-44AE-834C-AD25DCC0E2F5][Audit the policies that let
  the system tenant read every tenant]].

  The grid's work totals are the first such aggregate. The poller enters each
  tenant, reads that tenant's counters there, and writes the sum to
  =ores_compute_work_totals_tbl=, whose policy admits a session of the system
  tenant and no other. The sum is stored in a table of its own and not in the
  fleet row, because every session reads the fleet row. A function that runs
  with its owner's rights to count across tenants is not the way: it lets any
  database role read any tenant's counts.

** What follows for the grid

The fleet is Class I: hosts, wrappers, node samples and the services roster
describe the installation, and every session the permission admits should
read them whole. The work counts are Class T: workunits, batches and results
belong to the tenant that owns them.

The installation-wide work totals are now written by the poller into a table that only the system tenant reads (entry E8 of the
[[id:07177FE0-F0B9-44E8-B67C-CEAA6DCC696A][RLS and permission exceptions register]]).

So the two questions the screen raises — "is the grid healthy?" and "what is
my tenant running?" — are the same problem seen from two sides, and both
want the same answer: read the fleet as the installation, and enter a tenant
for its work. That makes the Operations grid a read-only screen over the
fleet, and moves *installation-wide work totals* to the audit, where the
cross-tenant mechanism is being decided.

The usage tab applies this. The compute service counts one tenant and groups it by party. The BFF enters each tenant in turn and reads that count there, so the operator's breakdown by tenant needs no policy that reaches across tenants. The page [[id:0EAAEDFD-7954-4649-A463-9EE377B023F7][Data read across tenants]] states the rule and what an exception needs.
