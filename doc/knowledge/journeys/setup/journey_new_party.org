This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A tenant administrator adds a party and links the accounts that work in it.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I want a legal entity my team can work as, with the accounts that work in it."
The tenant administrator finds the legal entity among those the deployment
holds, or adds it by name when it has no LEI, names the party, and chooses the
accounts that
work in it. The journey is done when the party is /active/, its essential data
is published, and its accounts can sign in to it.

* Actor and trigger

The tenant administrator: an account of type =user= that holds the
tenant-administrator role, signed in, and acting inside one tenant. The trigger
is *New party* on the Parties page. It also starts from *Add another party* at
the end of this journey. The manual path, "It has no LEI: add it by name", is
the same journey with the search skipped. The tenant administrator never
chooses the tenant: the session already names it.

This is one of the three journeys that carry server-side state. Provisioning
runs on the server and can fail half way. The state lives in =ores.workflow=,
so the person can leave the page and come back, and the shell can drive the
same steps. Every other group in this corpus describes a journey that keeps no
such state. The order is fixed: this journey needs a running installation, a
signed-in tenant administrator and a tenant that is already /active/.

* Steps

1. *Find the legal entity.* The person searches the legal entities the
   deployment holds, by name or by LEI. Each result shows the legal name, the
   country, the LEI and how many parties the hierarchy would create, so a
   subsidiary is recognisable as one. The person picks one, or chooses *It has
   no LEI: add it by name* and types the name instead. Nothing is created yet.
2. *Describe the party.* The person sets a short name, taken from the legal
   name, and ticks the *accounts that work in it*: this is the
   [[id:8AEB87D6-0CE2-1434-E71B-60BEAE20ADF0][account-party association]].
   Accounts that work in the party can sign in to it and act for it, and can
   switch between parties later. The tenant administrator's own account is
   ticked by default. There is no data-size question: counterparties are
   tenant-wide, and the party's bundles come from the tenant's seed profile.
3. *Review.* The person reads one summary: legal entity, LEI, short name, the
   accounts, and the party data. The screen states that nothing is created
   until the person confirms.
4. *Provisioning.* The person confirms, and the system starts the
   [[id:8AEB87D6-0CE2-1434-E71B-60BEAE20ADF0][provision]] workflow for the
   party. The party stage is one request and its run is one step kind, so the
   rail lists the profile's =provision_party= step, which publishes the party's
   bundles, activates the party and marks its onboarding complete, and the run
   joins the caller to the party. Each line reads pending, running, done or
   failed. *This is the step that carries the state.* The person can leave the
   page and come back. A failed step stops the instance, the party stays
   inactive, and a retry resumes from the failed step; the completed steps are
   kept.
5. *Next steps.* The party is /active/. The page offers three exits: switch to
   the new party and work in it now, set up its books, or add another party.
   The person can also just go back; the Parties page lists the new party as
   active. Where the deployment accepts self-registration, the page may also
   nominate this party as the one a new account joins; the tenant's first
   sign-in nominates it, and this is where a second party takes it over.

The rail is flat: a person sees five steps. Steps that change server state are
final, so there is no Back past them.

The party journey was the first built on the shared journey page, and it proved
the step library: the tenant journey's steps and the party journey's steps come
from the same place. It also settled two things. Counterparties are tenant-wide,
so the Qt wizard's dataset-size question is gone. And the accounts that work in
the party are chosen here, in the describe step, not in a later wizard.

The two =iam.v1.account_parties= subjects are declared in
[[id:D96E6889-A53B-41AF-A7F5-A3497A188BA0][ores.iam.account_party]], and
=iam.v1.account_parties.put_many= is what writes the accounts that work in a
party; the run the provision request starts joins the caller on its own.
=iam.v1.accounts.switch-party= is declared in
[[id:082C5D76-93C2-41E6-8193-9598B79DD07A][ores.iam.account_messages]], and
=iam.v1.accounts.list= is generated from
[[id:81D4D50F-A8D4-47F8-9500-89F6AC460469][ores.iam.account]]. The
=refdata.v1.parties= and =refdata.v1.books= subjects are generated from their
entity models. All of them exist in the generated web protocol.
=dq.v1.lei-entities.search= matches a name or an LEI over the legal entities the
deployment holds and states, per match, how many parties the hierarchy would
create; the BFF adds a route to it and the wire client parses the reply.
=workflow.v1.instances.steps= and =workflow.v1.instances.retry= are not in the
generated web protocol: the generator emits no =workflow= package for the
browser, so the BFF adds a route for each and the wire client parses the reply.

The party stage is one request, =iam.v1.parties.provision=. It takes a party,
by its identifier or its exact full name, the starting point whose party stage
runs, and the legal entity the party was built from; it resolves the party in
the caller's own tenant, and answers at once with the id of a run that publishes
the party's bundles, records that entity as the party's LEI, activates the
party, marks its onboarding complete and joins the caller to it. The starting
point states which bundles a party is published from, as the arguments of its
=provision_party= step, and a profile that orders no party step is refused. A
reference that matches more than one party is refused as well, because a run
acts on one party.

An empty starting point means the deployment's own party stage, which is the
first profile that orders one. A tenant administrator cannot name a profile: the
profiles are the system tenant's rows and a tenant reads only its own, so the
service that holds both is the one that chooses. The shell's =provision party=
therefore names none by default and the browser names none at all.

The LEI travels with the run rather than being written by it. A party identifier
is stamped with the party its writing session acts in, and the person who adds a
party works in another one, so the run is the only client that can record it: it
writes it with a client scoped to the party it is provisioning.

Every subject in the table exists on the wire, and the shell's =provision party=
sends =iam.v1.parties.provision= and follows the same progress read. The browser
reaches the search and the request that starts a party's run through the BFF, and
=POST /api/provision-party= is that request: it adds the party row, places it
under the tenant's own party, and starts the run.

* What is missing

1. *The chosen accounts are not written.* The describe step would pick the
   accounts that work in the party, and the screen does not offer the picker
   yet, because nothing writes them: the run joins the caller alone. Writing the
   other chosen associations is =iam.v1.account_parties.put_many=, and the
   request that provisions a party takes no account list, so no step of the
   party's own run writes them. A picker whose choices went nowhere would be
   worse than no picker.
2. *No scripted end-to-end proof.* No scripted run proves this browser's path
   the way =projects/ores.web/scripts/verify-login.ts= proves the sign-in, so
   the proof of the journey stays manual.
3. *No completion signal the browser can read.* The engine publishes
   =ores.workflow.workflow_instance_changed= on every state change, but the
   payload is an =entity_change_event= that carries =entity_ids=, and the schema
   the browser decodes reads =alpha2_codes= with every field defaulted, so the
   browser receives an empty identifier list. The reads
   =workflow.v1.instances.steps= and =workflow.v1.instances.retry= exist and are
   what a page follows a run with. That mismatch is owned by
   [[id:767A3138-2D4C-4330-8281-DBCFEACF244D][its capture]].
4. *A party hangs under the tenant's own party, and the person does not choose
   where.* A party hangs under another and exactly one of a tenant's parties
   sits at the top, so the row whose parent is unset is what a new party is
   placed under; a tenant that has none gets the party it has just added as its
   root. Choosing the parent, or moving a party afterwards, is the
   [[id:36FC0D92-F532-4B6C-99FE-B669DB87C0FE][Draw the reporting line]] journey
   and not this one.

The rest of the journey is in place, and the list below is the record of what
each part turned out to be.

1. *The legal-entity search reads the deployment's own data.*
   =dq.v1.lei-entities.search= matches a name or an LEI over the legal entities
   the deployment holds, and states, per match, how many parties the hierarchy
   would create. The deployment's records are the ones a tenant is built from;
   no live proxy to the public register is needed. The browser reaches the
   search through a BFF route, and the party named by hand is the same journey
   with the search skipped.
2. *No journey record, and none is added.* A journey is a definition, not a
   server-side object. Its progress state is a workflow instance in
   =ores.workflow=, with its entities, services and subjects, and the run is
   followed by the id the request answers with. Only the server-side workflow
   resumes, so the human steps around it do not survive a reload. The retired
   =iam.v1.journeys.get-active= candidate is dropped.
3. *The provision-party request is one stage.* =iam.v1.parties.provision= takes
   a party, by its identifier or its exact full name, a starting point and the
   entity the party was built from, resolves the party in the caller's tenant,
   and returns a workflow instance id at once. The run publishes the bundles the
   starting point orders, records the entity as the party's LEI, activates the
   party, marks its onboarding complete and joins the administrator who asked to
   it, so the request carries the whole active, onboard and publish stage. The
   LEI is written last, because an identifier's insert bumps the version of the
   party it belongs to and a party saved before it would then be refused as one
   that moved on.
4. *The party's bundles are the starting point's.* The =party_essentials= bundle
   is one of the bundles a =provision_party= step may order, as the step's own
   arguments, and every starting point that orders the kind orders it. A party's
   data is therefore the profile's, and the step the tenant run carries is the
   one the party stage runs. There is no separate publish request for a party on
   its own.
5. *A retry resumes a stopped run.* =workflow.v1.instances.retry= resumes a run
   that stopped on its failed step: it re-dispatches that step under its own
   identity, clears the run's error, and leaves every completed step and its
   result alone. The engine's =recover_in_progress()= stays for runs that were
   in progress when a service restarted.
