* Goal

The surface publish step spends its time waiting rather than computing, so a
fresh tenant's surfaces cost twenty minutes of a run. The step does its own
work in the process that holds the data, fits a party's surfaces several at a
time, and reads and writes in batches, so its time goes to the fits.

* Acceptance

- The step calibrates and publishes in the process that holds the data, and
  asks no service, itself included, for work it can do where it stands.
- A party's surfaces are fitted several at a time, so the party costs the
  slowest lane and not the sum of its surfaces.
- Nothing the step needs is read once per point or once per surface when one
  read would answer for the party: the lineage a grid already has, the quotes
  it reads and the recipes it fits are read in batches.
- The step's duration is measured on the same host, tenant and data before and
  after, and it publishes the same surfaces it did before.

* Plan

The measurements say where the time goes. In the run of =9666da04= the step
took 20 min 27 s: 140 calibrations at a median of 7.51 s and a p90 of 16.41 s,
against 120 publishes at a median of 0.04 s. A surface that ran in a quiet
minute completed in about 100 ms. So the step was not computing: it was
queueing, four requests per surface behind the tick ingest, the due-curve sweep
and the report export, in a service that ran them all on one lane.

The work is therefore in this order, each step measured on its own:

1. The step does the work itself. The calibration and the publish are called
   where the step stands, in the tenant's context narrowed to the party, as the
   calendar step calls refdata's service. The four requests per surface go to
   none.
2. A party's surfaces run in lanes, four at a time, because no surface reads
   another. Each lane reports the progress the engine reads as the heartbeat.
3. The reads and writes are batched. The largest single offender is the
   calibration's write, which reads the lineage of every grid point before it
   writes it: 65 reads per surface, about nine thousand in the run. One read
   answers for the points a grid is about. The quote read follows, five series
   read one at a time where one statement answers for the surface, and the
   recipe, read four times per surface where once per party answers.
4. The approval raise stays per surface here. It becomes one request for a set
   of surfaces in [[id:C756C87B-2284-4BCB-AAEC-F2EA4F2F6D6F][Approve a set of
   surfaces in one request]], which is a decision about what a decider answers
   for, and the seam is left for it: the party's surfaces are published by one
   call that today raises one request each.

* Notes

- The first two changes are structural and countable: the step no longer asks
  its own service for anything, and the read that dominated the write is one
  statement instead of sixty-five. The third is a compile-time property of the
  preparation loop, which walked every pillar and every source for every
  expiry.
- What is not proved is the wall clock. Two of the three runs after the change
  failed the smoke's own market-data criterion, and their surfaces step did a
  fraction of the work: the derived source quoted 2,978 of the 3,888 ids
  demanded, so three of the four parties held no blueprints when the step ran.
  The host also carried a load average of 45 to 56 throughout, from other
  worktrees building, which is most of what a wall-clock number would measure.
  The task waits for a run on a host that is not doing five things at once
  before it claims a time.
- The lanes exposed a real constraint the old requests hid: a lane holds a unit
  of work, and the service's pool has a fixed number of connections. One run
  reached =No connection came free within the acquire timeout= for a surface,
  which failed the step. A store that is momentarily full is not a surface that
  cannot be published, so a retryable store error is now offered the surface
  twice more before it is reported.
- The old path retried a transient failure because the NATS client did; calling
  the service in process took that layer away, and this is where it had to be
  put back.
- A party's snapshot holds every series the party sees, not only the ones a
  surface quotes, and only a surface point carries an expiry and a strike. The
  read therefore asks a datum whether it holds the field before it asks for its
  value: asking a TWD overnight swap for an expiry is the error the first run
  of this shape reported, =IR_SWAP does not hold expiry=.
- The plan a curve or a surface step writes stays a request, and that is a
  decision and not an omission: the configs, their pillars and the blueprints
  are refdata's tables, and the marketdata service's role holds the read
  privilege on them and no more. Writing them where the step stands would be
  refused by the store, and granting the write would move a refdata table's
  owner to a second service. The reads around them are the step's own, so the
  split follows the tables rather than the transport.
- One instant per party is a behaviour change worth naming: the party's surfaces
  are one reading of the market, so they are calibrated at the instant the
  snapshot was taken rather than at the instant each surface came round.
