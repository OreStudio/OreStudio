* Goal

State, in one place, how many units of work the marketdata service may hold at once: its step workers, the lanes each step runs, and the connections the pool gives them. Today the three are unconnected numbers, and the pool is the smallest of them.

* Acceptance

- The number of units of work the service may hold at once is stated once and
  is the number the pool serves: step workers, lanes per step and pool size
  agree, and a reader of any one of them can find the other two.
- A pool smaller than the lanes is either raised or served by a lane count that
  fits it, and the choice is written down with the reason.
- A step that meets a full pool still finishes: the retry is the fallback and
  not the plan.
- No step fails a party because a lane waited for a connection.

* Plan

The three numbers today, read from the tree:

| What | Value | Where |
|------+-------+-------|
| Step workers on the marketdata service | 2 | =provisioning_step_registrar.cpp=, =provisioning_step_workers= |
| Lanes per step | 4 | =run_party_surfaces=, =run_party_forwards=, =run_party_curves= |
| Connections in the pool | 2 | =database_configuration.cpp=, the =db-pool-size= default, with no =ORES_MARKETDATA_SERVICE_DB_POOL_SIZE= in =.env= |

So two steps and 4 lanes each can ask for eight statements at once while the
tick ingest and the due-curve sweep ask for their own. The retry added with the
lanes (=fit_and_publish_surface_with_retry= and its two siblings) offers a unit
twice more when the store says the pool is full, which is why the step
finishes; it is not a sizing.

The work is to state the budget, then make the three agree: either the pool
grows to the lanes the service runs, or the lanes are derived from the pool and
the workers rather than written as a constant. The measurement that decides it
is the number of connections the service actually holds at the peak of a run,
and the acquisition failures it records, not a wall clock.

** Why this now waits on the pooler

A pooled connection *is* a server connection today, so the same number answers
two questions at once: how much concurrency the service has, and how much of the
server's =max_connections= it takes. Three numbers cannot be reconciled while
they are one, which is why the pool defaults to two and the lanes ask for four:
the pool was sized to the server, not to the work.

Behind a transaction pooler the two questions separate. The service's
=db-pool-size= becomes how many statements it may have in flight, which the
lanes and workers do argue for; pgbouncer's =default_pool_size= and
=max_db_connections= become the server's budget, stated once per environment
instead of summed across every service in it. Sizing the pool before the pooler
lands would fix the client number to a server constraint that is about to move.

Measured on 2026-11-20 for the record: one PostgreSQL server carries six
development databases and =max_connections = 1500=; an idle environment held 38
connections across its fleet, all idle, at the default two a service
(=pg_stat_activity= grouped by =datname=).
