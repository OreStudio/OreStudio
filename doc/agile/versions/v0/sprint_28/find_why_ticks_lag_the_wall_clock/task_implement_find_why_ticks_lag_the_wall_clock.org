* Acceptance

- The place where ticks queue is found, with a measurement.
- The cause is stated, and it explains why the lag drains at 1.16 times real time.
- The fix is stated, and a follow-up story or task exists for it.
- A tick that is late shows how old it is, or the reason it does not is recorded.
- The ingest keeps up with a sustained load of thousands of ticks, shown by a measurement.
- The lag reproduces locally with a script that anyone can run.

* Plan

The design is in =doc/knowledge/architecture/tick_ingest_pipeline.org=. Three reviews
of it are recorded under Notes. The work follows its delivery steps, and each step
ends with a measurement.

0. Prove the write ceiling. A prototype in scratch tables of this environment's
   database, with no product code. It times batched inserts for four table shapes and
   four batch sizes, and it times =COPY=. Its result goes into the note.
1. Remove the per-row costs: the notification trigger, the series read for each tick,
   the commit for each row, and the wait for acknowledgements on producers and on the
   republish. Remove the two tick streams for perishable subjects.
2. Lanes, the ring, the age gate, the overload states and the recent window. Split
   the live subject into tokens, and move Hedgr to it.
3. The policy table, the durable tier, taps, a rollout by source, and scale out.

Each step is its own task and its own pull request. This task holds the investigation
and the plan.

* Notes

** The lag reproduces in brave_hopper

The environment showed no lag at 19:52 (newest observation 0.4 s old). It showed
a lag of 160 to 200 s from 19:56, after =pg_test_fsync= loaded the disk at 19:53. That
is a correlation, not yet a proof. The ingest stayed behind for the next ten
minutes, because it writes only 10 to 25 percent faster than ticks arrive. The ratio
matches the 1.16 of the report.

What the evidence shows:
- One thread does the work. Exactly one marketdata backend is active, and it waits
  on the commit sync (=WalSync=, =WALWrite=).
- Each target-tick makes a series =SELECT=, two =INSERT= statements, each its own
  commit, and a =js_publish= that waits for an acknowledgement.
- A commit costs about 4.3 ms on this disk, and five environments share the disk.

What it does not show yet: which of the four steps costs most, and whether
=synchronous_commit= off lifts the ceiling. The next experiment is a capacity test
with a controlled burst, with and without =synchronous_commit= for the marketdata role.

Capacity target from the owner: thousands of ticks, which this loop cannot reach.
GPU offload is not a candidate for this path: the work waits on commits and
acknowledgements and does little arithmetic.

** Result of the capacity experiments

The commit sync is not the main limit. Turning it off lifts the drain rate by 16
percent. The larger cost is the row trigger that calls =pg_notify=. A transaction
that notifies takes a database-wide lock at commit, and that lock is shared by every
database on the server. The ingests of five environments on this server wait for
each other. With the NOTIFY function empty, the rate rises by another 26 percent.
The rate is still about 210 rows each second, so the per-row work also matters: the
series read, the two checks, the trigger that closes the previous point, seven
indexes, and the publish that waits for an acknowledgement.

The experiments changed two things in this environment and restored both: the role
setting =synchronous_commit= and the function =ores_marketdata_market_observations_notify_fn=.
The restored function comes from the repo file, and a query confirmed it.

The owner added two requirements: a stale tick is worse than a lost tick, and the
target is tens of thousands of ticks each second. The proposal that meets them is
[[id:6F3F1A80-5B4C-4F33-ADF0-F42CDE8FF8C2][The tick ingest pipeline]].

** Result of step 0, first pass

The present observation table cannot carry the target. Batching it stops at about 8,300
rows each second for one connection, because the insert trigger and its two lookups of
the tenant table run for every row. Without the trigger it reaches about 33,000, and an
append-only table with one index reaches about 187,000 at 162 bytes for each row, against
1,199 for the present table. The figures are upper bounds, from scratch tables on a shared
server with the rows made inside the database. The note records them under "Step 0: the
measured write ceiling". The result changes step 1: it must move ticks to an append-only
table, and batching alone is not enough. The next pass repeats the test from the
application, with =COPY= through libpq, one tenant in each batch, and a table with history.

** Review of the pipeline design against every asset class

The owner asked for a review of the design as the base for all ticking data. The
review read the note against the way asset classes plug in today
(=adding_a_market_data_asset_class.org=, =feed_factory_and_kind_seam.org=) and against
Hedgr. It found these faults. Each one is fixed in the note.

| The ingest sees a source and a URI, and never the feed kind. | A policy keyed by the feed kind cannot work in the ingest. | Policy rows are keyed by instrument type, with a default row, so a new class needs no ingest change. |

| "Overwrite the oldest slot" was unsafe. | A writer must not touch a slot that the worker reads. | The worker detects that it was lapped by the sequence numbers and skips ahead. |
| Thinning and lane choice used "series" and "datum" loosely. | A curve is one series with many quotes. Thinning by series would drop quotes. | Defined the words. Lanes are keyed by series, and conflation, windows and thinning by datum. |
| A held-back value could wait forever. | The last tick of a burst never reached the consumer. | A trailing publish at the end of each conflation interval. |
| A curve may publish half old and half new. | A consumer would see an inconsistent curve. | A =consistency= field, and an open question on a set marker. |
| The CRM bridge is an =if= in the loop. | Derived data needs a place for every class. | Taps that read the conflated output, and derived ticks that re-enter with producer kind =DERIVED=. |

| The window read had no access check. | Any caller could read any party's recent ticks. | The read checks tenant and party against the bindings. |

each recorded in the note: Hedgr filters the whole party subtree on the client, the live

changed the design. The live subject now splits into tokens, so a consumer selects a

** The report that opened this task

Another environment measured this and has not confirmed the cause.

Measured:
- Tick time against the wall clock, the drain rate and the arrival rate are in the Measurements table.
- The producer, =fx_spot_feed.cpp=, stamps each tick with =system_clock::now()=, and Hedgr decodes =observation_time= unchanged. The stamp is a real UTC time, not a time zone fault.
- The broker is not the place of the backlog: no slow consumers and no pending bytes.

Inferred, not confirmed:
- The backlog sits in the ingest loop of the marketdata service of bright_faraday. The loop handles each tick one at a time, with database writes and a JetStream publish that waits for an acknowledgement. If the producers outrun the loop, the messages queue inside that process, where the broker cannot see them.
- The loop fell behind while the machine was loaded.

Unknown:
- The service's own log goes to the terminal that started it, so no one has read it.

What to decide:
- Wait for the backlog to drain, or restart the marketdata service so that ingest starts from now. Nobody has touched bright_faraday's services.
- Hedgr needs no fix. It judges age from the tick's own time and shows delayed data as STALE. STALE does not show how old the data is. The question is whether the chart footer should show it, for example "70 min old".

* Result

The lag reproduces locally, its cause is found, and the pipeline that removes it is designed
and reviewed. The first step of that pipeline is built in its own task.

- *Cause.* The ingest handled each tick on one thread. For each binding it paid a commit, a
  series read, a row trigger that called =NOTIFY=, and a wait for two JetStream
  acknowledgements. The ingest wrote about 143 rows each second against a demand of about
  120, so a short slowdown built a backlog that took hours to drain. The =NOTIFY= commit takes a
  database-wide lock that every database on the server shares, so the ingests of five
  environments on one server slowed each other.
- *Reproduction.* The lag appeared in a second environment after a disk load, and a burst of
  ticks from the injector in =evidence/= reproduces the drain rate on demand.
- *Design.* =tick_ingest_pipeline.org= proposes lanes, a bounded ring, an age gate,
  conflation, a recent window, batched writes, two durability tiers and a contract for every
  asset class. Three reviews are recorded under Notes, and each finding is fixed in the note.
- *Step 0.* The present observation table stops at about 8,300 rows each second for one
  connection because of its insert trigger. An append-only table reaches about 187,000 at 162
  bytes for each row against 1,199.
- *Step 1.* Built and merged in the task "Remove the per-row costs of the tick ingest". A
  burst drains about 16 times faster, and the live stream carries no tick older than 5 seconds.

Open, each its own task:
- The append-only tick table with its reader migration, which lifts the ceiling of the
  present table.
- Step 2: the lanes, the ring, the recent window and the live subject with tokens.
- Step 3: the policy table, the durable tier, the taps, and scale out.
- A decision on how an existing database drops the old notification trigger.
- Hedgr: the live tick carries no coordinate, and its ring drops the newest bucket when full.
