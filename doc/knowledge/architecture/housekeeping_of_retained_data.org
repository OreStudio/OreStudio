#+title: Housekeeping of retained data
#+description: Every retained store is made subject to a retention policy, applied by one executor and recorded as telemetry.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :housekeeping:retention:storage:scheduler:architecture:
#+created: 2026-11-24
#+updated: 2026-11-27

* Summary

A store is housekept when three parts are present: a retention policy declares
what the store keeps and for how long, an executor applies the policy, and a
telemetry record states what the executor did. Policies must have executors, and
executors must have telemetry. The platform exhibits both absences. The more
common is the missing executor: the schema installs a retention policy only where
the storage engine reports the licensed edition, and states the interval without
installing anything where it does not.

* Detail

** The three parts

| Part | Question it answers | What its absence produces |
|------+---------------------+---------------------------|
| Retention policy | what is kept, and for how long | a constant in code, invisible to the operator and changed only by a rebuild |
| Executor | who applies the policy | rows, objects and directories that accumulate while the policy reads as satisfied |
| Telemetry | whether it ran, and what it freed | no way to tell a working policy from an inert one |

** The policy is a record

A retention policy names its target, the action to take on it, the retention
period or threshold past which the action applies, the cadence at which it is
applied, whether it is enabled, and the fallback to take where the engine will
not act. The policy is stored as data: a period changes without a schema rebuild,
and one screen shows every policy beside the size of what it governs.

** What the platform states and what the installation decides

The configuration arrives as two datasets published into the system tenant, beside
the platform's other data: one stages the jobs the scheduler fires, the other the
retention policies. A publish reconciles what the platform states. For a job that
is its command, the payload it carries and what it is for; for a policy, the
target, the action and the reason the store is kept. The rest is the
installation's, and a publish leaves it alone: when a job fires, whether it is
enabled, and a policy's period, cadence and enabled state are written when the
row first arrives and carried from the most recent row the installation held,
even where the publish closed that row first.

The split follows the question that decides every dataset. The platform declares
the job in order to function, so a release that changes what the sweep runs
reaches an installation that is already running. The installation decides how
much of its own data it keeps, so no republish overrules that decision. The
dataset carries the defaults a new installation starts with, which is why the
first publish writes every field and the second writes none.

** The actions

Four actions treat a store by removing what it no longer needs:

- drop the chunks of a time-partitioned table that lie beyond the retention
  period;
- delete the rows of an ordinary table that lie beyond the retention period;
- expire the objects under a key prefix, which is the object store's lifecycle
  rule;
- release a working directory once what it produced is stored elsewhere.

Two further treatments hold a store without removing anything:

- overwrite a counter instead of appending a new version of it;
- recompute derived data instead of keeping a copy per owner.

The action is a property of the class of data, not of the table. Which class
takes which action is the subject of
[[id:21D539CD-AF3B-4479-81D5-F195756C064F][Classification of retained data]].

** The partition column

A chunk drop removes whole chunks, so it bounds a store only by the time the
chunk is cut on. A store chunked on a business date is bounded by that date and
not by its own age. The run output tables carried the date the engine stated,
which for a projected measure lies decades ahead: of ten million cashflow rows,
all but a few hundred were dated after the sweep that dropped them.

The chunk is therefore cut on the instant the rows were written, which is the
one time every row of a run shares and the time past which the run itself is
stale. Three consequences follow. A hypertable checks its partitioning column
for null before any row-level trigger runs, so a bitemporal table partitioned on
its write time needs a default for that column, because the insert trigger is
what otherwise sets it. A unique index on a hypertable must carry the
partitioning column, so a natural key index over such a table is declared with
the column rather than generated from the keys alone. And the column is the
store's, not the writer's: a hypertable routes a row by the partitioning column
before any trigger runs, so a trigger cannot move the row afterwards, and a
write that stated a value the trigger then rewrote would either fail the range
check of the chunk the row landed in or leave it in the wrong chunk. The
generated entity therefore leaves that column out of its insert, the default
stamps it, and the trigger no longer rewrites it.

** One executor

A single scheduled job applies every policy that is due. The job's schedule sets
the tick, and therefore the finest cadence the installation can express; each
policy states the hours between two applications of itself, and the sweep applies
a policy only once that period has passed since the last time it applied it
([[id:DFC57D4F-9AB2-4428-BDEC-BF44D106A75D][Scheduler]]). The period is the
policy's, because the stores differ in how quickly they accumulate what the policy
removes, and one tick serves them all. One executor serves
every class: the classes differ in what they hold and in when their contents stop
being useful, not in how a sweep runs. The established example is
[[id:F06175E8-CA73-4A21-BEC5-898D0A388E17][Approval request expiry]], whose
sweep a service registers at runtime from a system setting.

Each firing is recorded as a scheduler job instance, and each application of a
policy is recorded in the run ledger beside the policy it applied. The housekeeping
jobs appear in the scheduler's screens beside every other job.

** The fallback path

A time-series engine applies a retention policy as a background job, and the job
exists only where the engine's licensed edition includes it. Where that edition is
absent the policy is never created, and the schema states the interval without
applying it. The executor therefore performs the action itself wherever the engine
will not, and records which path ran. One declaration is honoured on either
edition, and the path taken is recorded.

** The telemetry

Each policy reports, per sweep, the size of its target before and after, the
rows, chunks or objects removed, the time taken and the outcome. The measured
size over time yields a growth rate.

The telemetry sits beside the declaration: a policy's row shows what it governs
and what the last sweep freed, so an enabled policy that frees nothing is
visible. The size comes from the database's catalogues for tables and chunks,
from the object store's listing for key prefixes, and from a directory walk for
the caches and working directories a node holds.

** What a person sees

A sweep is a scheduler job, so it appears in the scheduler's own views beside
every other job: its cadence, the instant it next fires, and the outcome of its
last run. A job of the installation belongs to the system tenant, so the views
that report jobs show it to the system tenant through the same tenant-scoped
read a tenant uses for its own work, and to no other tenant.

What a run removed is read as one row per policy, joined to the policy it names.
The join is why the figure sits beside the period rather than inside the ledger:
a period is a property of the policy now, and the ledger records only what the
run was given. A policy turned off keeps its history, and reads as off beside
the runs it made while it was on.

Tuning is a write to a policy, not to the job. A policy is a row of the system
tenant, so an operator holding the write permission changes a period, or turns a
policy off, and the sweep applies the change at its next firing. The two
cadences are stated apart: the policy's is the hours between two applications of
itself, and the job's is how often the sweep looks, which bounds the finest
cadence the installation can express.

** Telling a person without asking

A policy is data, so a store can be kept for ever without anybody editing a
schema, and nothing in a sweep complains about it: the sweep visits policies, and
a store no policy names is a row it never reads. The absence therefore has to be
said rather than discovered, and the installation says it through the inbox
([[id:7FAF3C0D-1131-4C32-9C29-3D1EF6D1005D][Notification requirements]]).

One scheduled pass reads the bounds view and the run ledger and raises a notice
per store that nothing effective bounds. The pass is the definer's, because it
reads the stores of every tenant and writes each one's inbox; the audience is the
permission the catalogue's own write requires, resolved in the tenant that holds
the store, and only a person is told: a service account holds the component's
wildcard and no person signs in as it.

Three kinds, one per case the screen distinguishes. A store nothing bounds reads
as one notice, and a store whose last sweep failed reads as another, because a
failure is an incident a person acts on and a missing policy is a decision nobody
made. A sweep that leaves policies unapplied is one notice for the sweep, not one
per policy: the ledger's =ran_at= is the transaction's instant, so every policy
one sweep applied shares it, and that instant is what the notice groups by.

The pass also clears. A store that comes back under a bound has the notice it
raised cleared for every person it was raised for, so the count of open
housekeeping notices follows the count of unbounded stores rather than the number
of times anybody looked. That is what makes the number worth reading: it is the
work left, and it reaches zero when the installation does.

The notice names the store and links to its page, and carries no size, no rate
and no period. A notice that repeated those numbers would be a second statement
of them, drifting from the page that reads them; the person who has to act needs
the plot and the policy, and both are behind the link.
