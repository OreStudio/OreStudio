#+title: The scheduler loop
#+description: How the scheduler sleeps until the next due instant: the in-memory job set and its re-arm, the due condition, the asynchronous firing, and the limits on what the loop does.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :knowledge:architecture:scheduling:
#+created: 2026-09-28
#+updated: 2026-09-28

The scheduler sleeps until the next due instant and runs the actions of the
jobs it holds. It keeps each job's next occurrence, sleeps until the
earliest of them, and on each wake fires the jobs whose occurrence has
arrived and recomputes theirs. A firing runs asynchronously: the loop does
not wait for it, so a slow action delays neither the next firing nor another
job. The loop re-reads the job set when a change event arrives and re-arms
its timer, and at startup it takes each job's last recorded firing as the
reference point, so a restart does not re-run what already ran. This page
describes the loop and the limits on what it does.

* Detail

** Sleeping until the next due instant

The scheduler holds one timer and no fixed grid. It sleeps until the
earliest next occurrence among the jobs it holds, and at that instant it
fires every job whose occurrence has arrived. Nothing else wakes the loop:
there is no per-job timer, and a wake that finds a job due is the only wake
that has work to do.

The cost of a wake follows the jobs that are due rather than the jobs that
are held: only a due job has a handler selected and an action started. A job
that is not due costs nothing at a wake, because its next occurrence was
computed when it entered the job set or when it last fired.

** The in-memory job set

The loop holds the active jobs and each job's next occurrence. It reads the
jobs across all tenants, because it runs the system's jobs as well as a
tenant's, and it keeps them for the wakes that follow.

The loop does not poll the job table. A change to a job definition is
delivered as an event, and the handler for that event re-reads the job set
and re-arms the timer. The re-arm is part of the contract rather than an
optimisation: a new or edited job may be due sooner than the instant the loop
is sleeping towards, and a loop that re-read the set without re-arming would
serve that job late.

Two properties follow from the re-read. An inactive or deleted job leaves the
set and stops being an occurrence the loop sleeps towards, and a job the loop
already holds keeps its next occurrence, so a change to one job neither
resets nor delays another.

** The due condition

At each wake the loop fires every job whose occurrence has arrived. An
occurrence has arrived when the job's active flag is true and the next
occurrence after its last recorded firing is at or before the current
instant, which is the condition
[[id:EF7AE225-7DBE-4D19-B3DC-F6720A4B52DA][the cron expression]] defines. The
loop holds no other notion of when a job should run, which is why changing an
expression needs no change to the loop.

** The firing does not block the loop

For each due job the loop writes the firing, selects the handler for the job's
action type, starts the action, and recomputes that job's next occurrence. The
loop's part in an action ends at that hand-off.

Three properties follow, and all three are deliberate.

- /Jobs do not delay each other./ The actions of different jobs run
  independently, so a job whose action takes a minute does not hold back the
  jobs fired in the same wake.
- /A job can overlap itself./ A job whose action takes longer than its
  recurrence interval can have more than one firing in flight. Each firing
  has its own record, and the live status read reports the number in flight.
- /The loop is independent of the action's result./ The result is written to
  the firing record when the action returns, and it does not affect the next
  occurrence the loop sleeps towards.

** The sleep and the wall clock

A cron expression names wall-clock instants, and a timer measures elapsed
time. The two part company when the wall clock moves: a step from a time
source or a daylight-saving change moves the instants a job names while a
timer keeps its original duration.

The sleep is therefore bounded and re-derived. The loop never sleeps on the
strength of one reading of the clock for longer than the bound, and on waking
it derives the next occurrence from the current wall clock rather than from
the interval it slept. A clock adjustment can therefore delay a firing by no
more than the bound, and it cannot leave the loop asleep past an instant that
has already arrived.

** Startup and the reference point

At startup the loop reads the active jobs and, for each, the instant of its
most recent recorded firing. That instant is the reference point for the due
condition and the base of the job's first next occurrence, so a job that
already ran is not run again because the process restarted. The reference
comes from the firing record:
[[id:29714C54-9022-494B-9751-CC6FCED10313][the firing]] is what the loop
reads to decide when a job is next due.

** The limits of the loop

The loop holds jobs, not work. It keeps no queue of pending actions and no
backlog of missed firings, so a firing either runs at its occurrence or does
not run at all. Two limits follow.

- /The loop does not retry./ A failed action is recorded as failed, and the
  job's next execution is the next occurrence its expression defines. A retry
  would be a second firing, which the expression did not define.
- /The loop does not track the work an action starts./ An action may start
  work that outlives it, and the loop neither waits for that work nor reads
  its result. That work belongs to the component that owns it, and
  [[id:13105F27-9C05-495E-B9A0-2EFC9B5AC811][Scheduling and workflows]]
  defines the boundary.
