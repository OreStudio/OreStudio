This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: An approver holding the kind's decide permission reads a curve-publish request, weighs the change and the checks, and approves, refuses or holds it with a comment.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I am the second pair of eyes. I want to see what this curve was before and
what it would become, and to say yes, no or not yet, on the record." The
journey is done when the request carries the approver's decision and a comment,
and the request's state shows what that decision did to it.

* Actor and trigger

The actor is the approver: the checker, an account other than the one that
raised the request, holding the permission the request's kind names in
=decide_permission_code=. The trigger is a request that is waiting. The journey
starts at the approval queue and ends when the request is approved, refused or
held, or when the approver leaves it for somebody else to decide. It picks up
the request raised in
[[id:239E4C3C-12E7-4A87-9DE3-16B9F093BC14][Ask for a publish to be approved]];
an approved request is applied in
[[id:53F31919-86FA-4E35-8594-9E72197C4A3B][Publish the approved set]].

How many approvals the request needs is a rule of its kind, not of this screen.
=approval_kind.approvals_required= is the count — zero, one or n — and
=approval_kind.decide_permission_code= says who may decide. Each approval must
come from a different person, so an n-approval request stays open until n
distinct people have approved it. A hold is available only when the kind's
=allows_hold= is true, and a comment on approval is required only when
=comment_on_approve= is true; a refusal always needs one.

A request waits on a person, so the queue is one paged read over the shared
record, filtered to the kinds the approver may decide. Two people may open the
same request; the second decision is refused by its version, and the screen
says who decided.

* Steps

1. *Open the queue.* The approver opens *Approvals* and reads the requests
   waiting in the tenant, oldest first: the kind, the curve, how long it has
   waited, and its state. The read is scoped by the decide permission, so the
   approver sees only the kinds they may decide.
2. *Open one request.* The approver opens a request. The system reads its
   header — who asked, when, and why — and the kind's detail that names what is
   asked: the curve configuration, the as-of instant and the party the request
   authorises, read by the request's id. The kind =marketdata.curve_publish= is
   seeded, so a curve publish appears in the queue of a person holding
   =inbox::approvals:decide_market_risk=, which in Acme is the Market Risk
   function: the head of market risk of each office.
3. *Read the delta against the last published.* The approver reads the
   candidate points beside the curve the system last published, point by point,
   so the decision is about a change and not about a number. The published set
   is known — the snapshot read states its as-of, its publisher and the request
   that authorised it — but no read returns the difference against it; see /What
   is missing/. Until one does, the panel names the published as-of and leaves
   the delta empty.
4. *Read the QA and validation checks.* The approver reads the checks that were
   run over the candidates — all pillars priced, discount factors monotonic, no
   arbitrage — with each result. No operation returns these; see /What is
   missing/. Until then the panel names the checks and marks each result
   unknown.
5. *Read the rule that decides this request.* The system shows how many
   approvals the kind needs, the permission it takes to decide, and when the
   request expires. The approver sees whether they are one of several.
6. *Read the decisions so far.* The system lists every decision already taken
   on the request, with who took it, when, and the comment. An approval by the
   asker is refused, so a request cannot be waved through by the person who
   raised it.
7. *Decide.* The approver chooses *Approve*, *Refuse* or *Hold* and writes a
   comment. A refusal always needs the comment, because the asker reads it. The
   system records the decision against the request and moves the request to the
   state the decisions reach: an approval closes it only once
   =approvals_required= approvals stand, a hold parks it, and a refusal closes
   it.
8. *Leave.* The approver returns to the queue. The request is gone from the
   waiting list if it is final, and still there, held, if it is not. The system
   tells the asker what was decided; nothing does that today.

Step 7 is the step whose effect outlives the screen: the decision is written
down, and the component that owns the kind applies it later. Approving this
request does not itself publish a curve.

* Screens and wireframes

One screen. The queue sits at the top, the selected request and the change it
proposes sit in the middle, and the checks, the decisions so far and the
decision panel sit at the bottom. The approver reads what is asked and what
would change before the answer controls, so the act of deciding comes last.

The queue, the read and the decision are generic: they are defined in
[[id:353B9AFD-C3B5-46BF-8D1B-E0362AE3CD23][ores.inbox.approval_operations]]. A
kind is what the queue and the decision are filtered by, and the subject a
decider reads is the kind's own read, in the component that owns the kind.

* What is missing

1. *The queue row shows no curve.* The kind is seeded with
   =inbox::approvals:decide_market_risk= as its decide permission, one approval,
   a hold allowed and a two-week window, so a Market Risk decider finds the
   request. The queue is one generic read over the shared header, and a header
   carries no subject, so the row shows the kind, the reason and the state. A
   decider who opens the request reads its subject on the kind's own read; the
   queue does not join to it. The candidate is a queue read that carries each
   kind's subject columns, which the model states as the kind's detail table.
2. *There is no delta against the last published.* A publish record marks the
   set in use and names the as-of it was built at, but no read returns the
   difference between it and the candidates. The candidate is a read such as
   =marketdata.v1.curve_candidates.diff=, taking the published as-of from the
   record. Until then the delta panel is shown empty, with the reason stated,
   and the approver reads the published as-of beside the candidates rather than
   a change.
3. *There is no QA or validation check operation.* No subject runs the checks
   over a candidate set and returns the results; the fit checks the desk
   speaks of are not an operation. The candidate is
   =marketdata.v1.curve_bootstrap.validate=, returning one row per check. Until
   then the panel names the checks and marks each result unknown, so the
   approver reads it as "not checked" and not as "passed".
4. *The queue does not read any kind's detail.* An approval request is a
   header; the kind keeps its detail in a table of its own, keyed by the
   request's id, in the component that owns the kind. A curve publish's detail
   exists — =curve_publish=, keyed by =request_id= — and the decider reads it by
   request when they open one. What no read does is join a page of the queue to
   each kind's detail, so the queue row has no curve in it. The candidate is a
   queue read that takes its subject columns from the kind's own detail table.
5. *The deciding group is not a field.* =approval_kind= names a permission code,
   not a group; which people may decide follows from who holds the code. The
   screen cannot say "Market data approvers" from the model. The candidate is a
   group named by the kind, or a permission bound to a group. Until then the
   screen shows the permission code and does not claim a group name.
6. *A hold names a reason from a lookup.* A curve publish allows a hold,
   because the approver's third answer is "not yet", so the hold takes a reason
   code from the hold reason lookup and never free text alone. The lookup is one
   list of the reasons the operational authorisation queue names, and a code it
   does not hold is refused with the codes it does.

* The decision

** The maker and checker pair on the kind

*** The rule

The kind names two permissions, and each one governs one half of the act:

| Field                   | What it permits                                    | Seed in Acme                                              |
|-------------------------+----------------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------|
| =raise_permission_code= | Raise a request of this kind: the maker's right    | =marketdata::curves:publish=, held by the market data maker roles |
| =decide_permission_code= | Decide a request of this kind: the checker's right | =inbox::approvals:decide_market_risk=, held by the heads of market risk |

=decide_permission_code= exists today. =raise_permission_code= does not, and the
decision is that the kind gains it.

The four-eyes rule is a rule of the record, not of the pair. The decision insert
trigger refuses a decision whose decider is the request's raiser, and the service
answers =four_eyes= with the words "You asked for this request, so someone else
decides it." The pair makes the rule reachable in the ordinary case: the maker
holds the raise and not the decide, and the checker holds the decide. A tenant
that grants one account both permissions has a weaker control, and the record
still refuses the self-decision.

The queue is filtered by =decide_permission_code=, and the raise is refused to an
account without =raise_permission_code=. Neither permission appears on a screen:
a screen draws the action or leaves it out, and the access mark says Editable or
Read only.

*** Per kind, and not per request

The pair belongs to the kind. A request cannot name a permission of its own, and
must not: a maker who could name a weaker checker would choose their own control.
A kind may name parts, and each part names a permission and an answer order; the
parts are per request, because a request names which functions answer it. The
pair is not a part, and one request never widens it.

The permissions are held by an account in the tenant, never by a party. A curve
publish for one party is therefore raised by a maker who holds the raise and
decided by the tenant's Market Risk, and the subject names the party so the
checker knows whose curve it is.

*** Why this answer

The record already enforces the separation that matters: the decider is not the
asker. What the pair adds is the ordinary case, where the desk cannot answer its
own request and the approver cannot raise one in the desk's name. One column on
the kind and one check on the raise give that, and no screen changes.

*** The alternative

A request names the permission its decider must hold, and the kind names only a
default. A maker then chooses the checker from a list the kind allows. This is
the answer if a tenant needs a request to route to the functions of the party
whose curve it is. It is rejected for now, because the queue would become a join
over the requests and their named permissions, and the four-eyes rule would be
the only fixed part of the control.

*** Open questions

1. What is the raise permission called? The recommendation is
   =marketdata::curves:publish=, in the market data component's namespace,
   because the act is the component's; the decide stays in the inbox's namespace
   because the inbox owns the queue. The alternative is
   =inbox::approvals:raise_marketdata=, which groups both halves under the queue.
2. Is the raise permission per curve family or per curve kind? The recommendation
   is per curve kind first: one code for an IR curve and one for an FX curve,
   granted to the maker roles that own each. The alternative is one code for
   every curve, which a desk that owns one family does not need.
3. Is the pair declared on the kind or in a policy? The recommendation is on the
   kind, beside =approvals_required=, because the kind is already the one place
   the count, the hold and the expiry are read. The alternative is the policy
   model the book changes use, and it would split one control over two
   mechanisms.
4. What happens when an account holds both? The recommendation is that the seed
   never grants both to one role, and that the request page says plainly that
   the person may decide, so a tenant that grants both can see what it has done.
   The alternative is to refuse the grant, which the IAM model has no rule for.
