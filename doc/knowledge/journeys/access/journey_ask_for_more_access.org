This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A member asks for a role beyond the one registration gave them, and a tenant administrator answers.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I cannot do my job with the access I have." The member asks for a role they do
not hold. The journey is done when the request has an answer the member can
read: the role is now theirs, or the request was refused and the refusal says
why.

* Actor and trigger

Two people act on one request, at different times. The requester is a member: an
account of type =user=, signed in inside one tenant, reading their own record.
The trigger is a refusal — a screen answers "permission denied" — or a task the
member cannot finish. It starts at *My access*, which already lists the roles
they hold, and it ends when they read the answer.

The answerer is a tenant administrator: an account inside the same tenant that
holds =iam::roles:assign=. The trigger is a request that is waiting. It starts
at the tenant's request queue and it ends when the role is granted, or when the
request is refused.

This is one journey with two actors, because it is one request with one
lifecycle and each actor owns one end of it. The screens stay apart: the member
never sees the queue, and the administrator never sees the ask. The
administrator's unprompted version of the same write is
[[id:3C753578-BC0E-433C-92B5-38031F050ADC][Change someone's access]]; here the
write answers a request instead of starting one.

A request waits on somebody, so this is the case the
[[id:CA94C762-B0C8-40E7-85CE-D4C6355023CC][user_journey contract]] calls
server-side state: the request is a
[[id:FDF3C079-4E18-4DB7-911F-7691AD1F5665][workflow instance]] in
=ores.workflow=, and both screens read that one instance. The member's rail is
not resumable across a reload — only the request is.

* Steps

** The member asks

1. *Open my access.* The member opens *My access*. The system reads the roles
   they hold, and beside them the roles the tenant offers that they do not hold.
   The first list is =iam.v1.ops.get_account_roles= and the second is
   =iam.v1.roles.list=.
2. *Read what a role grants.* The member opens a role and reads the permissions
   it bundles, so the ask is an informed one. The read is
   =iam.v1.ops.get_role_permissions=.
3. *Ask for the role.* The member picks a role and writes why they need it. The
   system opens a request that names the requester, the role and the reason, and
   puts it in front of the tenant's administrators. This is the step that leaves
   somebody waiting, so the request is written down rather than kept in the
   browser.
4. *Wait.* The member leaves. *My access* shows the request as waiting, with what
   was asked for and when. The roles they hold do not change: a request is not a
   grant. The member may withdraw the request here, and a withdrawal closes it.
5. *Read the answer.* The member returns to *My access*, or follows a notice, and
   reads the answer. An approval shows the role among the roles they hold. A
   refusal shows the reason the administrator wrote.
6. *Leave.* The request is closed, and it stays in the member's history.

** The administrator answers

7. *Read the queue.* The administrator opens *Requests* and reads what the
   tenant's members have asked for: who, which role, why, and how long it has
   waited. The system lists the requests waiting in the tenant, oldest first.
8. *Weigh the request.* The administrator opens one request and reads the member,
   the role, the reason, and the permissions the role bundles. The last of these
   is what turns a role name into a decision.
9. *Decide.* The administrator approves or refuses. An approval grants the role
   to the member through the ordinary assignment path, so the =account_role= row
   carries the same audit tail every grant carries, and the request closes as
   approved. A refusal writes the reason the member will read, and the request
   closes as refused.
10. *Leave.* The queue no longer lists the request. The grant takes effect at the
    member's next sign-in, as every assignment does.

The ask and the decision are final steps: there is no Back past a request that
has been made, or past a decision that has been given. A member who needs the
same role again asks again, and the second request is a new one.

* Screens and wireframes

Two screens, one per actor. The member's screen is *My access*, extended by two
panels: the ask, which pairs a role picker with the reason, and the requests,
which lists what the member has asked for and how each request ended. The four
panels answer one question — what may I do, and what have I asked for — so they
belong on one screen rather than on a screen of their own.

The administrator's screen is the queue. It shows the requests waiting in the
tenant, the selected request in full, and the decision. The decision is one
panel because the administrator reads the reason and answers at once; a request
that needs research is left in the queue rather than half-answered.

The queue is the Access group's screen, beside the roster rather than inside it.

* Entities composed

#+ATTR_HTML: :class hug-leading
| Entity | What it contributes | Model |
|--------+---------------------+-------|
| =account= | The member's identity in the header, and the requester on each queue row: username and full name | [[id:81D4D50F-A8D4-47F8-9500-89F6AC460469][ores.iam.account]] |
| =role= | The role asked for, the roles on offer, and the roles the member holds | [[id:6C0F5B1A-9A3E-4D2C-8B7E-2F4A1C6D9E30][ores.iam.role]] |
| =permission= | What the role grants, so both actors decide from the codes rather than from the name | [[id:76B113F4-410A-4DE5-9757-A4E263B7B8E7][ores.iam.permission]] |
| =account_role= | The assignment an approval writes, with its audit tail | no entity model — junction table |
| =workflow_instance= | The request itself: who asked, for which role, why, and where it waits | [[id:FDF3C079-4E18-4DB7-911F-7691AD1F5665][ores.workflow.workflow_instance]] |

The request is the one member of this table that is not an IAM record, and that
is deliberate: a request waits on a person, and a journey that waits on a person
keeps its state as a workflow instance. The instance's =type= names the request,
=request_json= holds the role and the reason, =created_by= is the requester, and
=state_id= is where the request has got to.

No credential field appears on either screen. =password_hash=,
=password_salt=, =service_password_hash=, =totp_secret= and cleartext passwords
stay on the server.

* Operations and messages

#+ATTR_HTML: :class hug-leading
| Step | Operation | Subject | Status |
|------+-----------+---------+--------|
| Open my access | Read the roles on my account | =iam.v1.ops.get_account_roles= | exists |
| Open my access | List the roles the tenant offers | =iam.v1.roles.list= | exists |
| Read what a role grants | Read the permissions one role bundles | =iam.v1.ops.get_role_permissions= | exists |
| Ask for the role | Submit a request for a role | =iam.v1.roles.request= | missing |
| Wait | Read my own requests and their answers | =iam.v1.roles.requests-by-me= | missing |
| Wait | Withdraw my request | =iam.v1.roles.withdraw-request= | missing |
| Read the queue | List the requests waiting in the tenant | =iam.v1.roles.requests= | missing |
| Decide | Refuse a request, with a reason | =iam.v1.roles.refuse-request= | missing |
| Decide | Grant the role the request asks for | =iam.v1.ops.assign_role= | exists |
| Either end | Follow the request's state | =workflow.v1.ops.get_workflow_steps= | exists |

The existing subjects are defined in
[[id:C47A4410-8624-431C-98FE-D05304CEC6D8][ores.iam.authorization_messages]].
=iam.v1.ops.assign_role= checks =iam::roles:assign= before it writes, so the
administrator's decision carries the check the member's ask must not. The three
reads prove that the caller has a session and check no further permission —
which is what the handlers in =ores.iam.core/messaging/role_handler.hpp= and
=authorization_handler.hpp= do — so a member can list the roles on offer without
holding an administrator's read.

* What is missing

1. *No request subject exists.* =iam.v1.roles.*= carries =assign=, =revoke=,
   =mine=, =by-account=, =permissions=, =suggest-commands= and
   the registrar's CRUD; nothing submits, lists, withdraws or refuses a request.
   The candidates are the five request subjects in the table above. The approval
   itself needs no new write: =iam.v1.ops.assign_role= already grants the role and
   already checks =iam::roles:assign=.
2. *The workflow engine cannot wait on a person.* The seeded =workflow_instance=
   states are =pending=, =in_progress=, =completed=, =failed=, =compensating=
   and =compensated=, and the transitions are start, complete, fail, compensate
   and retry. Nothing suspends a run, and nothing resumes it when somebody
   answers. Nor is there anywhere for the run to stop once it is answered: an
   approved, refused, withdrawn or expired request has no state that means it.
   The candidates are a waiting state beside =in_progress=, a terminal state per
   answer, and a step kind that means "a person decides", or a decision verb that
   resumes a suspended instance and closes it. Until they exist, a request cannot
   be held the way this journey says it is held, and the ask has nowhere to wait.
3. *The requester may answer their own request.* Four-eyes means the answer comes
   from an account other than the requester's. An administrator who holds
   =iam::roles:assign= can ask for a role and then approve it, which is the
   escalation this journey exists to prevent. The candidate is a guard on the
   decision: the approver must not be the requester.
4. *Nothing says which roles may be asked for.* Every role in the tenant is on
   offer, the administrative ones included. A tenant may want a role that nobody
   may ask for, and today there is no way to say so. The candidate is a flag on
   the role row, =is_requestable=, in the shape of =is_registration_default=: a
   flag on the row it points at, because row-level security decides which actor
   may set it.
5. *Nothing tells either person that something happened.* No subject tells the
   administrator that a request is waiting, and none tells the member the answer.
   The changed-event pipeline carries entity changes, not a person's notice. The
   candidate is a notification of its own, which is larger than this journey and
   outlives it.
6. *No query returns a request as a row.* The request is an instance whose
   payload is =request_json=, and =workflow.v1.instances.list= returns instances
   rather than a joined request. The queue needs the requester, the role and the
   reason together. The candidate is a projection beside the read, or the
   IAM-side read of step 7.
7. *A request that nobody answers waits forever.* There is no deadline and no
   reminder. The candidate is a review window, as a setting, after which the
   request closes as expired, so that a queue cannot rot.
