This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A tenant administrator brings a new trading counterparty on board: its legal identity, its identifiers, its contacts, and the netting agreements and collateral terms the tenant trades under.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I want to bring a new counterparty on board once — the legal entity, the names
we recognise it by, the people we contact, and the agreements and collateral
terms we trade under — so that the desk can trade with it and every screen
resolves it to the same entity." The journey is done when the counterparty reads
in the list, resolves by an authoritative identifier, and its agreements, netting
sets and collateral terms read back.

* Actor and trigger

The actor is the tenant administrator: an account with the =TenantAdmin= role,
acting inside their own tenant. The trigger is a new trading relationship, a
legal name or group change, or an ORE document that names a counterparty the
tenant does not hold. The journey starts at *Counterparties*, or at the
*Onboard a counterparty* button there, and ends at *Outcome*, at *History*, or
at *Refused*.

A counterparty is a tenant-wide record. The party-counterparty junction decides
which of the tenant's parties can see it, and a party sees only the
counterparties its junction rows name.

* Steps

1. *See the counterparties.* The person opens *Counterparties*. The system lists
   the tenant's counterparties: short code, full name, party type, status,
   business centre, identifiers, contact count, version, and last modifier. The
   person searches by code, name or identifier value, and filters by *Active*,
   *Closed* or *All*. The person chooses *Onboard a counterparty*.
2. *Identity.* The person enters the short code, the full name, the
   transliterated name when the registered name is not Latin, the party type,
   the status, the business centre, and the parent counterparty. The system
   offers the party types, the party statuses, the business centres, and the
   counterparties already stored. The short code is the natural key and is
   unique in the tenant. The full name need not be unique: branches share a name
   under distinct short codes. The parent is optional.
3. *Identifiers.* The person adds each identifier the tenant recognises this
   counterparty by: a scheme and a value. The system shows the list, marks the
   identifier the tenant resolves the counterparty by, and refuses a duplicate.
   The system takes the LEI first, then the BIC, as the authoritative
   identifier. An ORE alias is unique across the tenant. The system also shows
   which parties may trade with the counterparty.
4. *Contacts.* The person adds a contact for each purpose the tenant uses:
   Legal, Operations, Settlement and Billing. A contact carries the street
   address, the city, the state, the country, the postal code, the phone, the
   email and the web page. The natural key is the counterparty and the contact
   type, so the system keeps one row per type.
5. *Legal agreements.* The person records each netting agreement with the
   counterparty: the agreement number, the type, the governing law and a
   description. Under an agreement the person records its netting sets, and
   under a set its identifiers, its collateral terms (the CSA), and the
   currencies the CSA accepts as collateral. The two parties of an agreement are
   fixed and never change across its versions. A set with no agreement holds
   trades that do not net.
6. *Review.* The system shows one summary of everything the person entered and
   the operations the confirm will send. Nothing is written yet.
7. *Confirm.* The person chooses *Confirm and write*. The system writes the
   counterparty, its identifiers, its contacts, its agreements, its netting
   sets, its netting set identifiers, its collateral terms, its eligible
   currencies, and the visibility rows. It then shows the outcome: the short
   code, the identifiers, the contacts, the agreements, and the version
   written, with links to trade with the counterparty, to see who can see it,
   and to onboard another.
8. *Check the history.* The person opens the history. The system lists every
   version with who changed it, when, and why, and shows the field-level
   difference from the version before. The person can read the counterparty,
   its identifiers and its contacts as they stood in a version, and can revert
   to an older version, which the system writes as a new version.
9. *Handle a refusal.* When the server refuses a write, the system keeps what
   the person typed and states what was refused, from which step, and that the
   record did not change. The person corrects the value, or keeps the value
   already stored, and continues.

No step waits on the server after its reply, and the journey keeps no state.

* Screens and wireframes

One list screen and one journey screen. *Counterparties* is the list, with its
search and its tabs. *Onboard a counterparty* is one screen: a flat step rail on
the left, and the current step on the right. The steps are Identity,
Identifiers, Contacts, Legal agreements, Review, Outcome and History. Nothing is
written until the person confirms on Review, so Review is the last step that
reads and Outcome is the first that reports a write.

* Prototype

The prototype is the design record for the screens above.

- Directory :: =doc/prototypes/parties/onboard-a-counterparty/=
- States :: =landing=, =counterparty=, =identifiers=, =contacts=, =agreements=,
  =review=, =done=, =refused=, =history=

  =?fail=duplicate_set_id=, with =?fail=1= for the first kind

The landing screen carries the list and its search. The journey screen carries
the rail and one step at a time. The state is chosen from the query string and
is reachable from the foot bar.

screen; see /What is missing/.

* What is missing

1. *No authoritative-identifier flag exists.* The model stores no flag that
   names the identifier the tenant resolves a counterparty by, so the screen
   infers one: the LEI first, then the BIC. If the tenant must state which
   identifier is authoritative, the column does not exist. The candidate is a
   flag on =counterparty_identifier=, with the precedence as its default. Until
   then the screen shows the inferred one and says it is inferred.
2. *A counterparty carries one business centre.* The journey must show every
   centre the counterparty deals through, and there is no counterparty-to-centre
   row. Either the single column is the answer, or a junction like
   =party_country_junction= is needed. A second centre is drawn as a gap, not as
   a field. Until a junction exists the screen offers the one column and says
   so.
3. *No counterparty-side call reads the party-counterparty junction.* The
   junction calls =refdata.v1.party_counterparties.put= and
   =refdata.v1.party_counterparties.list_by_party_id= exist, but the subject
   reads by party, and the junction's right column carries no read of its own.
   The counterparty protocol has no call that reads the rows from the
   counterparty side, so the screen cannot list the parties that may trade with
   this counterparty. The candidate is
   =refdata.v1.party_counterparties.list_by_counterparty_id= and an index on the
   junction's counterparty column to serve it. Until then the screen shows the
   parties it already knows.
4. *The confirm is one act over eight subjects, with no composite write and no
   rollback.* The review draws the write as one act over the counterparty and
   its seven owned subjects. The server offers each write alone, so a failure
   half way leaves a counterparty with some of its rows. The prototype draws the
   outcome as one act; the server cannot yet promise it. The candidate is a
   composite write that stages the eight, or a rollback that closes the rows
   already written. The visibility row is a ninth write, and it is not part of
   the eight.
5. *No field-level diff in any reply.* Version history is read with
   =refdata.v1.counterparties_versions.list= and
   =refdata.v1.counterparties_versions.get=, and the screen computes the
   difference from the version before. No reply carries it. The child rows have
   their own history subjects, so a full difference of identifiers and contacts
   needs a read each. The candidate is a reply that carries the field-level
   difference. Until then the screen computes the difference and the child reads
   are one call each.
6. *A refusal names neither the record, the version, nor the field.* Each
   refusal arrives as a plain error on one subject. No reply names the record,
   the version it left behind, or the field that collided, so the refused screen
   composes those three from the step that failed. The candidate is a typed
   refusal that carries them.
7. *The landing list carries the counterparty alone.* The identifier and
   contact columns read from the owning rows, one call per row. The candidate is
   a list reply that carries the counterparty with its identifiers and contacts,
   or a filtered child read per page. Until then the screen pages through the
   child rows once.
8. *A counterparty's agreements and sets are not read in one reply.* The screen
   reads the agreements, then one call per agreement for its sets, then one call
   per set for its identifiers, its CSA and the CSA's eligible currencies. The
   candidate is a composite read of the counterparty's agreements, sets and
   collateral terms.
9. *Only a tenant administrator can run this journey.* No role below
   =TenantAdmin= holds the counterparty permissions; =Operations= holds the
   currency permissions and no counterparty permission.
