* Goal

The three Profile journeys run in =ores.web= against the real server. A member sets their own photo, name, job title and contact details, and a tenant administrator corrects a colleague's. The screen is one screen with panels, and each panel says who may write it. A reporting-line change is a proposal that a hierarchical superior or an administrator approves, not a plain edit.

* Acceptance

- The three Profile journeys run in =ores.web= against the real server: a
  member sets their own photo, name, job title and contact details, and a
  tenant administrator corrects a colleague's.
- The self-service subjects exist, and each is restricted to the caller's own
  account and to the fields a member owns. Every other field is refused with a
  code the screen branches on.
- The photo can be replaced: an upload path into =ores.assets= returns an image
  id, and the accepted formats and the maximum size are stated once and
  enforced by the picker and by the server.
- One Save writes both records and names the record that failed. Nothing on the
  screen claims the pair is atomic.
- A reporting-line change is a proposal. The screen states who must approve it,
  the value does not change while the proposal waits, and no approval is
  claimed that nothing performs.
- The screen says which panel the caller may write, and every panel the caller
  may not write says why.
- The two email fields are labelled so that neither reads as the other.
- A signed-in person reaches the journeys from the shell's navigation, and the
  trigger that opens it shows the person's own name and photo once the account
  has been read.
- Every gap the three journey documents record is closed or stated on the
  screen, and the statement is recorded.

* Decisions

- *The shell is a menu, the cards, and the avatar.* The header carries a menu of
  areas, an area opens its journeys as cards, and the person's own journeys are
  under their name and photo. Nothing else: there is no sidebar and no second
  list of what the cards already say. (Prototype verdict, 2026-10-01.)
- *The mode is read from the sign-in, never chosen.* A super administrator works
  in the system tenant's context, a tenant administrator in a tenant's, and a
  party user in a party's — the contexts the server already scopes everything
  by, so the mode cannot disagree with the data and there is no switch to get
  wrong.
- *People is the privileged party user's area.* Managing the people of a party
  is a party job done by somebody with authority in it, not a tenant
  administrator's job, and a regular party user is not shown the door to it at
  all.
- *The bootstrap journey is a door, not an area.* First run belongs to a plain
  installation, where there is no account to sign in with and therefore no shell
  to draw a menu in. It is the way out of the state the shell cannot be entered
  from, so no menu offers it. The system administration menu is the *Tenants*
  area, which holds the tenant management journeys: creating a tenant, and
  retiring or resetting one. *Tune the tenant* is the tenant administrator's and
  stays in their mode. (Product owner, 2026-10-01.)
- *The mode is stated by the server and read by the browser.* The session view
  and the login answer carry it, and the shell draws its menu from the value it
  is given. The BFF states it today, from the tenant the login answered with,
  because that is the fact the session already holds and the rule stays out of
  the browser; when =ores.iam= states it on the login answer the rule goes and
  nothing on the client moves. (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *A mode with no implemented journeys shows the session, not an empty menu.*
  The application mode declares no areas because a tenant-scoped session and the
  permission codes it needs do not exist yet. It shows the tenant and the party
  the person works in, which are facts, rather than drawing areas it cannot
  fill. (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *The roster of tenants is its own journey.* The list existed only as step 1 of
  *Retire or reset a tenant*, so finding out what tenants a deployment holds
  meant opening a journey that destroys one. Reading is its own goal, and the
  catalogue already has the shape: *See who has access* is the same read-only
  roster for the people of a party. *See the tenants* joins the Tenancy group,
  and *Retire or reset a tenant* picks its tenant from it. (Product owner,
  2026-10-01.)
- *A read the browser may not make is refused at the route, not only hidden.*
  The mode says which context a session acts in, so a route can refuse a session
  that is acting inside a tenant even while the subject behind it checks no
  permission. A menu that omits an area is structure; a route that refuses a
  caller is a boundary, and only the second one is authority. (Implementation,
  2026-10-01.)
- *The signed-in shell bounds its screen at the public shell's width.* A journey
  that stands something up draws the same banner before and after a sign-in, and
  a banner fills the column it is given. One shell capped its column and the
  other did not, so the same screen was a comfortable width at the door and a
  wall once somebody was through it. One number, in both shells.
  (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *An offer to inherit a password stands only while there is one to inherit.* A
  starting point may propose that the tenant's administrator takes the creating
  administrator's password, and a journey with no creating administrator has
  nothing to hand over. The proposal is dropped where the details are built
  rather than drawn and then refused, because a choice that cannot be kept
  leaves a step nobody can leave. (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *Two bounds, named for the shape they suit.* A rail, a form or a dialog takes
  the column; a list or a grid takes the workspace. One bound for every screen
  was wrong in both directions and produced both defects: a banner unbounded on
  a wide display, and a table held to the width a form wants with a dead margin
  on either side. The shell owns the bound so a journey cannot forget it, and
  the default is the column, because a form across a wide display is the harder
  defect to read. (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *A table is the page, not a panel on it.* A card that already carries the
  title and the count, drawn around a table, makes the table narrower than the
  screen and reads as a box in the middle of nothing. The title, the count and
  the primary action belong in a page header, and the table belongs under it.
  (Review, 2026-10-01.)
- *A control that opens nothing is not drawn.* The roster has no search, no
  filters and no row actions, because the list subject has no filter and the
  journey a row action would open is not built. They wait for the things behind
  them rather than appearing as buttons that do nothing. (Implementation,
  2026-10-01.)
- *A status is painted by the badge catalogue, not by the screen.* =ores.dq=
  holds the badge catalogue, so a screen holds no colours of its own and two
  screens showing the same status agree with each other. (Product owner,
  2026-10-01.)
- *The badge belongs to the value, not to a mapping.* A status row names the
  badge that paints it; nothing maps a value to a badge, because a value that
  names its own presentation cannot drift from it. (Product owner, 2026-10-01.)
- *The badge supplies the colours; the value supplies the words.* A pill's label
  is the value row's own name, never the badge's. A shared badge vocabulary is
  what lets one visual language cover every state in the platform, and it stops
  being that the moment it renames somebody else's state: a suspended tenant
  reads *Suspended* in the frozen colour, not *Frozen*. (Product owner,
  2026-10-01.)
- *A junction that can be replaced by a reference is not kept.* =badge_mapping=
  still serves the domains that have not moved and is read by nothing on the
  path that has; it goes when the last domain carries its own reference, not
  before, because deleting it early takes those domains' colours with it.
  (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *A run belongs to the tenant that asked for it, and acts on the one it names.*
  The two roles are different and the engine already keeps the first: a
  provisioning run's rows are scoped to the super administrator's own tenant, so
  the person who asked for the run can read it, while the tenant the run builds
  is the one every step acts on. A run owned by the tenant it creates could not
  be followed by the session that started it, because that session is in another
  tenant. (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)
- *The run names what it acts on, as a field and not only as payload.* A
  workflow instance states a target: a caller-supplied kind and id, opaque to
  the engine, naming the entity the run acts on. It does not stay in
  =request_json= alone, where nothing can index it, filter by it or report it,
  and where only the run's own handler can find it. The engine gains one generic
  field rather than =ores.iam= gaining a table that would leave the run itself
  unable to say what it is for, and leave the next component to build the same
  table again. (Product owner, 2026-10-01.)
- *A journey abandoned before it provisions is restarted, not resumed.* Nothing
  is written before the run starts, so there is no draft tenant and no draft
  status to invent: a person who leaves the form before submitting starts again,
  and what a screen has to offer back is the work that has a run behind it.
  (Product owner, 2026-10-01.)
- *A tenant can have any number of runs in flight.* No index limits runs per
  target, because a second attempt does not have to wait for the first one to
  stop. The roster shows the run that changed last. (Product owner, 2026-10-01.)
- *The target is a kind and an id, not an oresmd URI.* oresmd is the URI grammar
  for market data. It is not a general way to name entities, so the workflow
  target does not use it. (Product owner, 2026-10-01.)
- *A run is resumed from its own page, and the form is not restored.* The roster
  links an unfinished run to =/tenants/runs/:id=. That page shows the
  provisioning step alone: the same rail and the same retry. The hand-off step
  is not offered there, because the browser never stored the administrator
  password it needs. A tenant detail screen is a later piece of work.
  (Implementation, 2026-10-01.)

- *The screen is one page, and each panel saves itself.* Variant *B* of the
  prototype is accepted: the profile and the contact record carry their own
  versions and their own change reasons, so each panel writes its own record
  with its own reason and a half-finished edit never rides along with the one
  that is ready. The rail variant is rejected because this screen is a page a
  person returns to, not a run with a beginning and an end, which is what makes
  a rail right for the registration door. (Prototype verdict, 2026-09-30.)
- *The member writes their own record.* =iam.v1.accounts.update-self= and
  =iam.v1.account_contact_informations.update-self= are added, restricted to
  the caller's own account and to the fields a member owns: =full_name=,
  =job_title=, =image_id= and the contact record. Without them two of the three
  journeys are read-only, which is the largest gap the group document records.
  (Product owner, 2026-09-30.)
- *The photo is replaced, not only chosen.* An upload path into =ores.assets=
  returns an image id, and the accepted formats and the maximum size are
  stated. Step 2 of the flagship journey cannot happen at all otherwise.
  (Product owner, 2026-09-30.)
- *A reporting-line change is a proposal.* The person whose line it is proposes
  the change; their hierarchical superior or a tenant administrator approves it,
  and the line changes only then. An administrator acting on a colleague's
  record is the approver, so their own change takes effect. (Product owner,
  2026-09-30.) It is not a plain edit on the profile screen, and it is not a
  plain edit on Membership's tree either: both documents change to record the
  proposal.
- *One Save, two writes, and the failure is named.* The profile and the contact
  record are separate records and no composite operation is added now, so the
  screen sends both and names the record that failed. (Product owner,
  2026-09-30.)
- *Two emails stay two, and both are labelled.* =account.email= is the address a
  person signs in with; the contact record's =email= is the address colleagues
  use. The screen shows both, labelled apart, and neither reads as the other.
- *The sign-in and access panel is read-only here.* It belongs to
  [[id:5FB73D18-4734-485B-B414-9EBD1B76A239][Protect my account]] and
  [[id:3CA48F40-B601-497B-BA78-9822DBEBA8E5][Know what I may do]], so the
  profile screen carries it as a summary with the reason stated on it.
- *The two platform defects the end-to-end run found are captured, not fixed
  here.* The member's own saves are refused by the generated insert trigger's
  ordering, and an upload over the message fabric's payload cap cannot reach
  the validator; neither is in this branch's diff, so both go to the product
  backlog for triage: [[id:8F7AA9CF-C92C-484F-8B2E-3F14EBEC4168][A self write
  is refused on a tenant with accounts]] and
  [[id:820AC660-C3FC-4A45-998D-A65818FA4BB8][A profile image over the payload
  cap cannot reach the validator]]. (End-to-end run, 2026-10-05.)
- *An upload larger than 1 MB travels over HTTP storage, not the message
  fabric.* The 1 MB payload cap silently narrows the 2 MB the picker and the
  validator state, so the bytes of a large upload leave the request and reply
  fabric and travel over the storage surface instead. (Product owner,
  2026-10-05.)

* Result

Closed 2026-10-05. The screen, its routes and its wire contract are built and
reviewed, and the end-to-end run over the real stack proves everything the
branch owns: the administrator's write to a colleague's record passes — the
picker's read, the account read, the profile write with its unset fields
echoed — and so do the picture byte round trip, the member's reads, the read
gap's refusal, the grants read, the upload policy, and the refusal of the
administrator's route. 67 checks ran; 29 failed, every one of them traced to
the two platform defects the Decisions record: the member's own saves are
refused, so the member's picture and contact record never land, and the
administrator's checks that read them cannot pass. The one direct
administrator failure is the same self-write defect: the administrator writing
their own contact record.

The two defects are not fixed here because neither is in this branch's diff.
The member's own saves — =PUT /api/me/profile= and =PUT
/api/me/contact-information= — are refused with "The write failed." on any
tenant that holds accounts: the generated insert trigger validates
=modified_by= after the version replace has retired the actor's own row. An
image over roughly three quarters of a megabyte dies in the transport (http
503) before the validator can judge it. Both are captured in the inbox for
triage, and acceptance bullet 1 is met for the administrator and blocked for
the member until they land. The raw output is
=evidence/profile_journeys_run.txt= beside the task.

* Out of scope

- The approval itself. Nothing in the platform holds one today: the workflow
  engine can hold the instance, but the queue, the notification and the
  decision belong to the [[id:36FC0D92-F532-4B6C-99FE-B669DB87C0FE][Draw the reporting line]] journey and to the Membership
  group. The profile screen draws the proposal and states who decides; it does
  not build the decision.
- The roles a person holds, which is the Access group's work, and the password,
  the sign-in state and the sign-in audit, which are the Credentials group's.
- Which of the two email fields other screens treat as authoritative. The
  screen labels them apart; the modelling decision is its own work.
