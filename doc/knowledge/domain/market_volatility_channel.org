#+title: The market's volatility channel
#+description: What the market-wide factor should do to a price law: business time against sampling, a per-feed volatility loading with its normalisation, the tail channel through the mixture, and the bivariate Hawkes that would make activity and volatility excite each other.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :marketdata:synthetic:volatility:design:factor:
#+created: 2026-10-26
#+updated: 2026-10-26

* Summary

The market-wide factor moves the tick clock today and leaves the price law alone:
a panic arrives as more observations of the same random walk. A market that is
busy is also *more volatile*, and the same shared factor can carry that, because
the arrival work already publishes it and every price process already holds the
knobs a loading would scale. What is missing is three small seams — a setter on
the price process, a loading on the link and the config row, and one line in each
feed — plus the calibration that keeps a run's sigma what its configuration
states. This note states those seams, the tail channel the mixture adds, and the
bivariate Hawkes that would make activity and volatility excite each other; it
also settles whether a rate change moves time at all.

* Detail

** What the factor is today

One state machine and one shared heat, read once per tick:

  A(t) = S(t) + C(t) / R(t)

where =S= is the calm/active multiplier (calm derived so its long-run mean is
one), =C= is the heat any event adds to and decay fades, and =R= is the summed
mean rate of the series enrolled with the heat, so a series takes the share of
=C= its own rate is of the total. A feed reads it through
=market_link::rate_multiplier()=, which returns =sensitivity * A(t)=, and its
arrival process is re-rated by that number. The rates move; the prices do not.

The heat's long-run mean is not zero — it settles at the event rate times
jump/decay — so with the heat on, the average rate is above the configured one.
That is a known property of the rate channel, and it is the first thing the
volatility channel has to decide about rather than inherit.

** Sampling against time

A diffusion's variance follows *elapsed time*, not the number of observations.
Double the tick rate and a correctly written price law gives twice as many
samples of the same path per second, not twice the variance.

The processes in =ores.analytics.quant= are of two kinds and they behave
differently under a rate change:

- A process with a time-scaled law takes =next(elapsed_ticks)=, and the feed
  passes =elapsed_seconds * ticks_per_second=. A higher rate shortens each gap,
  so each step covers less time, and the variance per wall-clock second is
  unchanged. This is the correct behaviour, and it is what the arrival
  time-rescaling test asserts.
- A process that answers =supports_variable_step() == false= takes one nominal
  step per tick. When each step's variance is the same, the same nominal step now
  fits into a shorter gap, so **variance per wall-clock second rises in proportion
  to the rate**, and sigma with its square root.

** Three quantities, and the choice between them

Every feed carries three numbers, tied together:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| =r= | ticks per wall second: how often the feed *publishes* |
| =Delta= | simulated time per tick: how far the *law* steps |
| =v= | simulated seconds per wall second: how fast the *simulated world* runs |

They satisfy =v = r * Delta=, so two of them are the design and the third follows.
Which two are chosen is the whole question.

Measured before any code was written, the platform chooses them inconsistently:

- A *spot* feed takes the gap, so =Delta= is the wall gap and =v = 1=: the
  simulated world runs at real speed.
- A *curve* feed is built with =Delta= = one calendar day (the constant
  =1 / 365= year, =ir_curve_feed_dt=), so at one tick a minute =v ≈ 1440=: the
  simulated world runs fourteen hundred times faster than the spot feeds beside
  it.

So the market lives in two times at once, and nobody chose either. A rate change
on a curve moves time; on a spot feed it does not. That is not a defect in either
path but a missing decision.

*The decision:* =v= belongs to the **market factor**, one number for the run,
beside the state and the heat, and a feed's rate is pure sampling. A feed derives
=Delta = v / r= and hands it to its process.

- A higher rate therefore gives a *smaller* step, which is the safe direction for
  every numerical scheme.
- The default =v= is chosen so a curve at one tick a minute keeps the numbers it
  produces today (=v = 1440=). Spot feeds deliberately speed up to the run's =v=,
  because that is the inconsistency being removed.
- =v= is visible where a person watches the market, on the dashboard and the
  Market regime tab, because "how fast is this world running" is a question a
  demonstration asks and no screen answered.

** A step is a discretisation, not the model

Nothing here changes a model's specification. =dr = kappa(theta - r) dt +
sigma dW= says nothing about how often it is stepped. The processes that
implement the *exact transition* of their law — the affine family, Vasicek,
Hull–White, CIR, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, and the Gaussian mixtures as exact normal
increments — are the model for any =Delta=. The Euler and tree schemes
(Black–Karasinski, Black–Derman–Toy, HJM, LMM) approximate it, and their error
grows with the step.

Each such process therefore declares the largest step its scheme can take, and a
feed refuses a rate so low that the step would exceed it, rather than silently
producing a biased path. A step is also *calendar* time: real curves step on
business days and skip weekends and holidays, which this design does not do. That
is a limitation to state, not to build now.

The clean formulation is business time. Let

  Theta(t) = integral of A(s) ds

be the integrated activity, and let the price process run on business time with a
volatility per unit of it. Then "more activity" and "more variance per second"
are two dials instead of one, and a series that is merely observed more often is
visibly unchanged.

** The volatility loading

Three seams, each mirroring something the arrival work already has:

1. =i_stochastic_process= gains =set_volatility_multiplier(double)=, the sibling
   of =i_arrival_process::set_baseline_multiplier=. The default refuses; the
   processes that can honour it scale their own sigma — a Gaussian mixture scales
   its stdevs, a short-rate model its sigma — and keep their means.
2. =market_link= gains =volatility_sensitivity= beside =sensitivity=. A feed that
   follows the market in rate and not in volatility sets it to zero; the default
   follows the rate loading, because a market that only ticks faster is the
   artefact this note exists to remove. Both generation-config kinds gain the
   column, which is a schema change with a migration and a codegen run — the one
   part of the loading that is not a line of code, and the reason the task under
   it is sized as it is.
3. Each feed applies it on its tick, beside the arrival multiplier it already
   reads.

The law, with the loading written as a volatility multiplier:

  M_vol(t) = (sensitivity_vol * A(t))^gamma
  sigma_effective = sigma * M_vol(t)

and the exponent is what decides which quantity follows activity:

- *gamma = 1/2* gives variance proportional to activity, which is the
  mixture-of-distributions reading and the default: the same diffusion with more
  time in it, so Clark's mixture holds and a busy minute is a wider minute.
- *gamma = 1* gives sigma proportional to activity, and variance proportional to
  its square. That is the right choice for a series whose step count rather than
  its clock drives the variance — which is what a fixed-step process does today,
  and therefore the behaviour the first task removes.

The exponent is a knob because the evidence does not settle it; the default is the
one the literature supports.

*Calibration is the work, not the plumbing.* A run's sigma must stay the sigma its
configuration states unless a person pins a state. The state's long-run mean is
one by construction, so it needs nothing. The heat's is not: it settles at a rate,
the event rate times jump/decay, which is the derivation in
[[id:AC70CC11-7819-419A-A317-E543DE837160][Carry a market-wide arrival regime]].
So the volatility margin centres it, with a floor:

  A_centred(t) = max(S(t) + (C(t)/R(t) - E[C/R]), epsilon)
  M_vol(t) = (sensitivity_vol * A_centred(t))^gamma

Subtracting the heat's mean keeps the long-run mean of =A_centred= at one, so the
run's average multiplier is the loading and the sigma stays calibrated. The floor
is not cosmetic: without it a calm market with a negative margin can drive the
multiplier to zero or below, which makes a price freeze. With the floor the
long-run mean is one up to the mass of the draws that hit it, and the note's test
must accept that tolerance rather than an exact one.

*Two meanings of volatility* must be kept apart in every test:

- the sigma of the process, which is what the loading moves;
- the sigma a client computes from the ticks published, which also moves with
  sampling frequency and microstructure noise, and which rises even when the
  process is unchanged.

** The tails

A diffusion whose sigma doubles gives wider returns but the same shape. A panic
does not look like that: prices gap, and the kurtosis rises faster than the
variance. An FX feed's law is already a Gaussian mixture, so the tail channel is
a reweighting rather than a new model: as =A= rises, weight moves from the narrow
components to the wide ones, renormalised so the mixture stays a distribution and
the mean is unchanged.

That is the cheapest way to make a pinned panic produce the gaps a person expects,
and it composes with the loading only if it is *variance-preserving*: after the
weights move, the stdevs are rescaled so the mixture's variance is what it was,
and the loading remains the only channel to sigma. Without that rescaling the
reweighting raises the variance too, and the calibration test in the table below
would fail for a second reason that has nothing to do with the loading. The
reweighting sets the shape; the loading sets the scale.

** The control plane

No new verb and no new service. The pieces that exist carry the new number:

- *The switch.* =synthetic.v1.ops.pin_market_regime= stays as it is. Auto, Calm
  and Active move the rate and the volatility together, because a person pressing
  Active expects the prices to move, not only the clock.
- *The read.* =get_market_regime_response= gains the volatility multiplier beside
  the rate multiplier, so the shell's =synthetic regime get= and the BFF's route
  report both without a new subject.
- *The screen.* The Market regime tab shows both multipliers and the same raster;
  a feed's Arrivals tab gains one field, =volatility sensitivity=, beside
  =sensitivity=, edited and audited exactly as that one is.
- *Permissions.* Unchanged: the same two subjects, the same session rules, the
  same permissions.

The tests each part must pass:

| Part | Test |
|------+------|
| Sampling against time | A rate multiplier of two leaves the variance per wall-clock second unchanged for a process that takes the gap, and a fixed-step process is refused or converted before it can be re-rated |
| The loading, calibration | Over a long run with the heat on, the realized sigma equals the configured sigma |
| The loading, response | A pinned active market raises the realized sigma by the feed's loading, and calm lowers it |
| The tails | The kurtosis of returns rises with activity while the mean and the long-run sigma hold |
| The control plane | The switch, the read and the tab carry both multipliers without a new subject or permission |

** Out of scope: activity and volatility exciting each other

The complete object is a *bivariate* Hawkes: activity excites volatility and
volatility excites activity, and today's heat is one coordinate of it. The
rank-one kernel of the arrival note generalises:

  K = diag(n) + m * 1'

becomes a 2x2 block of the same shape, and stability stops being the scalar test
=max n + sum m < 1= and becomes a spectral radius: the largest eigenvalue of the
mean kernel must be below one. That is a model of its own, it depends on all of
the above, and it is captured rather than scoped here:
[[id:4A00CBE5-E42B-46A8-A09B-FD698A09E4BB][Make activity and volatility excite
each other]].

* Literature

Every part of this approach is a named idea with a paper behind it, and the ones
it deliberately does not adopt matter as much as the ones it does. Grouped by the
section that leans on each entry, with one line on what it gives us.

** Activity and volatility are one latent process

- [[https://jstor.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1973/01/01/subordinated-stochastic-process-model-finite-variance][Clark (1973), *A subordinated stochastic process model with finite variance for
  speculative prices*, Econometrica 41(1)]] — the mixture-of-distributions
  hypothesis: returns are a mixture over an unobservable information-arrival
  rate, and tick counts and |returns| are two proxies for it. The paper the
  loading's "variance proportional to activity" comes from.
- Easley and O'Hara (1992), *Time and the process of security price adjustment*,
  Journal of Finance 47(2) — trading time carries information, so a law indexed
  by clock time misses the variable that matters.
- [[https://acnpsearch.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/75764135][Ané and Geman (2000),
  *Order flow, transaction clock, and normality of asset returns*, Journal of
  Finance 55(5)]] — near-normal in transaction time, fat-tailed in calendar time.
  The sharpest case for keeping sampling and time apart, which is the story's
  first task.
- Engle (2000), *The econometrics of ultra-high-frequency data*, Econometrica
  68(1) — durations and returns estimated jointly: the statistical shape of what
  this note proposes.
- Engle and Russell (1998), *Autoregressive conditional duration*, Econometrica
  66(5) — the durations model the arrival processes echo.
- Karpoff (1987), *The relation between price changes and trading volume: a
  survey*, Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 22(1) — the empirical
  volume-volatility relation the factor stands in for.
- Easley, López de Prado and O'Hara (2012), *The volume clock*, Journal of
  Portfolio Management 39(1) — sampling on activity rather than on the clock, in
  practice rather than in theory.
- Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014), *The price impact of order book events*,
  Journal of Financial Econometrics 12(1) — the measured link between event
  intensity and short-horizon volatility.

** Business time

- Bochner (1955), *Harmonic Analysis and the Theory of Probability* — subordinating
  one process by another: the mathematics of a clock that runs on activity.
- Mandelbrot and Taylor (1967), *On the distribution of stock price differences*,
  Operations Research 15(6) — operation time, the first trading-time formulation.
- Madan and Seneta (1990), *The variance gamma model for share market returns*,
  Journal of Business 63(4) — a time change that already carries the tail, and the
  prior art for gamma rather than activity as the clock.
- Carr and Wu (2004), *Time-changed Lévy processes and option pricing*, Journal of
  Financial Economics 71(1) — time-changing a process *with jumps*, which is the
  honest version of "the tails move with activity".

** Stochastic volatility and regime switching — the models not adopted

- Engle (1982), *Autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity*, Econometrica
  50(4), and Bollerslev (1986), *Generalized autoregressive conditional
  heteroskedasticity*, Journal of Econometrics 31(3) — volatility clustering as a
  process on variance itself. The standard the generated series will be compared
  against, and what this design is not.
- Hull and White (1987), *The pricing of options on assets with stochastic
  volatilities*, Journal of Finance 42(2), and Heston (1993), *A closed-form
  solution for options with stochastic volatility*, Review of Financial Studies
  6(2) — stochastic volatility as a second factor with its own law: vol-of-vol and
  the leverage effect, neither of which activity-driven variance reproduces.
- Hamilton (1989), *A new approach to the economic analysis of nonstationary time
  series and the business cycle*, Econometrica 57(2) — the regime-switching
  ancestor of the calm/active chain.

** Hawkes, endogenous activity, and multivariate stability

- Hawkes (1971), *Spectra of some self-exciting and mutually exciting point
  processes*, Biometrika 58(1) — the model, and already the mutually exciting
  form: activity exciting volatility is a use of it, not an extension.
- Ozaki (1979), *Maximum likelihood estimation of Hawkes' self-exciting point
  processes* — how such a process is fitted, which is what a calibration path
  would need.
- Ogata (1981), *On Lewis' simulation method for point processes*, IEEE
  Transactions on Information Theory 27(1) — thinning, the method the arrival
  processes use.
- Daley and Vere-Jones (2003), *An Introduction to the Theory of Point
  Processes* — the reference for the conditions the kernel must satisfy.
- Bacry, Delattre, Hoffmann and Muzy (2013), *Modelling microstructure noise with
  mutually exciting point processes*, Quantitative Finance 13(1) — the closest
  published model to what the platform runs today.
- [[https://scholar.google.com.ec/citations?view_op=view_citation&hl=ja&user=JII6yu8AAAAJ&citation_for_view=JII6yu8AAAAJ:MXK_kJrjxJIC][Bacry,
  Mastromarino and Muzy (2015), *Hawkes processes in finance*, Market
  Microstructure and Liquidity 1(1)]] — the survey that states the multivariate
  stability conditions.
- Brémaud and Massoulié (1996), *Stability of nonlinear Hawkes processes*, Annals
  of Probability 24(3) — why a scalar rank-one test becomes a spectral radius.
- [[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X15000264][Aït-Sahalia,
  Cacho-Diaz and Laeven (2015), *Modeling financial contagion using mutually
  exciting jump processes*, Journal of Financial Economics 117(3)]] — contagion as
  two mutually exciting jump processes: the bivariate design with jumps, and its
  empirical case.

** The tails

- Merton (1976), *Option pricing when underlying stock returns are
  discontinuous*, Journal of Financial Economics 3(1) — the jump-diffusion whose
  tail a diffusion scale cannot reproduce.
- Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2006), *Econometrics of testing for jumps using
  bipower variation*, Journal of Financial Econometrics 4(1) — how a jump is told
  from a diffusion in observed data, which is how our tail test should be written.

** Cross-series correlation: asynchrony and the Epps effect

- Epps (1979), *Comovements in stock prices in the very short run*, Journal of the
  American Statistical Association 74(366) — measured correlation falls as the
  sampling interval shrinks. The limitation on any claim that cross-series
  correlation rises with the factor: it must be measured over a stated interval.
- Lo and MacKinlay (1990), *An econometric analysis of nonsynchronous trading*,
  Journal of Econometrics 45(1-2) — non-synchronous observations bias cross-series
  statistics, which is what desynchronising the feeds trades one way and the other.

** Long memory and intraday seasonality

- Andersen and Bollerslev (1997), *Intraday periodicity and volatility
  persistence*, Journal of Empirical Finance 4(2-3) — the intraday U-shape real
  markets have and the model does not.
- Admati and Pfleiderer (1988), *A theory of intraday patterns*, Review of
  Financial Studies 1(1) — why that shape exists: liquidity traders cluster.
- Baillie, Bollerslev and Mikkelsen (1996), *Fractionally integrated
  GARCH*, Journal of Econometrics 74(1) — the long memory in volatility that a
  finite-decay heat cannot reproduce.

** Sampling, noise and what "volatility" means in a test

- Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Labys (2003), *Modeling and forecasting
  realized volatility*, Econometrica 71(2) — realized variance as an estimator and
  its sampling dependence.
- Hansen and Lunde (2006), *Realized variance and market microstructure noise*,
  Journal of Business and Economic Statistics 24(2) — how much of realized
  variance can be noise at high frequency.
- Zhang, Mykland and Aït-Sahalia (2005), *A tale of two time scales*, Journal of
  the American Statistical Association 100(472) — noise biases realized variance
  upward, more so the more often you sample. The reason every test must say which
  volatility it measures.

* Limitations

What this design does not do, and what the papers above say about it. A note that
lists only its support is a sales document.

** Scope: what is not modelled

- *Not a stochastic volatility model.* There is no vol-of-vol, no leverage effect
  (returns feeding back into variance) and no correlation between price and
  variance as there is in Hull–White and Heston. Variance is a deterministic
  function of the activity factor, so the generated surface has one source of
  volatility randomness where a real one has two.
- *No jumps of its own.* Activity reweights a static mixture; a jump is not an
  event that arrives, excites the heat and excites further jumps. Aït-Sahalia et
  al. and Merton model the jump as a process; this design sets its probability.
- *Two regimes and fixed rates.* The calm/active chain is two states with
  transition rates a person sets; Hamilton's class allows many states and an
  estimated transition matrix. There is no parameter uncertainty and no regime
  uncertainty beyond the chain itself.
- *No intraday seasonality and no long memory.* Andersen and Bollerslev (1997)
  and Admati and Pfleiderer (1988) document the U-shape every real intraday series
  has; Baillie et al. (1996) document the long memory. The factor produces neither,
  so a generated day is stationary in a way a real day is not.
- *No explicit microstructure noise.* The bid-ask bounce that makes real
  high-frequency data noisy is absent; a client computing realized variance from
  our ticks gets a cleaner number than from a real feed of the same frequency.
  Bacry et al. (2013) model that noise explicitly, and Zhang et al. (2005) show
  why its absence changes the answer.

** Identification: what is assumed rather than estimated

- *The loading is a free parameter.* Nothing in the platform estimates it from
  data; it is set by hand, and calibration means "the long-run sigma is the one
  the config states", not "the loading matches a market".
- *Variance proportional to activity is a choice, not a measurement.* Clark leaves
  the mixing variable latent; this design makes it an observable factor and fixes
  the exponent to one. The exponent is a knob because the evidence does not settle
  it.
- *The heat is not the only latent variable.* A real market's information arrival
  is what Clark's mixture is over; ours is a constructed heat whose jump and decay
  are demo constants, so the factor is a stand-in for a latent process that in
  reality is unobserved.

** Measurement: what our own tests cannot see

- *"Volatility" means two things* — the process's sigma and the sigma a client
  computes from published ticks — and sampling frequency moves the second even
  when the first is unchanged. A test that does not say which one it measures can
  pass while the product is wrong.
- *Cross-series correlation is interval-dependent.* The Epps effect means a claim
  that a pinned market correlates the series must state the window; measured at
  tick frequency it will look weaker than at one minute, and desynchronising the
  feeds changes the number again (Lo and MacKinlay 1990).
- *A synthetic series cannot validate the model.* Every statistic here is
  measured on data the model produced, so calibration is proved against the
  configuration, never against a market. Fitting the factor to real ticks is
  outside this story and would need Ozaki's or Bacry's estimation machinery.

** Limits of the cited work, as they apply here

- Clark's mixture identifies activity only through its proxies; two very different
  activity processes can produce the same return distribution, so a generated
  series matching the returns does not prove the activity is right.
- Hawkes models are stable only inside their kernel's radius; our rank-one
  condition is necessary for the bivariate case but not sufficient, which is why
  the stability test has to become a spectral radius before two dimensions exist.
- The Hawkes literature is fitted to message or trade arrivals, not to a shared
  heat across asset classes; the enrolment-and-share device this platform uses to
  keep the heat bounded is an engineering choice with no paper behind it, and its
  long-run effect on cross-series correlation has not been measured.


* Deferred, and what would make each matter

None of the limitations above is in scope for the tasks this note scopes, and
none should be. They are listed here with the condition that would make each one
worth a story, so the decision is taken later on evidence rather than on memory.

| Limitation | What would make it matter | Where it would live |
|------------+---------------------------+---------------------|
| No vol-of-vol and no leverage effect | A generated surface has to price an option off the series, so skew and the price/variance correlation are read | New story: a second factor with its own law |
| Jumps reweighted, not arrived | A stress scenario needs a gap that spreads across series, not a wider walk in one | Extends task "Let the factor reach the tails" |
| Two regimes with hand-set rates | A scenario needs a third state, a rating ladder, or an estimated transition matrix | Extends whatever sets the chain's rates |
| No intraday seasonality | Generated ticks feed a realized-volatility or VaR backtest whose window spans a day | New task on the synthetic service |
| No long memory | A client compares our volatility persistence against a real instrument's | With the seasonality task, or a separate one |
| No microstructure noise | Someone validates our output against a real feed's high-frequency statistics, or prices an engine sensitive to noise | New task, following Bacry et al. |
| The loading is set, not estimated | Generated series have to be calibrated to a real instrument rather than to a configuration | New story: estimation rather than generation |
| Cross-series correlation depends on the window | A report claims the regime correlates the series, which the arrival story's acceptance already asks for | Measurement work inside that report |
| The rank-one stability test is not sufficient in two dimensions | The bivariate Hawkes is taken up | The capture for it, in the inbox |
| The enrolment-and-share device is unpublished | The enrolled population grows past a few hundred series, or a client asks why counts correlate as they do | An investigation against the shared heat |

The first two carry the most weight for a demonstration: a panic that cannot gap
and cannot skew is a panic a person recognises as synthetic. The rest become
important as the platform's output is used for something other than display.
