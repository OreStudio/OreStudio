#+description: What an asset class is, the eight ORE Studio asset classes and what each covers, and how the ORE and FpML coding schemes differ.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :trading:refdata:taxonomy:

* Summary

Wikipedia defines an asset class as "a group of marketable financial
assets that have similar financial characteristics and behave
similarly in the marketplace". It adds that assets in one class are
often, though not always, subject to the same laws and regulations.

ORE defines neither the concept nor a rationale for its own values,
and the ORE Studio descriptions characterise each class by /the risk
factor that drives value/: an interest rate product is priced off a
yield curve, an equity product off a spot price and a volatility
surface, a credit product off a hazard rate curve. Each class is
accordingly associated with a characteristic set of market data. That
characterisation is ours; no upstream source states it.

Eight asset classes are held, modelled as
[[id:8F6EE5AE-8B49-4A4C-97F8-E0517BE53775][=asset_class_code=]]: =fx=, =rates=, =credit=, =equity=, =commodity=,
=inflation=, =bond= and =cross_asset=.

*The classification is primarily a market data taxonomy.* The typed
enumeration is =ores::marketdata::api::domain::asset_class=, and every
one of its values is documented in terms of market data: =rates= is
"interest rate curves, vol surfaces and spreads", =equity= is "equity
spot prices, forwards, dividends and vol surfaces", =cross_asset= is
"cross-asset correlations". It is carried by =market_series= and
=feed_binding=, where it is the coarse taxonomy used for filtering,
and by =instrument_code=.

It is not a property of the product type. =trade_type= carries
=product_type=, not an asset class. A trade carries an optional
=asset_class= in its classification group, described in the model as
an override, so the value on a trade is an exception rather than the
norm.

* The eight asset classes

Each section opens with a general definition and then states what the
class covers here, including the market data a product of that class
requires.

** FX

Wikipedia defines the foreign exchange market as "a global
decentralized or over-the-counter (OTC) market for the trading of
currencies".

Covers FX spot, forwards, swaps, and vanilla and exotic options, with
the market data that prices them: spot rates, forward points, and
volatility surfaces quoted by currency pair.

FX is unique among the asset classes in that every trade inherently
involves two currencies rather than one underlying. That is why FX
risk appears as a cross-cutting concern in every other asset class: a
USD-denominated equity option still carries FX risk for a EUR-based
book.

** Rates

Wikipedia defines an interest rate derivative as "a derivative whose
payments are determined through calculation techniques where the
underlying benchmark product is an interest rate, or set of different
interest rates".

Covers deposits, forward rate agreements, interest rate swaps and
swaptions, caps and floors, and cross-currency swaps, together with
the curve-construction market data: discount factors, zero rates, and
forward rates bootstrapped from quoted instruments at standard tenors.

Rates is typically the first curve built in any pricing pipeline,
because discounting and forward-rate projection for every other asset
class ultimately depends on it.

** Credit

Wikipedia defines a credit derivative as any one of "various
instruments and techniques designed to separate and then transfer the
credit risk", moving default risk to a party other than the original
lender or debt holder.

Covers credit default swaps, both single-name and index, credit-linked
notes, and the underlying market data: CDS spreads, hazard rate curves
bootstrapped from those spreads, and assumed recovery rates.

Credit curves are quoted per reference entity, or per index series,
and combine with a rates curve to produce risky discount factors for
defaultable cashflows.

** Equity

Wikipedia defines stock as consisting of "all the shares by which
ownership of a corporation or company is divided".

Covers equity spot and forward positions, vanilla and exotic equity
options including barrier, Asian and variance-swap structures, and the
associated market data: spot prices, dividend yield curves, and
implied volatility surfaces quoted by strike and expiry.

Equity derivatives pricing layers a dividend and borrow-cost forward
curve and a volatility surface on top of the rates discount curve.

** Commodity

Wikipedia defines a commodity market as "a market that trades in the
primary economic sector rather than manufactured products".

Covers commodity forwards, swaps, and options including Asian and
spread structures, with forward curves and volatility surfaces per
delivery point and contract month.

Commodity forward curves differ in kind from FX and rates curves.
They are often in backwardation or contango, driven by storage cost
and convenience yield rather than by pure interest-rate parity.

** Inflation

Wikipedia defines an inflation derivative as "an over-the-counter and
exchange-traded derivative that is used to transfer inflation risk
from one counterparty to another".

Covers zero-coupon and year-on-year inflation swaps, inflation caps
and floors, and inflation-linked bonds, with the inflation fixing and
index curve, both historical and forward-projected, and inflation
volatility surfaces.

The index curve carries a seasonality adjustment for the index's known
within-year pattern, and inflation curves are built and discounted
against the nominal rates curve for the same currency.

** Bond

Wikipedia defines a bond as "a type of security under which the issuer
(debtor) owes the holder (creditor) a debt".

Covers vanilla fixed and floating-rate bonds, callable and convertible
bonds, and bond futures and options, with clean and dirty prices,
yield to maturity, and issuer spread curves quoted over the risk-free
or asset-swap curve.

A bond's value derives from a discount curve plus an issuer-specific
credit and liquidity spread. Government bonds are also a primary
source instrument for bootstrapping the risk-free rates curve itself,
so the class both consumes and supplies curve data.

** Cross asset

No single Wikipedia article defines this class, because it is a
modelling category rather than a market. The nearest general concept
is the hybrid security, which Wikipedia defines as one of "a broad
group of securities that combine the characteristics of the two
broader groups of securities, debt and equity".

Covers instruments and risk measures that genuinely span more than one
of the other classes: a hybrid note whose payoff depends jointly on an
equity index and an FX rate, or a correlation or quanto adjustment
that couples an equity or commodity underlying denominated in a
foreign currency back to the book's base currency. The market data is
cross-asset correlations and quanto adjustments.

This category exists for the small set of cases that a single-asset
tag would misrepresent. It is not a catch-all default, and a product
that fits one class belongs in that class.

* Two coding schemes

Asset class is not one taxonomy. Two are registered as coding schemes,
and they are close enough to be confused and different enough to
matter.

| ORE | FpML |
|-----+------|
| =fx= | =ForeignExchange= |
| =rates= | =InterestRate= |
| =credit= | =Credit= |
| =equity= | =Equity= |
| =commodity= | =Commodity= |
| =inflation= | =Inflation= |
| =bond= | =Bond= |
| =cross_asset= | — |
| — | =SecuritiesFinancing= |

Six values correspond directly and differ only in spelling. Two do
not. ORE carries =cross_asset=, which FpML has no equivalent for
because FpML classifies a hybrid by its product rather than by a
spanning category. FpML carries =SecuritiesFinancing= for repo and
securities lending, which ORE has no equivalent for.

A mapping between the two is therefore partial in both directions, and
an asset class value is meaningless without the scheme it belongs to.
[[id:1A95DFA1-932C-B094-4653-BFCAD266C2AD][Coding schemes]] sets out the mechanism that records which scheme a
value came from.

* Modeling notes

The three systems attach the classification to different things, and
that difference explains why only one of them needs a cross-asset
value at all.

** How ORE models it

ORE holds =enum class AssetClass { EQ, FX, COM, IR, INF, CR, BOND,
BOND_INDEX, PORTFOLIO_DETAILS }= in =ored/utilities/parsers.hpp=.

ORE does not define what an asset class is, and gives no rationale for
its values. The user guide uses the term throughout but never
definitionally: it says "depending on the underlying asset class" when
explaining why a barrier option is an =FxGenericBarrierOption= or a
=CommodityGenericBarrierOption=, and it constrains some products so
that "all underlyings must be from the same asset class". The term
consistently describes the underlying.

The user guide also counts six asset classes, not nine. Its product
coverage is IR, FX, EQ, COM, CR and INF, and the scripted trade module
covers "five of the six asset classes covered in ORE". The remaining
three enum values are not asset classes in that sense.

It attaches that enum to *underlyings*, not to trades. =Trade= declares
=virtual std::map<AssetClass, std::set<std::string>>
underlyingIndices(...)=, so a trade reports the indices it requires
grouped by asset class. One trade can therefore report indices under
several asset classes at once, and ORE never has to decide which
single class the trade belongs to.

That is why ORE has no cross-asset value. A hybrid note on an equity
index and an FX rate reports indices under =EQ= and =FX=, and the two
entries say more than one spanning tag could.

=BOND_INDEX= separates an index from a single bond, and
=PORTFOLIO_DETAILS= marks portfolio-level data rather than a market.
Neither is an asset class in the user guide's sense, which is
consistent with the guide counting six.

** How FpML models it

FpML publishes asset class as a coding scheme, and classifies the
*product*. Its values are =ForeignExchange=, =InterestRate=, =Credit=,
=Equity=, =Commodity=, =Inflation=, =Bond= and =SecuritiesFinancing=.

FpML needs no cross-asset value either, for a different reason. A
hybrid is classified by which product definition it is, and FpML has a
product for it, so the spanning case never reaches the classification.
=SecuritiesFinancing= exists because repo and securities lending are a
distinct product family in FpML rather than a distinct market.

** How ORE Studio models it

Asset class here classifies *market data series*, not products. The
typed enumeration is =ores::marketdata::api::domain::asset_class=, and
=market_series= and =feed_binding= carry it as the coarse taxonomy for
filtering. =asset_class_code= is the reference data table behind it.

A trade carries an optional =asset_class= in its classification field
group, described in the model as an override. =trade_type= carries no
asset class at all; its classification attribute is =product_type=,
whose values are =swap=, =fx=, =bond=, =credit=, =equity=,
=commodity=, =composite= and =scripted=.

*** Why the count differs from ORE's

The classification answers a different question from ORE's, so the
two memberships are not comparable and neither is a subset of the
other.

ORE's =AssetClass= classifies the underlyings a trade references.
Ours classifies the market data a series carries. A taxonomy of market
data needs a value for data that relates two classes at once, and
=cross_asset= is exactly that: the enumeration documents it as
"cross-asset correlations". A correlation between an equity index and
an FX rate is one series, and it belongs to neither class alone.

ORE needs no such value because a correlation is not a trade
underlying; it is an input a model consumes. The spanning case never
reaches ORE's enumeration.

The reverse holds too. ORE carries =BOND_INDEX= and
=PORTFOLIO_DETAILS=, which have no counterpart here because they
distinguish kinds of trade underlying rather than kinds of market
data.

So the eight values are not a claim that ORE Studio supports products
ORE does not. The product catalogue is bounded by ORE's =TradeType=
enumeration, and [[id:AB71AFF8-0B08-4610-9F12-C5A84D8AB892][trade modeling]] records that ORE is the ground truth
for it. The asset class enumeration is bounded by the market data we
publish, which is a different set with a different shape.

** The scheme named ORE is not ORE's enum

The coding scheme registered here as =ORE_ASSET_CLASS= holds =fx=,
=rates=, =credit=, =equity=, =commodity=, =inflation=, =bond= and
=cross_asset=. ORE's own =AssetClass= enum holds =EQ=, =FX=, =COM=,
=IR=, =INF=, =CR=, =BOND=, =BOND_INDEX= and =PORTFOLIO_DETAILS=.

The two differ in spelling throughout, and in membership at three
points: we carry =cross_asset=, which ORE does not; ORE carries
=BOND_INDEX= and =PORTFOLIO_DETAILS=, which we do not.

The scheme therefore records the classification ORE Studio uses for
market data series and trades, which was derived from ORE's
vocabulary rather than taken from it. A reader who expects
=ORE_ASSET_CLASS= to be ORE's enum will be wrong at three values and
at every spelling.

* Where the classification is used

Its primary use is filtering market data. =market_series= and
=feed_binding= carry it, and the Qt interface drives a combo filter
from the same values.

[[id:C5C95B29-2EF9-4DFC-9E04-428C41C1925D][ORE Products by Asset Class]] also groups the product pages by asset
class, but that grouping is a documentation convention rather than a
modelled attribute: no column on =trade_type= holds it. A product page
states an asset class because a reader looking for bond products
expects to find them together, not because the catalogue is keyed that
way.
