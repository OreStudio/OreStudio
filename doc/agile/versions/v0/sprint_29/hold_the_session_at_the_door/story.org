* Goal

A browser holds the bearer token today, in a place a script on the page can reach.
One injection owns the session for its lifetime, no deployment can revoke it, and
the token itself is the thing that leaks. A machine client should hold a token: that
is what a token is for. A browser should hold something that means nothing on its own
and that the server can end.

After this story the browser holds an opaque identifier in an HttpOnly cookie, and the
token stays in the server's memory, keyed by the identifier's SHA-256 so a dump of
that memory is not a set of usable credentials. Sign in and sign out are resources of
their own, =/api/v1/auth/sessions=, and a =me= alias answers who the caller is without
handing the token back.

* Acceptance

- The cookie carries a 256-bit identifier and nothing else, and the store keys on its
  SHA-256.
- The record holds the bearer token, the party, the tenant, the account, when it was
  made and when it was last used.
- Expiry slides on use and the server evicts what expires; signing in mints a new
  identifier rather than reusing one.
- =HttpOnly= and =SameSite=Lax= always, =Secure= and the =__Host-= prefix over TLS, and
  a name with the environment's suffix so two deployments on one host do not collide.
- =POST /api/v1/auth/sessions= signs in and =DELETE= signs out, and both are resources
  rather than operations on a component.
- =GET /api/v1/auth/sessions/me= answers the caller's identity, and no answer carries
  the token.
- An unsafe method on a session request carries the deployment's =Origin= and a custom
  header, or it is refused.

* Decisions

- The store is in the gateway's memory, not in the database. A session is a fact about
  a connection's custody and not about the domain, and the design puts it there; the
  cost is that a restart signs everybody out, which is the correct default for a
  credential.
- The identifier is random and opaque, and the store keys on its hash. A dump of the
  store then yields nothing a caller could present.
- The cookie is a transport capability, not an identity: a machine client may present
  it too, and a bearer token keeps working beside it.
- CSRF is refused by the gateway, before any handler: an unsafe method on a session
  request must prove intent, and a route should not have to remember to ask.
* Out of scope

- Delegated NATS calls per session. The port needs them, and they are their own
  increment.
- Switching party within a session.
- A shared stream per session across tabs.
