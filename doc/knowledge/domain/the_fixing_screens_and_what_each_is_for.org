Naming them apart is what keeps a party's control out of the system's facts.

* Summary

The word *fixings* names four surfaces in ORE Studio, and only one of them is
the fixings manager. Each answers one question for one actor, and the whole
confusion this note answers comes from reading any two of them as the same
screen with more or fewer buttons.

* The four questions

#+ATTR_HTML: :class hug-leading
| Screen | The question it answers | Actor | Writes | State |
|--------+-------------------------+-------+--------+-------|
| *Fixings* (the system view) | What did the sources state, and how far does each series reach? | Super administrator | Nothing | Prototype, task STARTED |
| *Public rates* | Is each public publisher arriving, on what terms, and may we fetch more of it? | System administrator | Terms, a pause, a backfill request | Built |
| *Fixings manager* (the party's) | Which fixings do my trades require, what does the party use, and is it official yet? | Party operations; front office keys | The party fixing: authorise, override, undo, enter a day | Not built; documented and captured |
| *Fixing series* (a drill-down) | What are the values of this one item, day by day? | Anyone the first two let in | Nothing | Built, unreachable |

The names to use, from here on:

#+ATTR_HTML: :class hug-leading
| Term | What it names | Not to be confused with |
|------+---------------+-------------------------|
| *Fixing observation* | The value a source publishes for one index on one date, stored once for the installation | The party fixing |
| *Party fixing* | One party's adoption of an observation for its own trades, with its status and any override | The observation |
| *Fixings* (the system view) | The list of every fixing series the platform holds, one row each, read only. *Fixings browser* is a good informal name for it | The fixings manager |
| *Fixings manager* | The party-level screen where a party's fixings are required, adopted, overridden, authorised and undone | Any system-level view |
| *Fixing series page* | One item by its address, the drill-down from both | A time series, which is another page |

* Why the system view is not the manager

The two read the same values and share nothing else.

The system view states what the platform observed, and it states the days it
did not observe: a derived index whose curve stopped shows a series that ends.
It offers no authorisation, because authorisation is a control over a party's
books and would let one party decide another's settlements. It is a view of
facts, and the only act it enables is to open a series.

The fixings manager decides what one party's trades fix on. Its rows are the
fixings the party's live trades require, whether or not any source stated them;
its act is to adopt the observation, override it, authorise it or undo that, and
every act is recorded and raises the event that fixes the party's trades. A
party may override a value it agreed with a client, and the override applies to
its trades alone, so two parties hold one observation as official and unofficial
at the same moment.

The consequence is the one this note exists to keep straight: a day the platform
never stated is a hole in the system view and a decision in the manager. The
system does not fill it, and the manager does not hide it.

* What each screen must not do

- The *system view* must not offer an override, an authorisation, or a way to
  fill a missing day. A screen that shows a hole and offers to fill it is the
  manager wearing the system view's title.
- The *fixings manager* must not change an observation: a correction is the
  publisher's act and reaches every party alike.
- *Public rates* must not publish into a party's trades, and it must say which
  feed it reads, because a second feed of one publisher is another way to the
  same fixing.
- The *fixing series page* must stay an act-free drill-down, whichever screen
  sent the person there.

* The open question: is the process a screen of its own?

The fixing process — observation, authorisation, application and reversal — has
a question of its own: *which trades does this fixing affect, and did the process
run on all of them?* Two readings are defensible and one has to be chosen before
the manager is built.

| Option | What it means | What it costs |
|--------+---------------+---------------|
| A. The manager shows it | The selected fixing carries its affected trades, and the control reports are a second tab of the same screen | One screen with two tabs; the trade read is scoped to the selected fixing |
| B. A screen of its own | A fixings process screen over a date range: what fixed, what did not, and the trades each touches | A second screen reading the same party fixings, and a person must know which to open |

This note recommends *A*, for one reason the documents already state: the
manager's rows *are* the party's fixings, and the affected trades are a property
of one row. A separate process screen would read the same rows and offer the same
acts, so the person would have to learn which of two screens is the real one.
The control reports stay reports — they answer whether the process ran, not what
this fixing did — and they open from the manager.

The decision is the product owner's; it is recorded here so the manager is not
built twice.

* Contradictions this note corrects

- The fixing series page and the new list both claim =/market-data/fixings=.
- *Super administrator* and *system administrator* are one actor written two
  ways; this note uses *super administrator* for the system view and reserves
  *system administrator* for the installation's own operations.
- *Fixings manager*, *party fixings manager*, *the party's fixings manager* and
  *fixings view* are one screen; this note uses *fixings manager*.
- A sprint 29 scenario calls the series page "Fixings"; the page is a *fixing
  series page*, and *Fixings* is the list.
- The controls page says ORE Studio has neither view yet; the system view is
  being built, and this note states which half is which.
