#+title: GJR-GARCH sets the daily variance of FX spot
#+description: Why FX spot carries a GJR-GARCH variance from day to day, how it scales the GMM's shocks, how it is calibrated from five years of fixings, and how it composes with the intraday volatility channel.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :marketdata:synthetic:garch:fx:knowledge:
#+created: 2026-11-03
#+updated: 2026-11-03

* Summary

A synthetic FX spot feed carries a GJR-GARCH variance from one market day to
the next. The GMM of the feed still gives the shape of each shock, its fat tails
and its regimes; GJR-GARCH gives the size of the day's shocks, so a volatile day
makes the next day volatile too, and a fall can raise volatility more than a
rise. The variance is calibrated from five years of daily fixings, with its
long-run level fixed to the realised variance and the rest estimated by quasi
maximum likelihood. Within the day the volatility channel spreads the day's
variance over busy and quiet periods, so the two models work at different time
scales and multiply. GARCH sets spot only. The volatility surface reads none of
its state; it has processes of its own, as
[[id:3C5FA141-16E5-4505-A36D-F6C29AE7679B][A volatility surface moves through its SSVI parameters]]
describes.

* Detail

** The decision, and what it changes

Before 2026-11-03 the knowledge base gave GARCH two different roles, and the
code gave it none:

- [[id:BB2B7C4C-FD4E-4DCA-BF1D-00F929622B87][Synthetic market data generation: approach]]
  used GJR-GARCH for *volatility surfaces*. It carried a GARCH variance from
  day to day and passed it to the MDN, which produced the surface. GARCH did not
  touch spot generation there.
- [[id:9D778F72-8FF6-4F01-AF4B-696DC7BA855A][Market volatility channel]] cites
  GARCH under "the models not adopted": volatility clustering as a process on
  variance itself, "what this design is not".
- The code has no GARCH process. FX spot is the GMM process of
  [[id:7493D798-DD0D-43A5-8DAB-6E63377253DC][The GMM component]], calibrated in
  level and volatility from public fixings.

The decision, taken on 2026-11-03 together with dropping the MDN: GJR-GARCH
drives the daily variance of FX spot, and it plays no part in the surface. Both
earlier notes are updated to say so. The approach document's section "Vol
surface update via GARCH state propagation" no longer applies, because the MDN
it fed is gone. The volatility channel keeps its own scope, the intraday, and
this note states how the two compose.

** Why spot needs a daily memory

The GMM draws every shock independently. The approach document says what that
costs, in its section "Preserving volatility clustering": i.i.d. draws destroy
the temporal autocorrelation of volatility, so a calm day is as likely after a
crash as after another calm day. Real FX returns are not like that. Large moves
come in clusters that last days to weeks, and
[[id:351CB816-28F5-428A-A289-414B6F4B9F36][GARCH volatility models]] explains
the stylised fact and the model.

The volatility channel adds clustering of a different kind. Its shared heat
rises with activity and fades with a decay of minutes to hours, so it clusters
volatility within the day. It does not carry a calm or a stressed state from one
day to the next, and its own literature section records two things
activity-driven variance does not give: the leverage effect, and a
volatility of volatility. GJR-GARCH gives the day-to-day memory and the
leverage effect, so it adds what the channel lacks rather than repeating it.

** The model

Let $r_d$ be the log return of spot over market day $d$, and $\sigma_d^2$ its
variance given everything up to the start of the day:

$$r_d = \mu + \varepsilon_d, \qquad \varepsilon_d = \sigma_d\, z_d$$

$$\sigma_{d+1}^2 = \omega + \left(\alpha + \gamma\,\mathbb{1}\{\varepsilon_d < 0\}\right)\varepsilon_d^2 + \beta\,\sigma_d^2$$

This is the GJR form of Glosten, Jagannathan and Runkle (1993). The
indicator adds $\gamma$ to the reaction after a fall, so with $\gamma > 0$ a
fall raises tomorrow's variance more than a rise of the same size.

*** The shock is the GMM, standardised

$z_d$ is not a normal draw. It is the feed's own GMM, standardised to mean zero
and variance one: if the mixture has mean $m$ and standard deviation $s$, then
$z = (x - m)/s$ for a draw $x$. The GMM keeps the job it does well, the shape
of a shock: fat tails, asymmetry, and the regimes of its components. GARCH
takes the job the GMM cannot do, the size of a shock as a function of the past.
This keeps every existing FX spot config valid: its components become the
shape, and the GARCH rows are added beside them.

The alternative the GARCH note describes, the skewed Student-t of Hansen
(1994), gives a shape with two parameters. It is not taken, because the GMM rows already exist,
are already calibrated from fixings, and already carry the tilt the volatility
channel applies to a law's tails.

*** The sign of the leverage in FX

In equity a fall is bad news, so $\gamma > 0$ always. In FX a "fall" depends on
how the pair is quoted: when USD/JPY falls, the yen strengthens, and in a
crisis the yen is often the currency that strengthens. So the asymmetry has a
direction per pair, and $\gamma$ may be negative. A negative $\gamma$ makes a
rise of the pair raise variance more than a fall. Variance stays positive if

$$\omega > 0, \quad \alpha \ge 0, \quad \beta \ge 0, \quad \alpha + \gamma \ge 0$$

so $\gamma$ ranges over $[-\alpha, \infty)$, and no separate direction flag is
needed.

*** Stationarity

The variance reverts to a long-run level only if the *persistence* is below
one:

$$p = \alpha + \beta + \gamma\,\kappa < 1, \qquad \kappa = \mathbb{E}\left[z^2\,\mathbb{1}\{z < 0\}\right]$$

For a symmetric shock $\kappa = 1/2$, which gives the familiar
$\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2 < 1$. The standardised GMM is not symmetric in
general, so $\kappa$ is computed from its components in closed form, and it is
computed once per config. The long-run variance is then

$$\bar{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - p}$$

and a shock decays with a half-life of $\ln(1/2) / \ln(p)$ days. Persistence
close to one, which daily FX returns commonly show, gives half-lives of weeks.
The GARCH note treats stationarity and the near-unit-root regime in depth.

** Calibration

The parameters come from the same daily fixings that already set the level of
the feed, read through the reader of
[[id:F4BBEF34-D84C-4AE6-A3AA-DC47C3EE06EE][Public sources publish daily fixings through an adapter]].
A GARCH fit needs years of data, not the 90-day window that sets the level, so
it reads five years of daily fixings. Every environment has them from a static
DQ snapshot of the public fixings, which the adapter brings up to date by
fetching only the days after its latest stored fixing.

1. *Variance targeting.* $\bar{\sigma}^2$ is the sample variance of the daily
   log returns, and $\omega = \bar{\sigma}^2 (1 - p)$. This removes one
   parameter from the fit and ties the long-run level to the data directly
   (Engle and Mezrich, 1996).
2. *Quasi maximum likelihood.* $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ maximise the
   Gaussian likelihood of the returns, subject to the positivity and
   stationarity bounds above. The Gaussian likelihood is used even though $z$ is
   a mixture: Bollerslev and Wooldridge (1992) show that the estimator is
   consistent when the conditional mean and variance are right, whatever the
   true shape. Five years is about 1,250 daily returns.
3. *The starting variance.* $\sigma_0^2$ is the last value of the fitted
   variance, filtered through the whole history. A feed that starts in a
   stressed period therefore starts stressed, not at the long-run level.
4. *A fallback.* When a pair has too little history for a stable fit, or the
   fit reaches the stationarity bound, the config carries authored values, as
   it carries authored GMM components today. The calibration result records
   which path it took, as the calibration measures already record how a start
   was resolved.

** Time: a GARCH day in a feed that ticks

GARCH is written per day. A spot feed ticks many times a day, at gaps the
arrival process chooses, as
[[id:F135B9AF-902F-46C5-847C-0820D8C92D28][Tick arrival]] describes. Three rules
join the two:

- *The day is the FX day.* A market day ends at the FX day roll, 17:00 New
  York time, on the market's operational clock. The variance $\sigma_d^2$ holds
  for the whole day and changes only at the roll.
- *A tick takes its share of the day.* A tick that covers a fraction $s$ of the
  day takes a shock whose variance is $\sigma_d^2\, s$. The GMM's
  components are scaled so the mixture's variance per day is $\sigma_d^2$:
  their deviations from the mixture mean are multiplied by $\sigma_d / s_0$,
  where $s_0$ is the mixture's own daily standard deviation, and the mixture's
  drift is left alone. This is the time-scaled rule the mixture already
  follows for =next(elapsed)=, with a scale that changes once a day.
- *The update reads the day's return.* At the roll, $\varepsilon_d$ is the log
  return of the generated path over the day, the sum of its ticks. The update
  needs nothing else.

** How GARCH and the volatility channel compose

The two models act at different time scales, and they multiply. GARCH sets how
much variance the day has. The volatility channel decides where in the day it
falls: its loading raises the variance of a tick in a busy period and lowers it
in a quiet one, normalised so that a run's volatility stays the volatility its
configuration states. Over a day, the expected variance is therefore still
$\sigma_d^2$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\text{tick}) = \sigma_d^2 \times L(\text{activity}) \times s, \qquad \mathbb{E}[L] = 1$$

The analysis this design came from asks for a feedback loop in both directions:
busy intraday trading should raise the next day's variance, and a high daily
variance should raise the baseline rate of the tick arrivals.

- *From the intraday to the day.* This direction needs no extra mechanism. A
  busy day, with the channel's loading high, produces a larger day return
  $\varepsilon_d$, and the GARCH update turns it into a larger
  $\sigma_{d+1}^2$. Realized GARCH (Hansen, Huang and Shek, 2012) formalises a
  variant that updates from an intraday realised variance instead of the
  squared day return, which is less noisy. Our generated path is known
  exactly, so its realised variance is free to compute; the variant is a
  possible refinement, not a requirement.
- *From the day to the intraday.* This direction is not adopted. A daily
  variance that raises the arrival rate is activity and volatility exciting
  each other, which the volatility channel leaves out of scope and captures as
  [[id:4A00CBE5-E42B-46A8-A09B-FD698A09E4BB][Make activity and volatility excite each other]].
  It belongs there, with that capture's stability condition.

** How it reaches the code

None of this exists yet. The intended shape, to be confirmed when the work
starts:

- A process type in =ores.analytics.quant= that wraps the geometric GMM
  process behind =i_stochastic_process=. It holds $\sigma_d^2$, the return
  accumulated since the roll and the time to the next roll. It supports
  =next(elapsed)=, so it runs under a Poisson or Hawkes clock, and it passes
  the volatility channel's tilt through to the mixture it wraps.
- Its parameters $(\alpha, \beta, \gamma)$, the target variance and the
  starting variance as rows beside the GMM components of the FX spot config,
  in the way the IR curve config holds its process parameter values.
- Validation that refuses a set outside the positivity and stationarity bounds,
  as the GMM's weight check refuses an all-zero set.
- The calibration step, beside the level calibration that already exists.

** What GARCH does not do

- It does not set the volatility surface. The surface's ATM level, skew and
  curvature have processes of their own; their shocks are correlated with the
  spot shocks, but they read none of the GARCH state. Their long-run levels
  come from the derived source, which reads the same fixings, so the two agree
  on the volatility of the pair without one driving the other.
- It does not model the weekend. FX does not trade from the Friday roll to the
  Sunday open, and the variance does not change over it.
- It is univariate. Two pairs that share a currency each have their own GARCH,
  and their variances do not move together. Joint variance across pairs is a
  multivariate GARCH, and the approach document's open decision OD-1 on joint
  modelling is where that belongs.
