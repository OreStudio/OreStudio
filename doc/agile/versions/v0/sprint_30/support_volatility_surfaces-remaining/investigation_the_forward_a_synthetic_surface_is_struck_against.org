#+title: Investigation: The forward a synthetic surface is struck against
#+description: Where the forward a synthetic FX volatility surface solves its strikes against comes from, when the system tenant is the vendor and publishes raw quotes only. A point-in-time record, written while the story that commissioned it was open, of an [[id:A7F8D9E2-3C4B-4A5D-B67C-8E9F0A1B2C3D][investigation]] into the forward a synthetic volatility surface is struck against.

It is a snapshot, not a description of the system. Whatever it concludes that the code must keep doing belongs on the page that owns the subject; cite that page from code, recipes and models, not this one.

* Context

A synthetic FX volatility surface publishes volatilities at delta labels --
=ATM=, =25RR=, =25BF=, =10RR=, =10BF=. A delta is not a strike. To find the
strike a quote belongs to, a reader solves for the strike at which the option
has that delta, in the market of its expiry:

  F(t, T) = S(t) * P_f(t, T) / P_d(t, T)

The forward is the whole of the quoted surface's frame: the ATM strike is it,
the wing strikes are placed around it, and the volatility at a delta is read
there. A surface quoted against a forward that its pair does not have is not
the pair's surface, however well its SSVI parameters behave.

The surface feed merged in #4673 quotes against =spot = 1=, =P_f = P_d = 1=,
so its forward is flat. That was deliberate and it is bounded: the emitter's
tests prove the quoted volatilities do not move by more than =1e-12= when the
spot level changes from 1.0 to 1.30, because a smile is read at a moneyness and
a common scale cancels; and they prove they do move by =7.8e-05= -- about
=0.008= volatility points -- when the ratio of the two discounts moves from one
to =0.99/0.97= at a unit spot. So a unit market costs exactly the rate
differential, and nothing else.

That cost is small, and it is not the reason this investigation exists. The
reason is that the surface is struck against one forward and read back against
another. The calibration on the party side resolves each expiry's market from
the party's published forward set, in =discounts_at= and =terms_at= of
=fx_vol_surface_calibration_plan.cpp=: an exact tenor where the set has one,
log-linear interpolation of both discount factors in days otherwise, base
currency as the foreign side. So the generator solves a strike with a flat
forward and the calibration reads that quote back with a real one. Every
surface a blueprint calibrates from these quotes is fitted to volatilities
observed at slightly wrong strikes, and nothing in the system reports it.

The question this investigation answers is therefore not "how do we improve
0.008 volatility points". It is: *where does the forward come from?* -- given
that the tenant which runs the generator is the vendor, and a vendor does not
publish discount curves.

Facts the code fixed, beyond the documents:

- *The run already holds a latent curve per currency, and it is not new work.*
  =i_yield_curve_process::discount_factor(ticks_ahead)= is documented as "the
  latent curve a single process state implies at every maturity, not just the
  next scalar tick", and =price_ir_curve_entry= prices every pillar of a curve
  feed -- deposit, FRA, swap -- off that one state. So the vendor's IR quotes
  are *observations of its own curve*, and the quote vector and the curve are
  two views of one object. The IR feed is not a quote generator that lacks a
  curve; it is a curve generator that publishes quotes.
- *The system has no other curve to read.* There is no market board, no
  published-curve read reachable from =ores.synthetic.service=, and no
  system-tenant schedule that publishes discount factors. =get_published_fx_forward=
  is scoped to the caller's party.
- *The surface's market is fixed when the emitter is built.* =fx_vol_slice_market=
  is passed to =fx_vol_surface_emitter::make= and never revised, so a curve that
  moved after the feed started would not reach the surface's strikes.
- *The two grids do not agree.* A curve feed steps a calendar day per tick and
  counts its horizon in ticks; the market's simulated time is the speed times
  operational time. Mapping a calendar tenor to the curve's own horizon is the
  publisher's job, which is why a shared curve has to travel as dated discount
  factors rather than as a process state.

* Paths taken

** What the documents and the literature require

The approach note states the requirement twice, and neither statement is
optional:

- Constraint 1: =FXFWD(T) = FX_spot * DF_for(T) / DF_dom(T)=, enforced by
  *derivation -- never sample FXFWD independently*, from steps 1 and 3 to step
  4.
- Constraint 3, "FX option ATMF anchor": the ATM strike of the surface is the
  =FXFWD= at each maturity, *automatic*, because "the surface's strikes are
  solved against the generated =FXFWD= from step 4".

The roadmap orders the work the same way: the FX option surface is Phase 2,
step 10, and step 4 -- the derived forward -- is a Phase 1 step. So the forward
is not something the surface arc can decide to do without; it is a dependency
the surface arc inherited and has not met.

The three frameworks agree, and this is the part worth stating precisely
because the surface arc has been built as if they did not.

- *Kienitz (2024)* generates from a Gaussian mixture over an *observation
  vector*: one joint distribution over the rates and, where it is conditioned,
  the surface too. His conditionals are the point of the method --
  "given this yield curve, what vol surface is consistent?". A surface generated
  with no reference to the curve is not a conditional of that joint law. It is
  an independent draw wearing the same parameterisation.
- *Van den Berg (2026a)* constructs the surface as a mixture of densities over
  the terminal underlying and enforces calendar-spread and butterfly
  no-arbitrage as conditions on those densities. Those conditions are stated
  against the forward: two expiries are compared at the same strike, and a
  strike is meaningful only as a moneyness. An interpolation that is
  arbitrage-free relative to one forward is not arbitrage-free relative to
  another, so the forward is an input to the construction.
- *Van den Berg (2026b)* is the sharpest case: the surface *is* the terminal
  return density of the underlying process, so the drift and the discounting
  come before the smile. There is no version of that object that does not know
  what it is a return on.

So the claim this investigation ends with is the owner's: *a latent volatility
surface implies a latent yield curve*. Not as an extra deliverable, and not
because a curve is a nice thing to have, but because the surface's parameters
are expressed relative to a forward, and the forward is a functional of the
rate state. In a system that generates the rate quotes itself, the rate state
is the generator's own.

Two caveats keep the claim honest. Neither paper says a *vendor* must publish a
curve: Kienitz holds the curve as a coordinate of the observation vector,
van den Berg takes the forward as given. And neither requires the curve to be
bootstrapped from the same quotes: what they require is that the forward used
is the one the quotes imply under covered interest parity, and that the surface
is conditioned on it. Our system is in a stronger position than either: it does
not have to infer the curve from the quotes, because it has the short-rate
model the quotes came from.

** Path A: keep the unit market**

Rejected. It fails constraint 3 and it is internally inconsistent with the
calibration, by the =7.8e-05= measured above. It is also the only path that
costs nothing today, which is why it was taken; the cost lands on the party
that calibrates.

** Path B: the run shares its own latent curve (a market board)**

The run caches, for each currency, the discount factors its own IR feed
implies, and the surface reads them. Nothing is published as market data: the
board is a cache of values the feeds already hold, on the scheduler's thread,
which is the shape the joint-market investigation already named as an option
("a market board the feeds publish to" versus "published values, a desk's
view").

It satisfies every requirement the documents set. The forward the surface is
struck against is, by construction, the forward the vendor's own rate quotes
imply, so constraint 1 and constraint 3 hold together and the surface is the
conditional object Kienitz describes. The vendor publishes no derived data, so
the ownership decision of 2026-11-09 stands: the system plays the vendor, and
curves remain a party's. And the party's calibration is not left adrift: the
clean quote set and its bootstrap give the true curve, which is what the
latent-surface note already claims for curves, so a party bootstrapping this
vendor's quotes recovers this curve to bootstrap tolerance -- the two forward
sets differ by the bootstrap, not by design.

** Path C: the system tenant publishes curves as derived market data**

Rejected. It contradicts the ownership decision, gives the vendor a maker-checker
lifecycle over its own internal state, and makes a party's curve and the
vendor's curve two published objects that can disagree. It also breaks the
vendor/contributor split the latent-surface note is built on: a vendor that
publishes the curve behind its quotes has published the truth the contributor
layer exists to hide.

** Path D: the surface reads a party's published forward set**

Rejected, and this is the path the current task text assumed. It inverts the
dependency: the vendor would quote against a derivative of its own quotes,
which means the vendor's prices depend on which tenant is provisioned, on that
tenant's bootstrap settings, and on whether that tenant has published at all.
It also does not compose: a run with no party, or with two, has no single
forward. It would work only in the special case where every party's curve is
identical to the vendor's, which is the case Path B states directly.

** Path E: the surface bootstraps the two currencies' published quotes itself**

Rejected as a variant of Path B with no benefit. It rebuilds, every tick, a
curve the run already holds exactly, and it introduces a second bootstrap
implementation to disagree with the calibration's.

* Conclusions

- The surface's forward is not a detail of the feed; it is the frame of the
  published surface, and the documents make it a hard constraint rather than a
  refinement. The current unit market is a known, measured defect.
- The curve the surface needs already exists inside the run. Every IR curve
  feed carries a short-rate model whose discount factors are analytic at any
  horizon, and every pillar it publishes is priced off that one state. The work
  is to *share* it, not to build it.
- Therefore a latent volatility surface does imply a latent yield curve, in the
  sense the frameworks use: the surface's parameters are relative to a forward,
  and the forward is the curve. It does not imply a *published* curve, and it
  does not imply the vendor becomes a source of derived market data.
- The awkward consequence is that the surface's market stops being a constant.
  Today =fx_vol_surface_emitter::make= takes one market per expiry for the life
  of the feed; under any path but A the market moves when the curve ticks, and
  the emitter has to take its markets per step. That is a change to the
  emitter's step, not to its laws, and it is the part of this work that the
  landed per-expiry markets make cheap.

* Recommendations

Choose Path B. The work, in order, each step ending in a test:

1. *The board, in the run.* =run_market= grows a per-currency block: a currency
   code and its dated discount factors, published by the IR feed when it ticks
   and read on the scheduler's thread only. The values are dated so a reader
   needs no tick arithmetic: the curve's own horizon mapping happens once, in
   the feed that owns the model. A test pins that the board's factor at a date
   equals =discount_factor= of the process that published it.
2. *The surface reads it.* Each tick, the surface builds one market per expiry
   from the pair's spot and the two currencies' boards, through
   =pair_market_at= -- the block merged in this branch, which the calibration
   already uses, so the two sides resolve a market by one rule. A pair whose
   currencies the run does not both carry is refused at start, naming the
   missing currency, rather than quoted against a flat forward.
3. *The emitter takes its markets per step.* The markets move from =make= to
   =step=. A test states that an unchanged curve and an unchanged SSVI state
   emit identical quotes, so the board adds no drift of its own; and a test
   states that a two-percent differential moves the 25-delta quote by the
   amount the strike solve implies.
4. *The round trip, stated honestly.* A test bootstraps a party's curve from the
   clean pillar quotes and shows the surface calibrated from it lands on the same
   strikes within the bootstrap's own tolerance. This replaces the acceptance
   line that asked the feed and the calibration to resolve an identical market,
   which holds only in the limit where the bootstrap is exact.
5. *The spot, later.* The quoted volatilities do not need the spot at all, so
   the board's spot is not on the critical path. It becomes necessary when the
   surface publishes strikes, and when the spot's own drift is made
   covered-interest-parity consistent, which is the parent of this question and
   is not in this story's scope.

Decisions for the owner before the work is raised:

- *The board's members.* Curves only, or every derived quantity the run could
  share -- equity forwards, commodity strips, the CAPFLOOR shift constraint 5
  asks for? Curves only is the smallest thing that answers this question.
- *What the board holds per currency.* The curve's own pillar dates, a fixed
  grid, or both. Pillar dates are what a party's forward set holds and what
  =pair_market_at= consumes, so they are the natural shape; a fixed grid to
  thirty years keeps a surface whose expiries run past the last pillar from
  being refused.
- *When the surface re-solves its strikes.* Every tick, or only on its own vol
  tick. Per tick is what a desk does and what the approach note's step 4 says;
  it costs one strike solve per expiry per contributor per tick.
- *Whether the IR feeds round-trip exactly.* A party's bootstrap uses ORE's
  engine, not ours, so "the true curve" is recovered to the bootstrap's
  tolerance and not to the last digit. If that is not good enough for the
  round-trip test, the alternative is to state the tolerance the test may
  assume, as the surface tests already do.
