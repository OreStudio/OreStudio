#+title: The market's volatility channel
#+description: What the market-wide factor should do to a price law: business time against sampling, a per-feed volatility loading with its normalisation, the tail channel through the mixture, and the bivariate Hawkes that would make activity and volatility excite each other.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :marketdata:synthetic:volatility:design:factor:
#+created: 2026-10-26
#+updated: 2026-10-26

* Summary

The market-wide factor moves the tick clock today and leaves the price law alone:
a panic arrives as more observations of the same random walk. A market that is
busy is also *more volatile*, and the same shared factor can carry that, because
the arrival work already publishes it and every price process already holds the
knobs a loading would scale. What is missing is three small seams — a setter on
the price process, a loading on the link and the config row, and one line in each
feed — plus the calibration that keeps a run's sigma what its configuration
states. This note states those seams, the tail channel the mixture adds, and the
bivariate Hawkes that would make activity and volatility excite each other; it
also settles whether a rate change moves time at all.

* Detail

** What the factor is today

One state machine and one shared heat, read once per tick:

  A(t) = S(t) + C(t) / R(t)

where =S= is the calm/active multiplier (calm derived so its long-run mean is
one), =C= is the heat any event adds to and decay fades, and =R= is the summed
mean rate of the series enrolled with the heat, so a series takes the share of
=C= its own rate is of the total. A feed reads it through
=market_link::rate_multiplier()=, which returns =sensitivity * A(t)=, and its
arrival process is re-rated by that number. The rates move; the prices do not.

The heat's long-run mean is not zero — it settles at the event rate times
jump/decay — so with the heat on, the average rate is above the configured one.
That is a known property of the rate channel, and it is the first thing the
volatility channel has to decide about rather than inherit.

** Sampling against time

A diffusion's variance follows *elapsed time*, not the number of observations.
Double the tick rate and a correctly written price law gives twice as many
samples of the same path per second, not twice the variance.

The processes in =ores.analytics.quant= are of two kinds and they behave
differently under a rate change:

- A process with a time-scaled law takes =next(elapsed_ticks)=, and the feed
  passes =elapsed_seconds * ticks_per_second=. A higher rate shortens each gap,
  so each step covers less time, and the variance per wall-clock second is
  unchanged. This is the correct behaviour, and it is what the arrival
  time-rescaling test asserts.
- A process that answers =supports_variable_step() == false= takes one nominal
  step per tick. The same nominal step now fits into a shorter gap, so **variance
  per wall-clock second rises with the rate** — as the square root of it, in
  sigma terms. A curve process that steps one calendar day per tick also runs its
  calendar faster; a rate change quietly moves time.

The second behaviour is a defect rather than a feature: an operator who doubles a
feed's rate to get more ticks also doubles its realized volatility without asking.
It must be fixed before a volatility loading is added, or the loading will be
measured on top of an unintended one.

The clean formulation is business time. Let

  Theta(t) = integral of A(s) ds

be the integrated activity, and let the price process run on business time with a
volatility per unit of it. Then "more activity" and "more variance per second"
are two dials instead of one, and a series that is merely observed more often is
visibly unchanged.

** The volatility loading

Three seams, each mirroring something the arrival work already has:

1. =i_stochastic_process= gains =set_volatility_multiplier(double)=, the sibling
   of =i_arrival_process::set_baseline_multiplier=. The default refuses; the
   processes that can honour it scale their own sigma — a Gaussian mixture scales
   its stdevs, a short-rate model its sigma — and keep their means.
2. =market_link= gains =volatility_sensitivity= beside =sensitivity=, and both
   generation-config kinds gain the column. A feed that follows the market in
   rate and not in volatility sets it to zero; the default follows the rate
   loading, because a market that only ticks faster is the artefact this note
   exists to remove.
3. Each feed applies it on its tick, beside the arrival multiplier it already
   reads.

The law, with gamma = 1 by default:

  sigma_effective = sigma * sensitivity_vol * A(t)

so variance is proportional to activity, which is the mixture-of-distributions
reading: the same diffusion, with more time in it. gamma is a knob, not a
conviction; gamma = 1/2 gives sigma proportional to activity, which is what a
price process whose step count, not whose clock, drives the variance does.

*Calibration is the work, not the plumbing.* A run's sigma must stay the sigma its
configuration states unless a person pins a state. The state's long-run mean is
one by construction, so it needs nothing; the heat's is not, so the volatility
channel must centre it — subtract the heat's stationary mean from the margin — or
a run's average volatility drifts up with the heat, and every calibration test
built on generated prices fails for a reason nobody can see.

*Two meanings of volatility* must be kept apart in every test:

- the sigma of the process, which is what the loading moves;
- the sigma a client computes from the ticks published, which also moves with
  sampling frequency and microstructure noise, and which rises even when the
  process is unchanged.

** The tails

A diffusion whose sigma doubles gives wider returns but the same shape. A panic
does not look like that: prices gap, and the kurtosis rises faster than the
variance. An FX feed's law is already a Gaussian mixture, so the tail channel is
a reweighting rather than a new model: as =A= rises, weight moves from the narrow
components to the wide ones, renormalised so the mixture stays a distribution and
the mean is unchanged.

That is the cheapest way to make a pinned panic produce the gaps a person expects,
and it composes with the loading: the loading sets the scale, the reweighting sets
the shape.

** The control plane

No new verb and no new service. The pieces that exist carry the new number:

- *The switch.* =synthetic.v1.ops.pin_market_regime= stays as it is. Auto, Calm
  and Active move the rate and the volatility together, because a person pressing
  Active expects the prices to move, not only the clock.
- *The read.* =get_market_regime_response= gains the volatility multiplier beside
  the rate multiplier, so the shell's =synthetic regime get= and the BFF's route
  report both without a new subject.
- *The screen.* The Market regime tab shows both multipliers and the same raster;
  a feed's Arrivals tab gains one field, =volatility sensitivity=, beside
  =sensitivity=, edited and audited exactly as that one is.
- *Permissions.* Unchanged: the same two subjects, the same session rules, the
  same permissions.

The tests each part must pass:

| Part | Test |
|------+------|
| Sampling against time | A rate multiplier of two leaves the variance per wall-clock second unchanged for a process that takes the gap, and a fixed-step process is refused or converted before it can be re-rated |
| The loading, calibration | Over a long run with the heat on, the realized sigma equals the configured sigma |
| The loading, response | A pinned active market raises the realized sigma by the feed's loading, and calm lowers it |
| The tails | The kurtosis of returns rises with activity while the mean and the long-run sigma hold |
| The control plane | The switch, the read and the tab carry both multipliers without a new subject or permission |

** Out of scope: activity and volatility exciting each other

The complete object is a *bivariate* Hawkes: activity excites volatility and
volatility excites activity, and today's heat is one coordinate of it. The
rank-one kernel of the arrival note generalises:

  K = diag(n) + m * 1'

becomes a 2x2 block of the same shape, and stability stops being the scalar test
=max n + sum m < 1= and becomes a spectral radius: the largest eigenvalue of the
mean kernel must be below one. That is a model of its own, it depends on all of
the above, and it is captured in the inbox rather than scoped here.

* Literature

The approach is not novel and should not be argued as if it were: each part of it
is a named idea with a paper behind it. The list is grouped by the section that
leans on it, and each entry says what it gives us rather than who said it first.

** Activity and volatility are one latent process

- [[https://jstor.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1973/01/01/subordinated-stochastic-process-model-finite-variance][Clark (1973), *A subordinated stochastic process model with finite variance for
  speculative prices*, Econometrica 41(1)]] — the mixture-of-distributions
  hypothesis: returns are a mixture over an unobservable information-arrival
  rate, and tick counts and |returns| are two proxies for it. This is the paper
  the loading's "variance proportional to activity" comes from.
- Easley and O'Hara (1992), *Time and the process of security price adjustment*,
  Journal of Finance 47(2) — trading time carries information, so a price law
  indexed by clock time misses the variable that matters.
- Engle (2000), *The econometrics of ultra-high-frequency data*, Econometrica
  68(1) — the joint model of durations and returns: activity and volatility
  estimated together, which is the statistical shape of what this note proposes.
- Engle and Russell (1998), *Autoregressive conditional duration*, Econometrica
  66(5) — the durations model the arrival processes echo, and the reason a
  clustering rate belongs in a price model at all.
- Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014), *The price impact of order book events*,
  Journal of Financial Econometrics 12(1) — the measured link between event
  intensity and short-horizon volatility.

** Business time, or why more ticks is not more variance

- Bochner (1955), *Harmonic Analysis and the Theory of Probability* — the
  subordination of one process by another, the mathematics of a clock that runs on
  activity.
- Mandelbrot and Taylor (1967), *On the distribution of stock price differences*,
  Operations Research 15(6) — operation time, the first trading-time formulation.
- [[https://acnpsearch.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/75764135][Ané and Geman (2000),
  *Order flow, transaction clock, and normality of asset returns*, Journal of
  Finance 55(5)]] — returns are near-normal in transaction time and fat-tailed in
  calendar time. The strongest statement of the case for keeping sampling and
  time apart, which is the first task of the story.

** Hawkes, endogenous activity, and its stability

- Hawkes (1971), *Spectra of some self-exciting and mutually exciting point
  processes*, Biometrika 58(1) — the model, and already the *mutually exciting*
  form: activity exciting volatility is not an extension of it but a use of it.
- Ogata (1981), *On Lewis' simulation method for point processes*, IEEE
  Transactions on Information Theory 27(1) — thinning, the simulation method the
  arrival processes use.
- Bacry, Delattre, Hoffmann and Muzy (2013), *Modelling microstructure noise with
  mutually exciting point processes*, Quantitative Finance 13(1) — the closest
  published model to what ORE Studio does today, noise included.
- [[https://scholar.google.com.ec/citations?view_op=view_citation&hl=ja&user=JII6yu8AAAAJ&citation_for_view=JII6yu8AAAAJ:MXK_kJrjxJIC][Bacry,
  Mastromarino and Muzy (2015), *Hawkes processes in finance*, Market
  Microstructure and Liquidity 1(1)]] — the survey to read before touching the
  kernel; it states the stability conditions in the multivariate case.
- Brémaud and Massoulié (1996), *Stability of nonlinear Hawkes processes*, Annals
  of Probability 24(3) — why the scalar rank-one test becomes a spectral radius,
  which is the condition the bivariate design has to state.
- [[https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X15000264][Aït-Sahalia,
  Cacho-Diaz and Laeven (2015), *Modeling financial contagion using mutually
  exciting jump processes*, Journal of Financial Economics 117(3)]] — contagion as
  two mutually exciting jump processes: the bivariate design, with jumps, and the
  empirical case for it.

** The tails

- Merton (1976), *Option pricing when underlying stock returns are
  discontinuous*, Journal of Financial Economics 3(1) — the jump-diffusion whose
  tail a diffusion scale cannot reproduce.
- Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (2006), *Econometrics of testing for jumps in
  financial economics using bipower variation*, Journal of Financial Econometrics
  4(1) — how a jump is told from a diffusion in observed data, which is how a test
  of our own tails should be written.

** Sampling, noise, and what "volatility" means in a test

- Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Labys (2003), *Modeling and forecasting
  realized volatility*, Econometrica 71(2) — realized variance as an estimator,
  and its sampling dependence.
- Zhang, Mykland and Aït-Sahalia (2005), *A tale of two time scales*, Journal of
  the American Statistical Association 100(472) — microstructure noise biases
  realized variance upward, more so the more often you sample. This is the reason
  the note insists every test says which volatility it measures.
