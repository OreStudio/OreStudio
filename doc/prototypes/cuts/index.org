* Summary

A cut fixes a body of data at one timepoint so that a valuation can be reproduced.
Three journeys describe the person's side of that — taking a cut, pricing a report
from one, and rerunning and auditing one — and no cut exists in the application
today: no component, no table, no read and no screen.

One prototype answers to the group. It draws every surface the three journeys name,
and it settles what the design note cannot: what a body that could not be assembled
looks like, what each state of a cut reads as on a screen, how a report comes to
price from one cut, what the resolution says before a run, and what a run states
about the body it priced from. There is no pricing surface, because there is none:
a cut is selected where a report is defined.

The conventions the take reads — the named cut times — have no prototype of their
own. They are two surfaces of the same page, because a person taking a cut reads a
cut time and a person maintaining one changes it, and the two are never on screen
at the same moment.

[[file:../index.org][Prototypes]] is the top page and the only inventory.

* One decision, and the surfaces it forces

The journeys are not a screen plan. Read one to one they would give a list, a
record, a take flow, a picker, a run card and a reference list, which is what this
page draws, but the grouping is what the decision note argues: a cut is a record
with a derived state, an authorisation that is the inbox's, and a second binding
that a run names.

The prototype therefore turns on two questions — *what does a person need to see to
trust a body they did not assemble?* and *how does a person know what a report will
price from before they run it?* — and every surface answers one of them: the counts
of the parts, the gaps where a part stands open, the accounts of the acts, the runs
that already rest on the body, the verdict on a reproduction, the badge on the
report, and the preview that states what the selection resolves to now.

* What it settles

The prototype settles four things the decision note left to the screens.

*The gaps are drawn where the body is.* A body that cannot be assembled is kept,
not thrown away, and its gaps are drawn as a table of part, item, why it stands and
what closes it. The take offers a draft in place of a take, and the record reads
the same table. The alternative — a take that fails with one message — was not
drawn, because it loses the list a person acts on.

*The state is drawn as two facts, not one.* The badge carries the derived state,
and a line under it carries the fact that qualifies it: which part a taken cut
waits on, and when a superseded cut was replaced. A screen that drew only the
badge would make a cut awaiting a signature look like one that holds none.

*The refusals are one sentence in two places.* The picker refuses a cut where it
is picked, and the run refuses the same body in the same words before the engine is
reached. The sentence names what the kind requires, so a body of a kind that
requires no authorisation is never refused on that ground. Neither offers a way past
a refusal except the remedy the state names: an authorisation, or a new take, or a
change to the configuration.

*A reproduction is a sentence before it is a number.* The comparison states what
was reproduced and what the two runs shared — the body and the engine — before it
shows a figure. That ordering is what makes the third verdict usable: an auditor
writes the reason down, and the figures are what follow from it.

* What none of them settle

Whether the gap table becomes a shared part of the standard, or stays the take's
own. Whether the comparison reads a run's archive into figures, or the run records
its figures as rows when it completes. Whether the cut's scope is one book group,
as drawn, or a composition of the cuts of its books. Whether one inbox kind serves
every occasion that needs a signature, as drawn, or each occasion registers its own.
And whether the selection earns its own step on the create flow, or stays a field on
the step that names the report.
