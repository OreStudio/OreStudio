This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A trader or quant inspects a candidate surface's smiles across tenors and its mesh, and checks the calendar and butterfly arbitrage between them.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I want to see the smiles this surface produces at each tenor, the mesh they
make together, and whether the surface has an arbitrage in it." The journey is
done when the person has read the smile at each tenor of interest, seen the
mesh, and read the calendar and butterfly arbitrage checks. The person does not
publish here.

* Actor and trigger

The actor is the trader or the quant. The trader owns the candidate surface;
the quant owns the model and reads the same screen. Neither decides to publish
here: an approver does that in a different journey. The trigger is a calibration
just run, a smile that looks odd at one tenor, a mesh that folds, or a
pricing question about an expiry and a strike. The journey starts at a
candidate surface and ends when the person leaves it, still a candidate.

This journey reads and diagnoses; it changes nothing. It is the surface-shaped
check the maker runs before the candidate goes to a second pair of eyes.

* Steps

1. *Open the candidate surface.* The person opens *Curves and surfaces* and
   selects the candidate surface. The system reads the blueprint that produced
   it, which names the pair, the model, the output grid and the series the
   points were written to.
2. *Read the surface.* The person chooses *Read surface*. A candidate is read
   from the reply of the calibration that produced it, which carries the grid
   the fit sampled; a published set is read from
   =marketdata.v1.ops.get_published_fx_vol_surface=. Both give the grid, its
   expiries and its strikes, so the surface on the screen is the surface the
   server computed.
3. *Read the smiles across tenors.* The person picks a set of tenors and reads
   the smile at each. Each smile is a slice: the vol at a fixed expiry across
   strikes, from the low-strike put wing through the at-the-money point to the
   high-strike call wing. A smile is one row of the grid, so the screen draws it
   without a second call.
4. *Overlay the quotes on a smile.* The person reads each raw quote of that
   tenor against the fitted smile. The calibration reply carries one fit per
   pillar, with the quoted vol, the fitted vol and their difference, so the
   overlay is read and not recomputed.
5. *Read the mesh.* The person reads the mesh: the vol across expiry and
   strike at once, either as a grid or as a shaded total-variance view. The mesh
   is the grid the reply carries, viewed whole rather than a row at a time.
6. *Check calendar arbitrage.* The person checks that total variance does not
   fall as expiry grows, at a fixed strike. The calibration runs the check and
   the reply reports each breach it found, with its expiry and its description.
7. *Check butterfly arbitrage.* The person checks that the risk-neutral
   density implied by each smile is non-negative, which fails when a smile is
   too concave. The calibration runs the check, reports each breach with its
   expiry, and any breach stops the candidate being published under any rule
   ([[id:186B44DD-51EA-45AA-971E-0C2D4D21A6DD][Publication of derived market data sets]]).
8. *Leave.* The person returns to the list. The candidate surface is unchanged;
   the person did not write or publish anything.

Every read here would be server-side and synchronous, so the journey carries no
state of its own.

* Screens and wireframes

One screen. The surface and the tenor picker sit at the top, the smiles and the
mesh sit in the middle, and the two arbitrage checks sit at the foot, so the
person reads the slices, then the whole surface, then its faults.

The calibration record is the surface's fit as computed: the smiles are its grid
by row, the mesh is the grid whole, and the two arbitrage checks are the
violations it recorded. The screen composes those rows rather than a surface
model of its own.

The checks are computed once, by the calibration, and both the reply and the
record carry them. A surface that breaches one cannot be published under any
rule, so the person reads the breaches on the candidate and does not need a
second opinion at the published set.

* What is missing

1. *A stored calibration has no read.* The record holds the grid, the pillar
   fits and the breaches, and nothing serves it by its id. A candidate can be
   inspected only in the reply that produced it, so the person cannot return to
   yesterday's calibration, and the person who decides cannot open the fit the
   maker saw. The change history states that the row changed and when; it does
   not give back the grid.
2. *The slice subject is served and no screen calls it.* =marketdata.v1.ops.get_series_slice=
   is served by =series_slice_handler=, which reads one component's term
   structure through =series_slice_reader=, and both the gateway and the BFF
   route =/api/curves/slice= to it. No web screen calls the route: the steps
   above read the grid whole, and the smile is one row of that grid. The first
   caller would be a view that wants one expiry on its own.
3. *The grid is as fine as the blueprint says.* The mesh is the output grid, so a
   strike between two columns has no value until something interpolates. The
   blueprint names the interpolation across expiries and not across strikes, so
   a view finer than the grid would have to state which rule it used.
4. *The arbitrage checks are the calibration's.* The reply reports breaches and
   a breach blocks a publish, but nothing returns total variance along an expiry
   axis or the implied density along a smile, so a person cannot see why a check
   failed beyond the description the breach carries.
5. *A published surface has no calibration read.* The publish row names the
   calibration it published and nothing serves that record, so the read of a
   published set cannot show the fit behind it.
