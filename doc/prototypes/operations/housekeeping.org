* Summary

Housekeeping is the one administration screen whose subject is absence: what
nothing deletes. The installation keeps nine stores; a default policy bounds
seven, the content digest bounds the package cache, and the screen's job is to
make the bound — or its absence — impossible to miss. Measured on a provisioned
tenant, the largest store with nothing bounding it was the compute host's own
scratch — 565 job folders and 1.9 GB — followed by the delivery counts at about
365 MB a day, while the bounded report outputs were doing exactly what their
policy said. The prototype argues three shapes for showing that in one place,
and the states a store is in when the news is bad, including the one that reads
as bounded and is not: a policy whose action the sweep cannot perform.

Everything here is mock data. The page calls no service, needs no build and no
session, and every variant opens from one file with a query parameter.

* Detail

** The question

The screen must answer, in the order a person asks it:

1. what is this installation keeping, and how large is each store;
2. which of those stores has nothing stopping it;
3. when a store is bounded, what bounds it and when that last took effect;
4. what housekeeping has actually done, and what it said when it did nothing.

The fourth question is why the runs log is part of this screen and not a
separate page: the same person asks both, in one sitting, and the answer to
"why is this still here" is a run's detail line.

** The variants

- *A — one table of every store.* Store, whether anything bounds it, what
  bounds it, size, growth, and a filter to the stores nothing bounds; the store
  a person opens gets a page of its own. Carries forward: it
  is the only shape that holds all twelve rows and their numbers at once, and
  it is the shape the logs screen already uses, so the person reads it twice a
  week without learning it twice.
- *B — cards grouped by where the store lives.* Database tables, chunked
  tables, the object store, the compute host's own disk. It is better at
  "what is big" and worse at "what is unbounded", because a card that reads
  "unbounded" among eight cards is one more card; the group heading is what
  makes the absence legible, and none of the group headings says bounded.
- *C — two panes, list beside the policy editor.* The maintenance shape: the
  store and its size stay in view while the period is changed, and the save bar
  states the change and the record it will write. This variant is the one the
  editing journey needs, and it is drawn here rather than in a prototype of its
  own because a policy is edited from the store it bounds.

** A store is not its target string

The first review of this prototype said the obvious thing: nobody outside the
database should have to read =ores_refdata_calendar_dates_tbl= to know that the
store holds the calendars' business days. A row needs a human name and a
sentence saying what the store is, and the physical target belongs beside them
as a fact rather than as the title.

Nothing in the tree carries that name today. A retention policy holds a =code=
(the policy's name, =report_cashflows_90d=), a =target= (the physical table or
prefix) and a =description= that describes the policy and not the store. Three
ways to give the screen its names were weighed:

- *Derive the name from the target.* =ores_refdata_calendar_dates_tbl= becomes
  "Refdata Calendar Dates" by stripping the product and table affixes. It is
  free and it is wrong twice: no description comes with it, and the targets
  that are prefixes or directories (=compute/input/=,
  =publish/run/wrappers=) have nothing to parse.
- *Use the policy's name.* =report_cashflows_90d= names the policy, and a store
  may have no policy at all, which is exactly the row that most needs a name.
- *A store catalogue.* One row per store the installation keeps: its code, its
  display name, its description, the shape its target has, the target itself,
  how to measure it, and the component that owns it. A policy then names a
  store, and "unbounded" becomes a store with no policy rather than a target
  string that appears in none.

The third is the layering the reference-data pages already state: the physical
name is the row's identity and the presentation name is metadata the platform
publishes. It also turns the story's inventory of every dimension that grows
from a document into data the screen can read, and it gives the size read
somewhere to hang: a store that says how to measure itself is one the size read
can answer for.

This prototype draws the first two ways badly on purpose and the third properly:
the fixtures carry a =label= and a =description=, the target sits beneath the
label in monospace, and the detail panel states both. When the catalogue exists,
the label and the description come from it; until then they are the screen's own
copy, which is a reason to build the catalogue rather than to write twelve names
into a client.

** The store is an entity, and the screen shows its record

The screen reads a store as a row of a catalogue, not as a target string with
some numbers beside it. The prototype's detail panel states the record the way
the entity would: *code*, *name*, *description*, *owner* (the component that
writes the store), *target kind*, *target*, *time column* (what a row delete
compares) and *measured by* (what the probe reads: relation rows, objects and
bytes, files and bytes). The policy is a separate block beneath, because a
policy is a decision about a store and not a property of one.

A store that is described this way answers the three review comments at once:
the name and the description are published rather than parsed out of a table
name, the size and its plot hang off the same row, and the row is what a person
clicks to open the details.

** The measurement belongs to the store, by a key or by a trigger

The samples are one row per store per cadence: the store it measures, when, and
what it found. Whether the reference is a real foreign key or a trigger-checked
one is an implementation choice the prototype does not draw; what it does draw
is that a sample cannot exist without its store, so the panel can state a
series as the store's own and the sampler can walk the catalogue to fill it.
The prototype shows the series as twelve points and says where they come from;
the numbers are fixtures.

** Latest activity around the store**

A store's panel ends with the activity that names it, newest first, from every
source that records one: the sampler's measurements, the sweep's runs and their
outcomes, a person's change to the policy, and the moments a store bounded
something — a node releasing a job's folder, for instance. One log, because
"what has happened to this store" has one answer, and reading it here saves the
trip to the runs screen for the common case. The full log is one click away and
stays the place a window is asked for.

** Growth is a series, not an adjective

The second review asked for the thing the screen is actually for: each store's
growth over time, so that it can be plotted. "Growing · +18 MB over 30 days" is
a sentence; a person cannot see a step, a cliff or a sawtooth in it, and those
are the shapes that say what happened. The prototype draws a sparkline in every
row and, on a store's page, a plot with size up the side and time along the
foot, ticked and gridded, and both are mock.

What a real plot needs is a sample per store per cadence, kept as a series:

- *The row.* One measurement of one store at one instant: the store it belongs
  to, when it was taken, the size in bytes, and whatever else the store is
  measured by (rows, chunks, objects, folders). The store's catalogue entry
  says how it is measured, which is why the catalogue is the thing to build
  first.
- *The store.* A Timescale hypertable chunked on the measurement instant, the
  same shape the grid, node, session and stream samples already use, so the
  screen reads a series the platform already knows how to keep and to bound.
  Its own retention policy is then one more row, and the sweep that drops
  chunks can drop this store's chunks too.
- *The sampler.* A scheduled job that walks the catalogue and writes one sample
  per store, on a cadence a person can state; hourly is enough to see a day and
  cheap enough to keep for a quarter. It is separate from the sweep: the sweep
  acts on a period, and the sampler only looks.
- *The read.* A series read by store and window, which is also the size read
  the screen needs today: the latest point is the "size now" the table shows,
  and the points before it are the chart.

The plot then answers what no single number can: a store that steps once and
flattens is a run that happened; one that climbs steadily is a decision nobody
made; one that sawtooths is a policy that drops and the store that regrows into
it. This is the analysis the review asked for, and it is the one thing on the
screen that a person will come back to look at rather than read once.

** Clicking a row opens the store's own page

The screen follows the shape the rest of the application uses: a list page, and
a page per record. Clicking a row opens the store's page — its name and
description, its record, its plot, its policy and its log — with a breadcrumb and
a back action at its head. The list keeps the comparison the person came for;
the page gives the store the room its record, plot and log need, and a place of
its own to link to.

The editor opens on the store's page rather than in a variant of its own, with
the store and its size still in view while the period is changed. A store with
no policy says so on its page and offers the way to give it one: the create form
states an action, a period, a cadence, whether it is enabled, and the reason,
and adding it turns the store to *waiting for its first sweep* on the page and in
the list.

** What the list shows, and why the plot left it

The list is for triage, not for reading: nine rows and one question, "which of
these should I look at". A twelve-point plot in every row is a shape a person
tries to read nine times, and none of the nine gets read. The plot stays on the
store's page, where there is one store and room to look at it.

A row now states four things, in the order the eye asks them:

- *whether anything bounds it*, in one word and one colour — Bounded, Unbounded,
  or Bounded by keying, in green, red and green. This is the column the list
  exists for, and it is the first question because it is the only one with a
  wrong answer;
- *the size*, in bytes with its unit, because that is the number the question
  starts from;
- *how close it is to a stated limit*, as a bar and a percentage — but only
  where a limit exists. A store with no budget says "no size budget" rather than
  drawing an empty bar, which would read as room to spare;
- *which way it is going*, as an arrow and the rate per week: ↑ +2.5 MB/wk,
  → flat, or "not measured". The rate is the honest summary of the plot without
  the plot: a store that stepped once and now sits flat reads →, and one that
  climbs reads ↑ with how fast.

The size budget is a decision this prototype takes and the product does not have:
nothing states a cap on a store today, and a cap with no consequence is the same
decoration the ticks' retention policy was. If the budget is added, something has
to happen at it — an alert to the administrator, a sweep that acts early, or a
refusal to keep accepting rows — and that mechanism belongs with the field, not
after it. The prototype draws the indicator so the shape can be judged; the field
and its consequence are for the task that adds them.

** An unbounded store is the screen's verdict

The sweep visits policies. A store no policy names is not visited at all: nothing
deletes from it, nothing reports on it, and it appears in the run log only as an
absence. So a store with no policy is not "unconfigured"; it is kept for ever,
and it grows at whatever rate its writers write, until the database or the disk
is full. The three journeys call that the failure the screen exists to prevent,
and the list has to say it before it says anything else.

Two things follow, and the prototype does both.

The test is *bounded*, not *has a policy*. A policy that is switched off, that the
sweep records as skipped, or whose last run failed leaves the store exactly as
unbounded as no policy at all. The column therefore reads Bounded, Unbounded or
Bounded by keying, and the reason — no policy, off, skipped, failed — sits beside
it in the Bounded by cell, where the diagnosis belongs. This is not pedantry: a
default that asks for a row delete is declared, enabled and recorded skipped on
every sweep, and its store is unbounded.

The verdict is red. An unbounded store has no reading that is not bad: it is the
one state on this screen with a wrong answer, and amber would file it beside a
store that is merely close to a budget.

The cost is stated in bytes. A row says not only that nothing bounds it but what
that means: *nothing removes it · about 7.4 GB a year*, from the same twelve-week
series the plot draws. For a store whose size cannot be read the row says the
worse thing honestly: *size not measured, so growth is invisible*. The plot and
the alarm are one measurement seen two ways, which is why the samples have to be
built before either works.

The top of the page states the count — *1 of the 9 stores is unbounded* — with
the way to filter the list to it. The list is then a work list, and its empty
state is the goal: *every store has a bound*.

** Bounded by default, and the one store a default cannot cover

An alarm that fires on most of the list is wallpaper, so the count has to reach
zero and stay there or nobody reads it. The reviewer decided the shape: the store
catalogue declares a default bound for every store that can take one, and
provisioning creates the policy, so an installation never ships unbounded. The
cost is the opposite risk — data deleted on a default nobody read — which is why
the default is a quarter for run output and a day for scratch, and why a policy
states the reason it is kept.

The fixture now shows that world. Seven of the nine stores carry a default
policy, the package cache is bounded by its content digest, and one store is
unbounded: the calendar dates. That one is the interesting case, because no
default can cover it: a policy bounds by *age*, and a business day behind the
horizon is not old, it is out of range. The days are re-derivable from the
calendar's rules, so the bound they need is a delete by range — every day before
the horizon start — which the policy model cannot express. The screen says so
rather than showing a policy that would delete the days pricing still needs.

The empty state changes meaning with this decision. It is no longer a fresh
installation: it is an installation whose defaults somebody removed, and it reads
as the alarm it is. The page therefore names the fixture it is showing whenever a
deep link selects one, and a link to a state opens the list rather than the page
of a store that the state no longer chooses.

** Telling a person without them opening the screen

The screen is pull. Housekeeping needs push as well, or nobody learns that a
store is unbounded until it fills a disk. The platform already holds the
mechanism: =ores.inbox= raises notifications of a stated kind, to an audience by
permission, with a link to the thing and a message key the screen renders in the
reader's language ([[id:7FAF3C0D-1131-4C32-9C29-3D1EF6D1005D][Notification
requirements]]). Housekeeping contributes kinds and raises them; it does not
build a channel of its own.

| Case | Who is told | What the message says |
|------+-------------+-----------------------|
| A store is unbounded when the installation is provisioned | The system administrator, or the tenant's, by the store's scope | Which store, that it has no bound, and a link to its page |
| A sweep records a policy skipped | The owner of the store | The action asked for, the action the sweep performs, and the store it leaves unbounded |
| A sweep fails | The owner of the store | The store, the action and the server's message |
| A store passes its size budget | The owner of the store | The size, the budget and the rate |

Two rules keep the messages worth reading. A notification names the store and
links to it; it never carries the numbers to act on, because a person who has to
act needs the plot and the policy. And a sweep that skips four policies raises
one message, not four: the inbox groups by kind and subject.

The audience follows the store, not the person raising it. A store the platform
owns is the system administrator's problem; a store a tenant's writes are the
tenant's. That is the same scope the catalogue states for the store, which is why
the catalogue has to carry it.

** The states a store can be in

| State | What the screen says | Why it matters |
|-------+----------------------+----------------|
| fresh | Seven defaults hold, one store is unbounded, and the last sweep freed 0 B because nothing is older than its period | A zero is not a failure, and the screen must not imply one |
| skipped | The defaults are declared and four cannot be applied, because the sweep performs a chunk drop and nothing else | The dangerous state: it reads as bounded on paper |
| failed | Unbounded — the last sweep failed; the detail is the server's message | The store is unbounded until somebody reads the message |
| disabled | Unbounded — the policy is off, so the store is kept for ever | A decision, and it should read as one |
| empty (=Defaults removed=) | No default holds: somebody removed them, and every store but the package cache is unbounded | The alarm the state exists for |

** What is fake

Everything. The sizes, the growth rates, the seven policies, the runs and the
person signed in are mock data taken from one environment at one moment: 283 MB
of calendar dates, 186 MB of report cashflows after 39 runs, 29 MB of delivery
counts growing at about 27,000 rows an hour, 1.9 GB of host scratch. No request
leaves the page.

** The verdict

Variant A is accepted: one table of every store, and a page of its own for the
store a person opens. It carries the screen because it holds every
store and every number at once and because it is the shape the logs screen
already uses, so the person reads it twice a week without learning it twice.

Variants B and C stay in the page as the alternatives A was weighed against, not
as shapes to build. Their arguments are recorded above, and C's editor is no
longer a variant of its own: a policy is changed in A's panel, where the store
and its size are already in view.

The screen still cannot be built until three things exist: the store catalogue
that carries a human name and a description, the samples and the size read that
the plot stands on, and the retention-run wire type PR #5126 adds for the log.
The prototype is the argument for building all three.
