* Summary

Currency option prices depart from the Black-Scholes formula in two ways: implied
volatility varies with the strike, the smile, and with the maturity. Backus,
Foresi, Li and Wu explain both by approximating the distribution of the log
exchange rate with a Gram-Charlier expansion, a normal density corrected by its
skewness and its excess kurtosis. In that setting the implied volatility smile
is approximately a quadratic function of moneyness: skewness tilts it and
kurtosis bends it. The relation runs both ways, so the paper reads skewness and
kurtosis off observed smiles. For ORE Studio the relation is used the other way
round: given the skewness and kurtosis of a pair's historical returns, it gives
a plausible smile, which is how the synthetic generator obtains a starting
surface where no implied volatility is published.

* Source

David Backus, Silverio Foresi, Kai Li and Liuren Wu, "Accounting for Biases in
Black-Scholes", working paper, first draft August 1997, this version November
1997. [[https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/fin/papers/0207/0207008.pdf][PDF]].

* Problem being solved

The Black-Scholes model assumes log-normal prices, so a single volatility should
price every strike and maturity. Currency options show otherwise: implied
volatility rises away from the money, and the implied volatility of
at-the-money options rises, on average, with maturity. The paper asks how much
of each bias departures from normality can account for, and how to read those
departures from option prices.

* The method

** The density

The conditional distribution of the log spot rate over $n$ periods is written as
a Gram-Charlier expansion: a normal density times a polynomial in the
standardised variable, whose coefficients are the skewness $\gamma_{1n}$ and the
excess kurtosis $\gamma_{2n}$. Moneyness is measured by

\begin{equation}
d = \frac{\ln(S_t / K) + (r_{nt} - r^*_{nt})\,n + \sigma_n^2/2}{\sigma_n}
\end{equation}

(their equation 9), the $d_1$ of the Black-Scholes formula with $\sigma_n$ the
standard deviation over the option's life; $d = 0$ is a call at the money and
$d > 0$ a call in the money.

** The smile

Proposition 1 gives the call price as the Black-Scholes price plus terms in the
skewness and the kurtosis. Proposition 2 inverts it to an implied volatility
(their equation 16):

\begin{equation}
v_n(d) \approx \sigma_n\left(1 - \frac{\gamma_{1n}}{3!}\,d - \frac{\gamma_{2n}}{4!}\,(1 - d^2)\right)
\end{equation}

Skewness enters linearly in $d$, so it tilts the smile: negative skewness raises
the volatility of out-of-the-money puts relative to calls. Kurtosis enters
through $1 - d^2$, so it lowers the at-the-money volatility below $\sigma_n$ and
raises the wings. The approximation drops terms in the square and higher powers
of $\sigma_n$; the paper's Figure 4 shows it close to the exact inversion of the
call price for $|d| < 1.5$ and above it beyond.

** Reading moments from a smile

A least-squares fit of a quadratic to an observed smile gives $\sigma_n$,
$\gamma_{1n}$ and $\gamma_{2n}$. The paper's example, a one-month Deutschemark
smile, gives a skewness of $-0.168$ and an excess kurtosis of $1.591$. It
reports that both kurtosis in currency prices and the Black-Scholes biases
decline with maturity.

* Key assumptions and limitations

1. *First order in the higher moments.* The smile is accurate for moderate
   skewness and kurtosis and for $|d| < 1.5$. Beyond, the true smile is lower
   than the approximation.
2. *The expansion is not always a density.* A Gram-Charlier expansion with too
   much skewness for its kurtosis is negative somewhere. Jondeau and Rockinger
   (2001) give the region of skewness and kurtosis in which it stays positive.
3. *The moments are risk-neutral.* The paper reads moments from option prices.
   Moments estimated from historical returns are physical-measure moments and
   carry no risk premium.

* Applicability to synthetic market data generation

Reading the smile at the market's delta pillars turns three moments into the
three FX quotes. Forward delta is $\Phi(d)$ for a call, so a 25-delta call sits
near $d = \Phi^{-1}(0.25) = -0.674$ and a 25-delta put near $d = +0.674$; the
10-delta points sit near $d = \mp 1.282$, inside the range where the
approximation holds. With $\sigma$ the volatility over the expiry:

| Quote | From equation 16 | At 25 delta | At 10 delta |
|-------+------------------+-------------+-------------|
| ATM ($d \approx 0$) | $\sigma(1 - \gamma_2/24)$ | | |
| Risk reversal | $\sigma\,\gamma_1\,\lvert d\rvert / 3$ | $0.225\,\sigma\gamma_1$ | $0.427\,\sigma\gamma_1$ |
| Smile butterfly | $\sigma\,\gamma_2\, d^2 / 24$ | $0.019\,\sigma\gamma_2$ | $0.068\,\sigma\gamma_2$ |

The skewness and kurtosis of an $n$-day return of independent daily returns are
$\gamma_1/\sqrt{n}$ and $\gamma_2/n$, so the risk reversal falls as
$1/\sqrt{t}$ and the butterfly as $1/t$. How the synthetic calibration uses
this, including the risk premium the third limitation leaves out, is in
[[id:3F9D6EAE-9FB8-4A0D-9BAF-1EA3EA93AB4F][Synthetic prices are calibrated from free public sources]].

* Open questions

1. *Kurtosis term structure.* Daily returns that cluster keep their kurtosis
   over longer horizons than $1/n$ implies, so the scaling above understates
   long-dated butterflies. A GARCH model of the daily variance implies its own
   term structure of kurtosis, which would be the better scaling.
