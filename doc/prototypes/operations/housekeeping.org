* Summary

Housekeeping is the one administration screen whose subject is absence: the
installation keeps a dozen stores, three of them bounded by a policy, and the
screen's job is to make the unbounded ones impossible to miss. Measured on a
provisioned tenant, the largest unbounded store was the compute host's own
scratch — 565 job folders and 1.9 GB — followed by the delivery counts at
about 365 MB a day, while the bounded report outputs were doing exactly what
their policy said. The prototype argues three shapes for showing that in one
place, and the states a store is in when the news is bad, including the one
that reads as bounded and is not: a policy whose action the sweep cannot
perform.

Everything here is mock data. The page calls no service, needs no build and no
session, and every variant opens from one file with a query parameter.

* Detail

** The question

The screen must answer, in the order a person asks it:

1. what is this installation keeping, and how large is each store;
2. which of those stores has nothing stopping it;
3. when a store is bounded, what bounds it and when that last took effect;
4. what housekeeping has actually done, and what it said when it did nothing.

The fourth question is why the runs log is part of this screen and not a
separate page: the same person asks both, in one sitting, and the answer to
"why is this still here" is a run's detail line.

** The variants

- *A — one table of every store.* Store, bounded-by, state, size, growth, last
  sweep, freed, with a detail panel for the selected row. Carries forward: it
  is the only shape that holds all twelve rows and their numbers at once, and
  it is the shape the logs screen already uses, so the person reads it twice a
  week without learning it twice.
- *B — cards grouped by where the store lives.* Database tables, chunked
  tables, the object store, the compute host's own disk. It is better at
  "what is big" and worse at "what is unbounded", because a card that reads
  "unbounded" among eight cards is one more card; the group heading is what
  makes the absence legible, and none of the group headings says bounded.
- *C — two panes, list beside the policy editor.* The maintenance shape: the
  store and its size stay in view while the period is changed, and the save bar
  states the change and the record it will write. This variant is the one the
  editing journey needs, and it is drawn here rather than in a prototype of its
  own because a policy is edited from the store it bounds.

** A store is not its target string

The first review of this prototype said the obvious thing: nobody outside the
database should have to read =ores_refdata_calendar_dates_tbl= to know that the
store holds the calendars' business days. A row needs a human name and a
sentence saying what the store is, and the physical target belongs beside them
as a fact rather than as the title.

Nothing in the tree carries that name today. A retention policy holds a =code=
(the policy's name, =report_cashflows_90d=), a =target= (the physical table or
prefix) and a =description= that describes the policy and not the store. Three
ways to give the screen its names were weighed:

- *Derive the name from the target.* =ores_refdata_calendar_dates_tbl= becomes
  "Refdata Calendar Dates" by stripping the product and table affixes. It is
  free and it is wrong twice: no description comes with it, and the targets
  that are prefixes or directories (=compute/input/=,
  =publish/run/wrappers=) have nothing to parse.
- *Use the policy's name.* =report_cashflows_90d= names the policy, and a store
  may have no policy at all, which is exactly the row that most needs a name.
- *A store catalogue.* One row per store the installation keeps: its code, its
  display name, its description, the shape its target has, the target itself,
  how to measure it, and the component that owns it. A policy then names a
  store, and "unbounded" becomes a store with no policy rather than a target
  string that appears in none.

The third is the layering the reference-data pages already state: the physical
name is the row's identity and the presentation name is metadata the platform
publishes. It also turns the story's inventory of every dimension that grows
from a document into data the screen can read, and it gives the size read
somewhere to hang: a store that says how to measure itself is one the size read
can answer for.

This prototype draws the first two ways badly on purpose and the third properly:
the fixtures carry a =label= and a =description=, the target sits beneath the
label in monospace, and the detail panel states both. When the catalogue exists,
the label and the description come from it; until then they are the screen's own
copy, which is a reason to build the catalogue rather than to write twelve names
into a client.

** The states a store can be in

| State | What the screen says | Why it matters |
|-------+----------------------+----------------|
| fresh | Applied; freed 0, because nothing is older than the period | A zero is not a failure, and the screen must not imply one |
| skipped | The policy is enabled and the store is not bounded: the sweep performs a chunk drop and this policy asks for something else | The dangerous state: it reads as bounded on paper |
| failed | The sweep ran and raised; the detail is the server's message | The store is unbounded until somebody reads the message |
| disabled | The policy is off, so the store is kept for ever | A decision, and it should read as one |
| empty | No policy at all, with the way to add one | The store was never decided about |

** What is fake

Everything. The sizes, the growth rates, the policies, the runs and the person
signed in are mock data taken from one environment at one moment: 283 MB of
calendar dates, 186 MB of report cashflows after 39 runs, 29 MB of delivery
counts growing at about 27,000 rows an hour, 1.9 GB of host scratch. No request
leaves the page.

** The verdict

(None yet. The shape is proposed, not accepted: variant A carries forward on the
strength of its density and its likeness to the logs screen, but the screen
cannot be built until a size-of-target read exists, and the runs log cannot be
read at all until PR #5126 adds the retention-run wire type. The prototype is
the argument for building both.)
