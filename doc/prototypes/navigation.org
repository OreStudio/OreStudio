* Summary

Every journey in the catalogue reaches its screen by the person "choosing" it,
and no document owned how. This is the answer, as one design rather than a
comparison: a *menu* across the header, the *cards* it opens, and the person's
own journeys under their *name and photo*.

What the shell shows is decided by **who signed in**. A super administrator
works in the system tenant's context, a tenant administrator in a tenant's, and
a party user in a party's — the contexts the server already scopes everything
by. So the mode is read from the sign-in and is never chosen, and there is no
mode switch to get wrong.

It is throwaway. It is not production code, it reads no service, and every value
on it is mock data.

Open the [[file:navigation/][shell]].

* Detail

** The verdict

Accepted on 2026-10-01: the menu, the cards and the avatar, with the mode read
from the sign-in. This is the shell the Profile journeys hang from, and the
implementation is scheduled from it.

** What was rejected, and why

The page went through four review rounds. Each rejection is the reason the
design is what it is, so they belong in the record even though the shapes
themselves are not drawn any more.

| Shape | Why it went |
|-------+-------------|
| An account menu holding every journey | At twenty journeys it holds neither the administrator's work nor the groups, and its trigger tried to answer a question about the account rather than about the product. |
| One axis of journey groups across the header | A journey group is a topic ("Profile", "Credentials"); an *area* of activity is above it ("Reference Data", "Trading"). A trading system has the areas, and the Qt client's first level already said so. |
| A sidebar beside the area's cards | It listed the same journeys the cards already list, one beside the other. The cards are the navigation; a second copy of them is a cost with no reader. |
| "Administration" as an area | A name that means everything we could not place. The scopes are Me, People and Tenant, and they account for all twenty journeys with nothing left over. |
| *Bootstrap* as an area | The first run journey belongs to a plain installation, where there is no account to sign in with and so no shell to draw a menu in. An area nobody can be in while it is open is a door, not a destination. |
| A mode the person chooses | The mode is the context the server already scopes everything by — the system tenant, the tenant, the party the session acts for. Reading it from the sign-in is the only version that cannot disagree with the data. |

** The mode comes from the sign-in

| Sign in as | Mode | The context |
|------------+------+-------------|
| Super administrator | System administration | The system tenant: the deployment's tenants, the registry they share |
| Tenant administrator | Tenant administration | A tenant: its parties, its role catalogue, its settings — not its data |
| Privileged party user | Application | A tenant and a party, including the people who work in it |
| Regular party user | Application | A tenant and a party |

The four are the platform's own axes, not a new taxonomy. The system tenant has
a fixed id and holds the deployment's registry and the shared reference data; a
tenant row is stored under it; and the session already carries the party it acts
for. A mode is the answer to "which context am I in", and the server answers it
before the browser draws anything.

** The menu holds areas, and the cards hold journeys

The menu is one row of *areas* — the trading floor's own shape, which the Qt
menu bar already had right. It changes with the mode:

| Mode | The menu |
|------+----------|
| System administration | Tenants |
| Tenant administration | Parties, Access, Tenant |
| Application | Reference Data, Market Data, Trading, Analytics, Compute, Reporting, People |

An area the catalogue has not reached stays in the menu and says so in as many
words. An area that belongs to somebody else does not: a regular party user is
not shown the door to the people of their party, and no mode offers an
administrator journey to the person who may not run it.

*** The bootstrap journey is not an area

*First run* was drawn under a *Bootstrap* area, and that was wrong. It belongs to
a plain installation: an empty database with no account in it. Nobody can be
signed in while it is the screen, so it is not a place the shell can be in and
not a door the shell can offer. It is the way out of the state the shell cannot
be entered from, and that makes it a door rather than a destination.

The system administrator's work is what the first run leaves behind: a
deployment with an account, and no tenant until one is made. So the *Tenants*
area is the whole of the system administration menu, and it holds the tenant
management journeys the catalogue has: creating one, and retiring or resetting
one. *Tune the tenant* is not among them — the tenant administrator maintains
their own tenant's details, from inside it.

This is the one place the extraction's grouping was not the menu's grouping, and
the correction is recorded here rather than in the catalogue: the catalogue's
*Setup* group and its *Tenancy* group both hold journeys the *Tenants* area
offers, because a group is what a designer looks a journey up under and an area
is where a person goes to find it.

The cards are one per catalogue topic, and they are where a journey is chosen.
One area's cards, or every area's, depending on whether the person picked an
area from the menu or is on the landing page.

** The person's own journeys are not an area

Every system puts "things about me" under the avatar, and nobody gives them a
menu. The header carries the person's name and photo, and it opens their own
journeys, gathered by topic: profile, credentials, and in a party their access
and where they work. The super administrator keeps their profile and gets no
party journeys, because there is no party to choose.

** The three states

| State | What it shows |
|-------+---------------|
| =home= | The landing page: every area the person has, each with its cards. |
| =journey= | One journey running under its mode, with the cards it came from. |
| =fullscreen= | A journey that stands something up. It owns the screen, so no menu and no cards are drawn. |

** What is real and what is not

The areas, the journeys, the modes, the routes and which journeys have been
built are the catalogue's own. Six of the eleven areas are empty because the
extraction has been run for one component, and the prototype renders that
rather than inventing screens to fill them.

Everything else is mock. The people, the tenants and the parties are invented,
the photos are the repository's own stock faces, and nothing reads a service.

** Where the code lives

=doc/prototypes/navigation/=. Three files of code plus the two photos, plain
HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework and no build step. The site build copies
the directory through =ores-deploy-web-app=, beside the other prototypes.

** What it does not settle

How a person learns that a journey exists but cannot be run. Nothing here states
what a deployment switches on or off, because no document says a journey has a
switch.

The mode is read from the sign-in, and the contract that delivers it — the
server stating the mode with the session, and the menu taking its authority from
the permission codes the session holds — is
[[id:37D5E28C-A83F-4EDC-891B-4F6F23B2BB42][Session modes and capabilities]].

How the account a person signs in with comes to *be* the privileged one. The
mode is read from the sign-in, which is only honest if a super administrator's
account is distinguishable from a person who happens to hold the role — and
today nothing distinguishes them, or says when they held it. That is its own
problem, with its own process, and it is captured at
[[id:AC2BA866-D2C3-4C8B-AF5A-D573D1317A78][Privileged access needs a recorded
break-glass process]].

Where a party user with no areas of their own lands once the party's data is
real: today six of their seven areas are empty and their avatar is where the
work is.
