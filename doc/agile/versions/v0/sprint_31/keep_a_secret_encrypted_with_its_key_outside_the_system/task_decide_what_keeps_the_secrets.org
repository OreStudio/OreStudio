* Goal

Decide, before any code, three things that every later task depends on:
whether ORE keeps the secrets it must use in a service of its own or inside a
component it already has, where the key that protects them lives, and how a
secret reaches the service that needs it without a plaintext crossing the bus.
The decision has to be reconcilable with the component model, the layer rules
and IAM's stated responsibilities, because the cheapest place to get it wrong
is a component that a Foundation component links.

* Acceptance

- The place a secret is kept is decided against the component model: the two
  component shapes, the sub-component role catalogue, the collision rule and
  the layer invariant, not against preference.
- The alternatives are recorded with what each would have cost.
- The home of the key that protects the secrets is decided, and stated as a
  property of one unit rather than of a file.
- How a secret reaches a consumer is decided, and it answers the objection that
  a secret must not travel in the clear over NATS or HTTP.
- The boundary with IAM is stated concern by concern, so that neither component
  can grow into the other's subject.
- The secrets the workspace holds today are inventoried, and each is either
  given a phase or excluded with a reason.

* Notes

** The analysis

Three placements were tested against the same constraints, and the details
live in [[id:F9F1A2BA-94C2-4DAA-8689-F0871AA56801][Secret Custody in the
Vault]] rather than here.

- *The vault inside =ores.security=.* The component would hold the kernel and a
  Domain service at once. The kernel is linked from Foundation
  (=ores.variability.core=) and from Infrastructure (the service runner builds
  the verifier and checks the permission codes in five headers of
  =ores.service=), so a component that Foundation links cannot be documented as
  a Domain component. The move is cheap in effort — twenty-four template lines
  own every generated include — and expensive in the model, which is why it was
  rejected.
- *The vault inside =ores.iam=.* Cohesion fails: custody of a third party's
  secret is not identity lifecycle, authentication, authorisation or
  enforcement, and the component that holds the most identity data should not
  also hold the key to every secret.
- *=ores.vault=, a Domain composite.* Chosen, with =api=, =core=, =service= and
  =client=, and =ores.security= unchanged as the kernel.

** The transport

The objection that a secret must not travel in the clear over NATS or HTTP is
answered by sealing in both directions rather than by relying on mutual TLS:
the write seals to the vault's transport public key, and the resolve seals to
the consumer's, whose key the vault looks up by the subject in the verified
token and never takes from the request. The mechanism, its keys and its
residual risks are in [[id:4F28F755-7DA1-4EDD-9983-256622B6088A][Sealed Delivery
of a Secret to Its Consumer]].

** The inventory

[[id:DBF20EC5-63BB-4DE5-957C-5322DE0E487F][Secrets Register]] accounts for all
twenty-nine values the environment file holds, plus the provider client secret
that is currently nowhere, and states for each whether it moves, stays or is
deleted. The migration is three phases, and each ends in a state the tree can
run in.

* Result

ORE keeps a secret it must present to another party in =ores.vault=, a Domain
composite with =api=, =core=, =service= and =client=; =ores.security= keeps the
primitives and stays a Foundation component, gaining the sealed box beside the
symmetric sealer. The key that protects the stored secrets is a system
credential of the vault's unit, never the database, the checkout or the
environment file. A secret is written sealed to the vault and resolved sealed to
the consumer, so no plaintext crosses the bus in either direction. IAM keeps
every one-way credential and every decision about who may ask; the vault keeps
custody and adds only the record of each use.

The decision is recorded in the story's =* Decisions= and described in three
knowledge pages: the custody page, the delivery page, and the register.
