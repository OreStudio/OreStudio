* What the starvation looks like

The pool opens every one of its connections when it is made and never opens
another, so the number is a ceiling on concurrency and not a cushion. Two is too
small for a service that runs two provisioning steps at once and four lanes in
each: the surfaces step met =No connection came free within the acquire timeout=
in a run, and the answer was to offer the unit twice more rather than to give the
service the connection it was asking for. A retry is a symptom; the pool should
have grown.

So the requirement is not "a bigger number". It is that a service may burst to
the concurrency its work really has, and give the connections back when the burst
is over.

* Acceptance

- The number of units of work the service may hold at once is stated once and
  is the number the pool serves: step workers, lanes per step and pool size
  agree, and a reader of any one of them can find the other two.
- A pool smaller than the lanes is either raised or served by a lane count that
  fits it, and the choice is written down with the reason.
- A step that meets a full pool still finishes: the retry is the fallback and
  not the plan.
- No step fails a party because a lane waited for a connection.
- The pool states two numbers, the connections it keeps and the connections it
  may burst to, and opens the second only when a caller is waiting for one.
  A connection above the first is closed after it has been idle for a while, so
  an idle service holds the first number and not the second.
- The burst ceiling is at least the service's real concurrency: its step workers
  times its lanes, plus the handler work beside them.
- On a host without a pooler, the burst ceiling is small enough that a fleet's
  bursts still fit the server's =max_connections=. On a host with one, the
  ceiling may be large, because the pooler's cap bounds what reaches the server.

* Plan

The three numbers today, read from the tree:

| What | Value | Where |
|------+-------+-------|
| Step workers on the marketdata service | 2 | =provisioning_step_registrar.cpp=, =provisioning_step_workers= |
| Lanes per step | 4 | =run_party_surfaces=, =run_party_forwards=, =run_party_curves= |
| Connections in the pool | 2 | =database_configuration.cpp=, the =db-pool-size= default, with no =ORES_MARKETDATA_SERVICE_DB_POOL_SIZE= in =.env= |

So two steps and 4 lanes each can ask for eight statements at once while the
tick ingest and the due-curve sweep ask for their own. The retry added with the
lanes (=fit_and_publish_surface_with_retry= and its two siblings) offers a unit
twice more when the store says the pool is full, which is why the step
finishes; it is not a sizing.

The work is to state the budget, then make the three agree: either the pool
grows to the lanes the service runs, or the lanes are derived from the pool and
the workers rather than written as a constant. The measurement that decides it
is the number of connections the service actually holds at the peak of a run,
and the acquisition failures it records, not a wall clock.

** The shape the numbers should take

=db-pool-size= is one number today: connections opened at construction, kept for
life, and simultaneously the service's concurrency and its share of the server.
The proposal is to split it:

| Setting | Means | Direct (a developer's own database) | Pooled (a shared instance) |
|---------+-------+-------------------------------------+----------------------------|
| =db-pool-min= | connections kept, opened at first use | 2 | 1 |
| =db-pool-max= | ceiling a burst may reach | the work, about 8-16 | the work, about 8-16 |
| =db-pool-idle= | how long a connection above the min lives | tens of seconds | tens of seconds |

The maxima are the same in both columns on purpose: the service's concurrency is
a property of its work, not of what is in front of the database. What differs is
what the server sees - a pooled host sees pgbouncer's cap, a direct host sees the
burst itself, which is why the direct case only carries it on a database nobody
else is using.

The pool needs to be able to grow, which it cannot do today: =pq::pool= opens
everything in its constructor and its =acquire= only waits. Growing on demand and
reaping above the minimum is the change, and it is small and local: the pool is
one class with one mutex, one condition variable and one vector.

An alternative worth rejecting out loud: raising =db-pool-size= for everyone.
That gives the burst and keeps the connections forever, and on a shared host it
multiplies by the number of services in every environment - the opposite of what
the pooler is being brought in to fix.

** Why this now waits on the pooler

A pooled connection *is* a server connection today, so the same number answers
two questions at once: how much concurrency the service has, and how much of the
server's =max_connections= it takes. Three numbers cannot be reconciled while
they are one, which is why the pool defaults to two and the lanes ask for four:
the pool was sized to the server, not to the work.

Behind a transaction pooler the two questions separate. The service's
=db-pool-size= becomes how many statements it may have in flight, which the
lanes and workers do argue for; pgbouncer's =default_pool_size= and
=max_db_connections= become the server's budget, stated once per environment
instead of summed across every service in it. Sizing the pool before the pooler
lands would fix the client number to a server constraint that is about to move.

Measured on 2026-11-20 for the record: one PostgreSQL server carries six
development databases and =max_connections = 1500=; an idle environment held 38
connections across its fleet, all idle, at the default two a service
(=pg_stat_activity= grouped by =datname=).
