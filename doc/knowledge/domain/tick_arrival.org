#+title: A tick clock draws its gaps from an arrival process
#+description: How a synthetic feed decides when its next tick comes — the existing tick clock takes its gaps from an arrival process that is periodic, Poisson or Hawkes — and how the elapsed time reaches the price, so that bursts change when prices arrive and not the law of the price.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :synthetic:producers:arrival:point_process:design:
#+created: 2026-10-23
#+updated: 2026-10-24

* Summary

A synthetic feed has two separate jobs. One job decides /what/ the next price
is. The other decides /when/ it arrives. The
[[id:E1C64302-FA31-4C25-8E92-38265013F644][generator library]] holds the first
job: a value process whose =next()= returns a price. The existing =tick_clock=
holds the second, with one fixed period. This page argues for one change to
the second job and one to the first.

1. *The tick clock stays the only clock.* It stops reading a constant period
   and asks an *arrival process* for each gap. Today's behaviour becomes the
   *periodic* arrival process, so a feed can still be fully regular. The
   Poisson and Hawkes processes are two more arrival processes.
2. *The value process is told how much time has passed.* A bursty clock then
   changes when prices are seen, and does not change the law of the price in
   real time.

*Status:* design. The classes named here are the plan of the "Add a Hawkes tick
arrival process" story and they do not exist in the tree yet. This page changes
to describe the code when the code lands.

* Detail

** What exists today

The one clock every feed runs on is =tick_clock= in
=ores.synthetic.api/feeds/tick_clock.hpp=, described in
[[id:46FF8EF4-3874-4C9A-9222-69DA691E793F][the FX spot producer]]. It takes
=ticks_per_hour=, turns that into one fixed period, sleeps for the period in
slices of a hundred milliseconds so that =stop()= is seen quickly, and then runs
one tick. Every gap is equal, so a feed with a =ticks_per_hour= of 3600 ticks
once a second, every second, for ever.

The note
[[id:BB2B7C4C-FD4E-4DCA-BF1D-00F929622B87][Synthetic market data generation: approach]]
already plans the other modes. Its section on the tick clock lists Fixed,
Poisson, Regime-switching and Hawkes, and it names the Hawkes parameters. That
note says what a user may choose. This page says where the choice lives in the
code.

** Plugging into the clock

=tick_clock= keeps everything it does now: the slicing, the stop check, the
guard around one tick and the count. One thing leaves it, the line that
computes the period. In its place the clock holds a gap source and asks it for
the next gap before each sleep. The source is an interface in
=ores.analytics.quant=, because that library must not depend on the synthetic
service:

- =i_arrival_process= has one method, =next_gap()=, that returns the time in
  seconds from the last tick to the next. The process owns its state and its
  random engine and is not thread-safe, like every value process.
- =periodic_arrival_process= returns the same gap every time. It is what
  =tick_clock= does today, moved behind the interface, and it is the default.
  A feed that wants no randomness at all keeps it.
- =poisson_arrival_process= returns an exponential gap
  ([[id:A296C7C0-780D-46DD-A8B4-8B7998C17B8C][Poisson Process]]).
- =hawkes_arrival_process= returns a gap that depends on the earlier ones
  ([[id:D72B9899-C7EC-4F27-BE17-89AE6FC18A7A][Hawkes Process]]).
- =process_factory= gets a third entry, =make_arrival_process=, beside
  =make_process= and =make_yield_curve_process=. Like the second, and unlike
  the first, it throws on a name it does not know. A silent fallback to a
  periodic timer would hide a mistyped mode as a feed that quietly stays
  regular.

*** Why "periodic"

The obvious word, "steady", is taken. =std::chrono::steady_clock= and the
asio =steady_timer= mean a clock that never goes backwards, which says nothing
about how far apart the ticks are. "Fixed" is the word the approach note uses
for the user, and it is vague about what is fixed. A point process with equal
gaps is called *periodic* in the literature, and a reader of the code meets the
same word. The approach note's "Fixed interval" is the periodic arrival process.

The class names follow the snake_case of the rest of the code. The existing
interfaces use =IStochasticProcess= and =IYieldCurveProcess=. A task in the
story renames them, and this page follows the rename.

** The parameters

A feed names its mode and one set of numbers.

| Mode | Parameters | Meaning |
|------+------------+---------|
| Periodic | ticks per hour | Equal gaps. |
| Poisson | ticks per hour | The mean rate. Gaps are exponential. |
| Hawkes | ticks per hour, branching ratio, decay | The long-run mean rate, how clustered the stream is, and how fast a burst fades. |

The Hawkes row uses the parametrisation in
[[id:D72B9899-C7EC-4F27-BE17-89AE6FC18A7A][Hawkes Process]]: the mean rate
$\bar\lambda$, the branching ratio $n$ and the decay $\beta$. The factory turns
them into the baseline $\mu = \bar\lambda(1-n)$ and the jump $\alpha = n\beta$.
The reason is that =ticks_per_hour= keeps its meaning in every mode. A person
who switches from Periodic to Hawkes keeps the same load on the pipeline, and
changes only how that load is spread in time. The approach note's
$(\mu, \alpha, \beta)$ is the same process in other coordinates.

The unit of the arrival process is the second, and =ticks_per_hour= stays the
unit of the config. The factory converts once. The second is the unit because
the clock sleeps in wall time. The process itself has no notion of a unit, and
a template parameter would add noise for no gain.

** How the elapsed time reaches the price

A random gap makes the old convention wrong. Each value process steps by one
tick of a fixed length. The Gaussian mixture processes take their moments
per tick. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process takes a year fraction =dt= when it is
built and uses it for every step. If a gap is half a tick or three ticks, a
step of one tick is too big or too small.

So the step takes the elapsed time:

- =IStochasticProcess= gains =next(double elapsed)=. =elapsed= is the time since
  the last step, counted in *nominal ticks*, where one nominal tick is the tick
  length the process was configured for: the length of one period at the
  feed's =ticks_per_hour=. It has no unit and it needs no calendar.
- =next()= stays, and means =next(1.0)=.
- The clock turns a gap in seconds into nominal ticks by dividing by the
  nominal period, and passes the result to the tick body. A periodic clock
  passes exactly one. A Poisson or Hawkes clock at the same =ticks_per_hour=
  passes numbers whose long-run mean is one.

A process applies =elapsed= by the rule that is exact for its law:

| Process | Step for elapsed $s$ |
|---------+----------------------|
| Geometric mixture | The component's log-return is $N(m_k s,\ \sigma_k^2 s)$. Each component is a Brownian motion seen for $s$ ticks. |
| Arithmetic mixture | The same, on the increment. |
| Ornstein-Uhlenbeck | The exact transition with $\Delta t = s \cdot dt$. The formula already holds for any step. |

The mixture rule is a modelling choice. Each regime is a Brownian motion, so
means scale with $s$ and variances scale with $s$, and the weights stay as set.
It is exact if the regime holds for the whole step.

At $s = 1$ every rule reduces to today's draw, with the same random numbers in
the same order. A periodic feed therefore produces the same prices as before for
the same seed, and the existing tests are the proof.

*** What the rule buys

The price process, in real time, no longer depends on the tick times. A feed
that ticks twice as often sees half-size steps, and the sum of those steps over
an hour has the same law. Volatility per unit of real time stays constant, so a
burst of ticks shows many small moves and a lull shows few large ones. A
Hawkes clock changes when the price is observed and leaves the price alone.
This is also the limit of the model. In a live market bursts and large moves
come together, and here they are independent. Linking the two is a different
model and is out of scope.

*** Processes that do not take it

The short-rate processes of the yield curve path precompute their step from
=dt=. A lattice such as Black-Derman-Toy has steps fixed by its construction,
and the matrix processes (Heath-Jarrow-Morton, Libor market, the affine and
quadratic Gaussian models) precompute a covariance for one step. Only the three
engines above take =next(elapsed)= in this story. The others throw when called
with an elapsed other than one, and the validation refuses a non-periodic mode
for a process type that does not support it. The IR curve feed stays periodic,
and its =discount_factor(ticks_ahead)= counts nominal ticks. The exact
single-factor short-rate processes can take the same rule later with no change
to the interface.

** What validation guards

=validate_process_parameters= is the one rule set that the service and the Qt
client share. It gains the arrival rules:

- the rate is positive and finite;
- the decay is positive and finite;
- the branching ratio is at least zero and strictly below one;
- a non-periodic mode is allowed only for a process type that takes a variable
  step.

A branching ratio of one or more gives a stream that does not settle. A ratio
very close to one gives gaps so short that the pipeline cannot keep up. The
process also stops after a fixed number of events in one request and reports
that it did so, which guards the tick loop against a parameter set that the
rules let through.

** What stays the same

The pipeline after the feed does not change. A tick carries its time, the
marketdata ingest loop plans and stores it, and the rule in
[[id:F64DB34E-084D-4437-B536-392AA26D8B77][No code path may behave differently because a source is simulated]]
holds: the arrival process is a property of the generator and no later stage
reads it.
