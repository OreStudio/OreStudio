* Goal

Provisioning completes on a busy host. A step reports its progress as a heartbeat and fails only when that heartbeat stalls, never on elapsed time. No request does work that grows with the data. Each step runs in the service that owns its work, so iam carries only identity work and no workflow that is not about identity waits on it.

* Acceptance

- A step that keeps reporting progress is never failed for taking long, and a step whose heartbeat stops fails within a bounded time.
- The shell shows each running step's progress from its heartbeat.
- No request handler does work that grows with the tenant's data inside one request.
- Every provisioning step that is not identity work runs outside ores.iam, and ores.iam links no synthetic, marketdata or reporting core library.
- Acme provisioning completes on a loaded host, and a check books every generated trade type through the database.

* Decisions

- *Iam stays off the critical path of non-identity work, 2026-11-12.* The
  user's rule: a key service like iam must not be anywhere near the critical
  path of a workflow whose activity is not identity. Today every provisioning
  step is dispatched to =iam.v1.ops.provision_tenant_step=, so iam generates
  trades, publishes curves, attaches photos and schedules reports, and links
  the synthetic, marketdata and reporting libraries to do so. Its step
  consumer runs one step at a time, so the parallel steps queue: a run on
  this branch waited two minutes before iam took =set_staff_passwords=.
- *A step fails when it stalls, not when it is slow, 2026-11-12.* The user's
  rule: a fixed timeout makes no sense while a step is working; the step
  reports progress as a heartbeat, and only a stalled heartbeat times out.
  The claim lease a step holder renews is already a heartbeat, but the
  engine also applies a fixed per-step deadline, and a step's own requests
  run under the fixed 30 second request deadline with no progress channel.
- *The evidence, 2026-11-12.* Acme provisioning failed with "NATS request
  timed out" because =set_staff_passwords= reset 74 passwords in one request,
  about 30 seconds of hashing on a busy host. A page of eight unblocked it
  (PR 4648); that is the bounded-request rule applied where it failed, not
  the redesign. The same run showed =compass db recreate -k= refusing while
  the stopped services' connections still drained, for about 15 seconds.
- *Builds on running steps concurrently, 2026-11-12.* The story
  [[id:1D550641-953B-4F17-BFA4-B1DB1EFBE62C][Run a service's steps concurrently]] gave a service a pool of step
  workers. That shortens the queue inside one service; this story removes
  the reason the queue forms in iam.

- *A moved step writes as a service account, not as a person, 2026-11-14.*
  =generate_trades= writes trades whose author is a trader of the party, and
  the trading service takes the tenant, the party and the author from the
  caller's claims rather than from the request. iam mints those claims in
  process, through =internal_impersonation_service=, and that service says it is
  deliberately not on any subject. Two facts settle the question of what a
  moved step should be given.
  - Trading filters =ores_trading_trades_tbl= by tenant *and*, restrictively,
    by party: =trades_party_isolation_policy= keeps a session to the parties it
    can see. A session scoped to the party the work belongs to therefore has
    the reach the work needs, and impersonating a trader would narrow it no
    further.
  - The run's actor is already named in the step command, and the provisioning
    service already writes other rows as its own service account.
  So the shapes weigh up as:
  1. *A tenant-scoped writable session for the service account.* The service
     asks to enter a tenant it serves and acts as itself. Nothing new can be
     impersonated, the authorship the store records is true, and the capability
     is the one the service already has. Cost: generated trades name the
     service, not a person.
  2. *The step's own actor.* The request names the step being served and iam
     mints for that step's recorded actor. Cost: a service can write history in
     a person's name, and buys no reach the party-scoped session lacks.
  3. *The party's own traders, or any account the caller names.* Faithful
     authorship, and the largest surface: a compromise of the service becomes
     the identity of any person it names, with that person's permissions and
     visible parties, and every write it makes is theirs in the trail. This is
     the shape to avoid.
  Recommendation: shape 1, and it is implemented: the session is scoped to the
  tenant's party, which is the reach the work needs, and the store keeps a
  booking to the session's author, so a request that names a person is
  overwritten (case =a_booking_is_stamped_with_the_sessions_actor_not_the_requests=). Authorship of generated
  data is a simulated fact, and attributing it to real people creates a
  capability to write in their names for no reach it lacks. Shapes 2 and 3 stay
  recorded as the ways to get per-trader authorship if the product decides the
  demonstration needs it, with the cost stated.
- *A service reads the installation's blueprints as itself, 2026-11-15.* The
  two steps that give a party its own curves and surfaces copy blueprints that
  live in the system tenant, and they read them through an administrator
  session there because a tenant-scoped session cannot see the system's rows. A
  service session is refused the system tenant by this story's own rule, so the
  question was how the blueprints reach the service.
  - *Widening the read to every tenant was tried first, and withdrawn.* The
    four blueprint tables joined the thirty-nine that already say "every session
    reads its own rows and the system tenant's rows", with the party scope
    passing them too. It works, and the cost is not the policy: a read is
    narrowed twice, so the repositories had to widen as well, and a
    repository-wide widening changes every reader. The scheduled
    =publish_due_curves= sweep then saw the installation's 20 curve configs
    beside the tenant's own 2, and =messaging_publish_due_curves_tests= caught
    it. Every consumer of those repositories would have to filter, which is a
    footgun, not a rule.
  - *A read subject in refdata would not avoid the question.* Row-level
    security reads the caller's tenant, so such a subject either widens the
    policy exactly as above or opens a session inside the system tenant, which
    is the hole the rule closes.
  - *Shipping the blueprint content in the seed profile* moves where the truth
    lives and duplicates it, and a template that changes leaves every tenant
    stale.
  So the service reads the catalogue itself, in the system tenant where it
  lives, through its own database context: =tenant_context::with_system_tenant=
  over the marketdata service's context, the same way the feed ingest loop
  already reads the installation's feeds and mappings. No session inside that
  tenant is entered, no person is impersonated, and nothing a tenant may read
  changes. The rows the step copies are exactly the installation's, so a second
  run copies the template and never a copy of a copy.
- *The system tenant is not entered, so its own curves wait for a decision,
  2026-11-17.* Every marketdata kind of step 4 moved but =publish_system_curves=.
  That step publishes the *installation's* curves, in the system tenant, as the
  installation's administrator, through the impersonation service iam keeps to
  itself. A move is a port only while the authority travels with it, and here it
  does not: =enter_tenant_as_service= refuses the system tenant outright ("The
  system tenant is not entered; it is the deployment's own"), which is the rule
  this story set so that no service session reaches the installation's rows.
  A service that works in the system tenant therefore works as itself there,
  and two questions follow that no slice of this task can answer by itself:
  which party of the system tenant owns the installation's published sets, and
  whether the service's own role may be the maker of an installation-wide
  publish. Until someone states them, iam keeps the step and the impersonation
  it uses, and the task stays open.
- *Four services work one run at once, 2026-11-17.* The measured run has iam,
  refdata, synthetic and marketdata working overlapping steps: between 15:10:59
  and 15:13:32 marketdata served a party's curves and its surface blueprints
  while iam reset passwords and scheduled reports, and refdata's calendars had
  been building since 15:09:57. The acceptance line "the parallel steps of a
  run execute in different services at once" is measured now, not argued. The
  cost the run also shows is that the surface publish is the long pole, at
  20 min 27 s of 26 min: it stays alive on its progress reports, and its retry
  budget is taken per party, so a wider run needs that budget measured rather
  than assumed.
- *A command that cannot read the state it guards on refuses, 2026-11-14.*
  A recreate asks whether anything is still connected before it drops, and an
  answer that never came back used to read as "nothing is connected". The
  count is cluster-wide, so an absent database answers zero and silence is
  never absence: the command refuses and says what it could not read, because
  the cost of guessing is a dropped database.
- *A config draws one asset class, 2026-11-14.* A generated trade is named by
  its config, so two asset classes drawn from one config book the same trade
  ids and every one after the first is refused as already booked. The check
  that books every generated type found this, and a provisioner has to state a
  config per class, as the config table already says it should.
- *A service serves one family of step commands per stream, 2026-11-13.* The
  first end-to-end run of the per-service dispatch failed because ores.refdata
  declared a second step family on the consumer its data-quality publish steps
  already used: the second family replaced the first one's filter, the countries
  publish was never delivered, and the run that nested it stood still for twenty
  minutes with nothing in the log to say why. A family now names a group of its
  own, and =serve_step_commands= refuses a second family on one stream rather
  than let a service silently stop serving one.
- *A moved step writes as its own service, 2026-11-13.* The calendar step wrote
  =modified_by = "system"=, which the account trigger refuses now that the tenant
  has accounts. A step that acts for the tenant being provisioned writes as the
  service that runs it, which is an account of the system tenant, so the trigger
  accepts it and the row says which service did the work.

* Out of scope

- The account read that the password and picture steps make with
  =limit = 10'000= is the one request left that grows with the tenant's data. It
  is captured as
  [[id:DC63BCCB-0967-4A2D-B978-2033DF7C943D][Provisioning asks for every account in one request, and drops what is past ten thousand]],
  because bounding it means giving the step a page to advance through, which is
  the shape of the step that moves to its owning service.

- Changing what provisioning provisions: the steps, the profile and the data
  stay as they are; only where and how a step runs changes.
