#+title: Bind Before You Call Your Own Service
#+description: A service registers every subscription before it makes any request, including its own, because a subject is not callable until its handler is registered.
#+type: nats_pattern
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :pattern:nats:ownership:

+#+updated: 2026-10-14

This page defines the [[id:E7A9C1D3-5B8F-4A20-9C6E-1D3F5A7B9C11][nats
pattern]] *Bind Before You Call Your Own Service* in the
[[id:B4E6F1A2-7C3D-4E5F-8A9B-0C1D2E3F4A5B][NATS service patterns]]
language. It is the mechanism that makes
[[id:1D02DB5B-1E50-4AA0-9780-A3F1CF237349][Service Bootstrap Phases]] safe.

* Also known as

- The two-phase bootstrap; wiring before readiness activities.

* Context

A service's startup does two different kinds of thing: it wires subjects to
handlers, and it does work that itself needs a subject — minting its service
token, warming a cache, reading a bootstrap status. The second kind calls a
subject, and some of those subjects belong to the service itself.

* Problem

In what order may a service register handlers and call subjects, when some of
the subjects it calls are its own?

* Forces

- Dispatch is on its own thread, so a subject is callable the instant it is
  subscribed.
- A service that calls its own subject before registering the handler waits
  out a timeout.
- With several replicas, another replica may answer; a single-replica
  development environment has no one else to answer.
- The failure looks like a hung service, not an ordering bug.

* Solution

Two phases, in this order: register every subscription, then do everything that
calls a subject.

#+begin_src cpp :results verbatim
// Phase 1 — wire every subject. Nothing here calls a subject.
auto subs = messaging::registrar::register_handlers(nats, ctx, signer);

// Phase 2 — everything that calls a subject, including our own.
warm_the_cache(nats);           // safe only because phase 1 finished
mint_the_service_token(nats);
announce_ready();
// ... run until signalled ...
nats.drain();
#+end_src

Three rules:

- *Register first, call second.* No call to any subject before the last
  subscription is registered.
- *A self-call is still a call.* A subject the service itself answers obeys the
  same rule as a subject another service answers.
- *The order is the structure, not a comment.* Phase 1 is one call that returns
  the handles; phase 2 is what follows it.

* Failure handling

#+ATTR_HTML
| Concern | What the pattern does |
|---------+-----------------------|
| Errors | A self-call in the wrong phase does not error; it waits out the request deadline and reports a timeout. |
| Timeouts | The symptom of the wrong order is a request timeout at startup, with a subscriber that looks healthy. |
| Retries | Retrying does not help: the handler will never be registered yet. |
| Idempotency | Registration is idempotent per process; the phases are about order, not repetition. |
| Observability | The handler registration lines precede any request line, so the order is visible in the log. |

* Pitfalls

- *Warming a cache before registering.* The service waits out a deadline
  against itself and may exit as unhealthy.
- *Working in development, failing under load.* Another replica answers the
  self-call, so the bug hides until a single-replica environment.
- *Minting a token inside the registration path.* The registration is now a
  call, and the phases have merged.
- *Registering after readiness.* A caller that reaches the service in between
  gets no responders.

* Resulting context

- Benefits :: Startup has no ordering hazard, and a subject is either not
  advertised or callable.
- Liabilities :: Anything that needs a subject must be expressed as phase 2
  work rather than as part of wiring.

* In ORE Studio

The service runner registers handlers and keeps the returned handles, then
performs readiness activities such as minting the service token or warming a
cache, then drains on shutdown. The party-cache self-login case is the worked
example of the order mattering.
