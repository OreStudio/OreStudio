#+description: The SVI family of implied-variance parameterisations: raw, natural and jump-wings SVI for one expiry, SSVI and extended SSVI for a whole surface, what each parameter does, and which forms carry closed-form conditions for the absence of static arbitrage.

* Summary

SVI, the /stochastic volatility inspired/ parameterisation, writes the total
implied variance of one expiry as a function of log-moneyness with five
parameters. Gatheral introduced it in 2004, and it is widely used because it
fits listed and OTC smiles well and has the right asymptotic shape in both
wings. Its weakness is that no simple condition on the five parameters rules out
arbitrage: a raw SVI smile can imply a negative density, and two SVI slices can
cross. *SSVI*, the /surface SVI/ of Gatheral and Jacquier (2014), restricts
the family so that a whole surface is described by the ATM total variance of
each expiry and two or three further parameters, and for that restriction the
absence of static arbitrage reduces to inequalities on the parameters. The
extended SSVI of Hendriks and Martini (2019) lets the skew parameter vary by
expiry and keeps those inequalities. This note defines each form and states the
conditions; the surfaces built on it are described in
[[id:5B513D53-5DF7-4540-99C0-60D909678873][FX Volatility Surface]].

* Layperson's mental model

Picture the smile of one expiry as a length of bendable wire shaped like a
shallow bowl: its height is how expensive options are in general, and its two
rising arms are how much dearer far-from-the-money options are. Raw SVI gives
you five knobs to bend that wire: raise or lower the whole bowl ($a$), steepen
both arms ($b$), tilt it so one arm rises faster than the other ($\rho$), slide
it sideways ($m$), and round off its bottom ($\sigma$). Five knobs fit almost
any smile. But nothing stops you bending the wire into a shape no real market
could produce, with a kink that implies a negative probability, and when you
bend one wire per expiry, nothing stops two of them from crossing.

SSVI swaps the separate wires for one frame that holds them all. Each expiry
still has its own height, its ATM total variance $\theta_t$, but every wire
shares one tilt ($\rho$) and one stiffness ($\eta$), and the frame bends each
wire from those few numbers. The frame comes with marks on its knobs: keep the
heights rising with expiry and the tilt and stiffness inside their marked
ranges, and no wire can kink or cross another, whatever values you pick inside
the marks. That is what lets a generator turn the knobs continuously without
ever producing an impossible surface.

#+begin_src cpp
// total implied variance at log-moneyness k, for an expiry whose ATM total variance is theta
double ssvi_total_variance(double k, double theta, double rho, double eta) {
    const double phi = eta / std::sqrt(theta);
    const double x = phi * k + rho;
    return 0.5 * theta * (1.0 + rho * phi * k + std::sqrt(x * x + 1.0 - rho * rho));
}
#+end_src

* Detail

** Coordinates

Every form below is written in two quantities. The /log-moneyness/ against the
forward to expiry $t$ is

\begin{equation}
k = \ln\left(\frac{K}{F_t}\right)
\end{equation}

and the /total implied variance/ is

\begin{equation}
w(k, t) = \sigma_{BS}^2(k, t)\, t
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_{BS}$ is the Black-Scholes implied volatility. Total variance is
the natural coordinate because it adds across time: a surface with no calendar
spread arbitrage is one whose total variance does not fall as $t$ rises at any
fixed $k$. The no-arbitrage conditions themselves are in
[[id:3F7A2C91-84BE-4E12-B5D0-1D6E5F08A432][Vol Surface No-Arbitrage Conditions]].

** Raw SVI

For one expiry and a parameter set $\chi_R = \{a, b, \rho, m, \sigma\}$, raw
SVI is (Gatheral and Jacquier, 2014, equation 3.1):

\begin{equation}
w(k; \chi_R) = a + b\left(\rho\,(k - m) + \sqrt{(k - m)^2 + \sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}

with $b \ge 0$, $|\rho| < 1$, $\sigma > 0$ and $a + b\,\sigma\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}
\ge 0$, the last ensuring the variance is never negative. The smile is a
hyperbola in total variance, so each wing is asymptotically linear in $k$, as
Lee's moment formula requires of any arbitrage-free smile. Each parameter has
one visible effect:

| Parameter | Effect on the smile |
|-----------+---------------------|
| $a$ | Raises or lowers the general level of variance |
| $b$ | Steepens both wings together |
| $\rho$ | Rotates the smile: a lower $\rho$ steepens the put wing against the call wing |
| $m$ | Translates the smile along $k$ |
| $\sigma$ | Widens the rounded minimum, lowering the ATM curvature |

The *natural* SVI of the same paper (equation 3.2) is the same family
reparameterised, with an explicit map between the two (Lemma 3.1). The
*jump-wings* SVI-JW (equation 3.5) rewrites the parameters as quantities a
trader reads off a smile: the ATM variance $v_t$, the ATM skew $\psi_t$, the
slopes $p_t$ and $c_t$ of the put and call wings, and the minimum variance
$\tilde{v}_t$. If smiles scaled exactly as $1/\sqrt{w_t}$, the SVI-JW
parameters would not change with the expiry.

** Why raw SVI admits arbitrage

Raw SVI parameterises one slice, and a surface built from it is a separate set
of five parameters per expiry. Two failures follow. A single slice can carry
/butterfly arbitrage/, a negative risk-neutral density at some strike, and
Gatheral and Jacquier give an example with plausible parameters (Example 3.1).
They state that general conditions on the five parameters that exclude it
appear impossible to find, because the density is a highly non-linear function
of them. And two slices can cross, which is /calendar spread arbitrage/; the
paper reduces the check to whether a quartic polynomial has real roots (Lemma
3.3), which is a numerical test rather than a constraint a parameter can be held
to. Martini and Mingone (2020) later characterise the absence of butterfly
arbitrage in raw SVI completely, as conditions that are exact but not simple.

** SSVI: one surface, few parameters

SSVI describes the whole surface at once (Gatheral and Jacquier, 2014,
Definition 4.1):

\begin{equation}
w(k, \theta_t) = \frac{\theta_t}{2}\left(1 + \rho\,\varphi(\theta_t)\,k + \sqrt{\left(\varphi(\theta_t)\,k + \rho\right)^2 + 1 - \rho^2}\right)
\end{equation}

- $\theta_t = \sigma_{BS}^2(0, t)\,t$ is the ATM total variance of expiry $t$.
  It is the only quantity that varies by expiry, and it is directly observable
  from the market; the authors note it as the natural place to impose dynamics
  on the surface (Remark 4.1).
- $\rho \in (-1, 1)$ sets the skew. The smile is symmetric if and only if
  $\rho = 0$, as in a stochastic volatility model whose spot and volatility are
  uncorrelated.
- $\varphi$ is a positive function of $\theta$ that sets the curvature.

The ATM volatility skew is (equation 4.2)

\begin{equation}
\left.\partial_k \sigma_{BS}(k, t)\right|_{k=0} = \frac{\rho\sqrt{\theta_t}}{2\sqrt{t}}\,\varphi(\theta_t)
\end{equation}

The paper gives two forms of $\varphi$:

- The *Heston-like* form (Example 4.1),
  $\varphi(\theta) = \frac{1}{\lambda\theta}\left(1 - \frac{1 - e^{-\lambda\theta}}{\lambda\theta}\right)$,
  consistent with the implied variance skew of the Heston model. Its short-expiry
  smile flattens, as that of a pure diffusion does.
- The *power-law* form (Example 4.2), $\varphi(\theta) = \eta\,\theta^{-\gamma}$
  with $\eta > 0$ and $0 < \gamma < 1$. Its short-expiry smile is V-shaped,
  which is what observed surfaces and models with jumps show. With
  $\gamma = 1/2$ the SVI-JW parameters of every slice are constant (Lemma 4.1)
  and the ATM skew is $\rho\eta/(2\sqrt{t})$, falling as $1/\sqrt{t}$.

** The absence of static arbitrage in SSVI

Corollary 4.1 of the paper: an SSVI surface is free of static arbitrage if, for
all $\theta > 0$,

1. $\partial_t \theta_t \ge 0$;
2. $0 \le \partial_\theta\left(\theta\varphi(\theta)\right) \le \frac{1}{\rho^2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\right)\varphi(\theta)$;
3. $\theta\varphi(\theta)\,(1 + |\rho|) < 4$;
4. $\theta\varphi(\theta)^2\,(1 + |\rho|) \le 4$.

The first two exclude calendar spread arbitrage (Theorem 4.1) and are necessary
as well as sufficient. The last two exclude butterfly arbitrage (Theorem 4.2);
Lemma 4.2 shows the third is necessary and the fourth tight. Unlike raw SVI,
every condition is an inequality on the parameters, so a surface can be held
inside the region by bounding them.

For the power law with $\gamma = 1/2$ the conditions take a particularly simple
form. Condition 2 holds for every $\rho$, because
$\partial_\theta(\theta\varphi)/\varphi = 1/2$ never exceeds
$(1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2})/\rho^2$. Condition 4 becomes
$\eta^2(1 + |\rho|) \le 4$, which holds for any $\rho$ when $\eta \le \sqrt{2}$.
Condition 3 becomes $\eta\sqrt{\theta}\,(1 + |\rho|) < 4$, which, with
$\eta \le \sqrt{2}$, holds whenever $\theta < 2$, that is whenever
$\sigma_{ATM}\sqrt{t} < \sqrt{2}$. So the region is: $\theta_t$ rising in $t$,
$|\rho| < 1$, $0 < \eta \le \sqrt{2}$, and total ATM variance below two. Remark
4.4 of the paper notes the general point behind the last bound: a power-law
SSVI is free of static arbitrage only up to some maximum expiry.

** Extended SSVI

A single $\rho$ ties the skew of every expiry to one number, which limits the
term structures of risk reversal an SSVI surface can represent. Hendriks and
Martini (2019) make $\rho$ a function of the expiry, the *extended SSVI*
(eSSVI), and give necessary and sufficient conditions for that surface to be
free of calendar spread arbitrage. The extension typically fits short expiries
better. A later paper on a global parametrisation of eSSVI that keeps it free of
arbitrage is listed below and has not been read in full.

** In ORE Studio

SSVI with the power-law curvature and $\gamma = 1/2$ is the surface the
synthetic volatility generator evolves, as
[[id:BB2B7C4C-FD4E-4DCA-BF1D-00F929622B87][Synthetic market data generation: approach]]
describes; its parameters are the generator's state, held inside the region
above. A tenant's surface blueprint may fit SSVI, SABR, a spline or another
smile model to the quotes it reads, so the generator's choice does not bind the
tenant's.
