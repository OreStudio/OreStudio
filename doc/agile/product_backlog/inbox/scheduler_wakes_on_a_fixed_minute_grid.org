#+title: scheduler wakes on a fixed minute grid, not the next due instant
#+description: The loop arms a timer for the next wall-clock minute boundary and evaluates every job it holds on each wake, so a scheduler with one daily job wakes 1440 times a day and parses every job's cron expression every time. The due instant is known in advance: the loop should sleep until the earliest next occurrence among the jobs it holds, re-arm when the job set changes, and bound the sleep so a wall-clock adjustment cannot strand a firing.

* What

Sleep until the next due instant, instead of waking on the next minute
boundary. The loop holds each job's next occurrence, sleeps until the
earliest of them, and on each wake fires the jobs whose occurrence has
arrived and recomputes theirs. Two requirements belong to the change:

- The change event that already tells the loop to re-read the job set must
  also re-arm the timer, because a new or edited job may be due sooner than
  the instant the loop is sleeping towards. Without it, a job registered
  with an imminent occurrence waits for a sleep it was not part of.
- The sleep must be bounded and re-derived. A cron expression names
  wall-clock instants, and a monotonic timer does not follow the wall
  clock, so an adjustment (an NTP step, a daylight-saving change) moves the
  due instants while the timer keeps its original duration.

* Why

The loop computes the next wall-clock minute boundary and sleeps until it:

#+begin_src c++
boost::asio::steady_timer timer(ioc);
timer.expires_after(next_minute_boundary(now) - now);
#+end_src

Every wake then evaluates every job the loop holds, and each evaluation asks
the job's expression for its next occurrence after the job's last recorded
firing. The due instant is therefore known in advance and nothing uses it:

- A scheduler holding one job that runs daily wakes 1440 times a day to find
  nothing due.
- Every wake evaluates every held job, and asking an expression for its next
  occurrence parses that expression: the parse happens inside the call, so
  the cost is one parse per job per wake — 1440 parses per job per day for a
  job that fires once. The live status read pays the same parse per job when
  it derives each job's next fire time.
- The rate of the wake is unrelated to the schedules held. The granularity
  is right (a minute is the shortest interval a cron expression can name),
  but the loop wakes on a fixed grid rather than at the instants the jobs
  name.

Correctness is not in question. A job whose occurrence passed while a tick
was slow or the service was down is still fired once at the next wake,
because the reference point is the job's last recorded firing and missed
occurrences are not replayed. The defect is the work done per unit time, and
that work grows with the number of jobs held, most of which are not due at
any given wake. The same design also leaves every job's next occurrence
uncached, so the monitor's status read repeats work the loop has already
done.
