#+title: Privileged access needs a recorded break-glass process
#+description: The mode a session runs in is a property of the account it signed in with, so a super administrator and a tenant administrator are distinguishable only if the account is. Getting there needs roles that request elevation, an approval, and a time-boxed recorded grant with no overlapping windows. The reporting-line proposal needs the same approval primitive, so it should be built once.

This page is a [[id:671F18E4-E09C-4B3B-BD24-D33DF8AE38A6][capture]] in the *inbox* bucket of the product backlog — a pre-sprint idea, not yet pulled into a sprint as a story.

* What

A way to give a person privileged access for a stated reason and a stated
period, to record who approved it, and to be able to say afterwards who did what
while they held it.

The shape proposed on 2026-10-01: roles that let a person *request* super
administrator or tenant administrator access; other people who approve the
request; and, on approval, a recorded temporary login with a time window and a
password. No two windows overlap. There may be more than one such account, so
that two people can work at once. Once this exists, the mode a session runs in
can be read from the account it signed in with.

* Why

The shell reads its mode from the sign-in — a super administrator lands in
system administration, a tenant administrator in tenant administration, a party
user in the application — and that is only honest if the account a person signs
in with *is* the privileged one. Today it cannot be: nothing distinguishes the
person who is a super administrator from the person who happens to hold the
role, and nothing says when they held it.

Two problems follow, and the second is the one that bites.

- *Attribution.* An action taken with privileged rights is attributable to an
  account, not to a person who was granted the right for an afternoon. A review
  asked "who did this, and on whose authority" cannot answer it.
- *Standing privilege.* An account that is always a super administrator is a
  target every day of its life. The point of a break-glass process is that the
  privilege exists only while somebody is using it, and that its use is loud.

This is also the *second* place the platform needs an approval. The reporting
line is the first: a person proposes a change to who they report to, and a
hierarchical superior or a tenant administrator approves it — and the work
recorded that nothing in the platform holds an approval today. The two are the
same missing primitive: *a request, an approver, a decision, and a record of
all three*. It should be built once.

* What other systems do

Two families, and they differ in where the traceability lives.

| Family | How it works | Examples |
|--------+--------------+----------|
| *Elevation on your own account* | A role is assigned as *eligible* rather than active. The person activates it for a bounded period, with a justification and a second factor; an approver may be required; the identity never changes. | Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management; Google Cloud Privileged Access Manager; Okta Identity Governance; Salesforce Elevate |
| *A separate privileged account, checked out* | The credential lives in a vault or is generated per grant. A person checks it out for a window; the vault checks it back in, rotates it, and records the session. | CyberArk and Delinea checkout; SAP GRC "firefighter" ids; Teleport and HashiCorp Vault short-lived credentials |

What the two share, and what is worth copying:

- *The grant is a record, not a setting.* It has a requester, an approver, a
  reason, a start, an end and a revocation. It is readable afterwards.
- *It ends by itself.* A window that has to be closed by hand is a window that
  stays open.
- *Activation is authenticated again.* Elevating is a step that asks for the
  second factor, not a consequence of being signed in.
- *Use is loud.* The systems that do this well alert on activation; the ones
  that do it badly are discovered during an incident review.
- *Break-glass is not the same as just-in-time.* The emergency case is
  deliberately outside the approval flow, because the emergency may be that the
  approvers cannot be reached. It is a small, fixed, heavily monitored set of
  accounts whose *use* is the alarm.

The overlap rule is where the two families differ in practice. A vault that
holds one credential admits one holder at a time, which is where "no overlapping
windows" comes from for free. Elevation has no such limit: two people may hold
the same role at once, and the record of who did what is kept by identity
instead. The proposal wants both — no overlap, and more than one holder when
needed — which is a *pool* of accounts, and a pool is a vault with a checkout
queue. Saying which of the two is wanted is the first decision.

* Open questions

1. *Elevation or a separate account?* Elevation keeps one identity and makes
   attribution trivial, and it needs no password handling at all. A separate
   account needs a vault, a rotation policy and a checkout queue, and its
   attribution rests entirely on the checkout record. The proposal describes the
   second; the first is what most products now do.
2. *Who approves, and what if they cannot?* The approver for tenant
   administration is presumably another tenant administrator; for system
   administration, another super administrator. A single approver is a single
   point of failure, and the emergency path has to exist beside it.
3. *Does the mode follow the account or the session?* If elevation is on the
   person's own account, the session carries the elevated role and the shell
   reads its mode from that — which answers the open question about how the
   browser learns its mode without a second identity. If it is a separate
   account, the mode is simply what the account is, and the person's own account
   never changes.
4. *What happens at the end of the window?* The session must lose the mode
   without losing the work. A session that silently keeps a privileged token
   after its window closes is worse than no window at all.
5. *What is the audit surface?* The platform has a sign-in audit and versioned
   records. Whether a privileged session is its own recorded thing — a session
   log with the grant beside it — or whether the existing history tables carry
   it, is undecided.
6. *What does the reviewer see?* Whoever reviews privileged access periodically
   needs a list: who held what, when, on whose approval, and what they did. That
   list is a report, and no journey asks for it yet.

* Impact on the modes

The shell prototype records the mode as read from the sign-in and never chosen.
That holds under either family, but what "the account" means differs:

- Under elevation, the mode is a property of the *session's active roles*, and
  the shell reads it from the same place the server uses to authorise.
- Under a separate account, the mode is a property of the *account*, and the
  person is a different identity for the duration.

Either way the four modes, the menu each shows and the cards under them do not
change. What changes is where the browser reads the mode from, which is the one
thing the implementation still has to decide.
