#+title: Reference data classification by publication level
#+description: The rule that classifies a new kind of reference data as platform, world or tenant, and the tenant, the bundle and the write mechanism that follow from the classification.
#+type: knowledge

* Summary

ORE Studio does not read its reference data from the place where it is
authored. A set of staged rows, called a *dataset*, is written into a database
tenant by a *publish function*, and the datasets that belong together are
published in a single run called a *bundle*. Every dataset therefore has a
destination, and the destination is not a free choice: a classification assigns
it, and the same classification determines which tenant holds the rows, which
bundle carries them and which mechanism is permitted to write them. This page
states the classification. It recognises three *levels* — *platform*, *world*
and *tenant* — and assigns each kind of reference data to exactly one of them;
the level then settles the rest. The mechanisms the classification depends on
are cited rather than reproduced: the tiers are treated in
[[id:78E47817-A984-4325-AA73-B8B87C56BC3B][Data classification by substitutability]], the ownership
classes and their policies in
[[id:5A5D6490-AF64-4AAF-8B3D-48EF3EDAF3D2][Tenant ownership and the
information flow]], and the artefact and the publish function in
[[id:F19A6B98-1CAD-4206-913B-A4EAAED2DEAF][From an entity model to a published row]].

* Detail

** The need for a rule

Reference data reaches an installation from three kinds of source, and the
three differ in who owns the values rather than in what the values describe. A
vocabulary the platform defines, such as a coding scheme or a day-count
convention, has one correct set. A standard the platform adopts, such as the
ISO currency list, also has one correct set, and the platform records which
source it adopted. An entity such as a book or a set of instrument conventions
belongs to one tenant, and every valid instance of it is equally correct.

A datum held in more than one place admits more than one value as soon as one
copy is updated, and no reader can then be said to be authoritative. The
architecture therefore permits exactly one copy of a shared row and confines
the copy to a single tenant. The rule that follows is a direct consequence of
that constraint, and it is stated here in the order in which a developer
applies it.

** The classification

The level is the first of three questions that the data answers affirmatively.
The questions are ordered from the most general ownership to the most specific,
so that a kind of data with more than one plausible owner is assigned once and
not argued each time.

1. *Does the platform define these values in order to function?* The values
   belong to the *platform* level. A coding scheme, a template image, a market
   data provider, an adapter, a supplier or an ORE parameter has one correct
   set, and the platform is its author.
2. *Does the platform adopt them from an external source?* The values belong to
   the *world* level. ISO currencies and countries, the FpML vocabularies, the
   GLEIF records and the QuantLib calendars are adopted, and the source is
   recorded on the dataset.
3. *Does one tenant own them?* The values belong to the *tenant* level. The
   parties, conventions, books, portfolios and badges of a tenant are that
   tenant's, and no other tenant reads them.

Data that answers none of the three is produced by use, which is to say by a
trade, an activity or a measurement. Such data is never published, and the
write path that produces it is its only delivery.

The first two questions are those of
[[id:78E47817-A984-4325-AA73-B8B87C56BC3B][Data classification by substitutability]], posed from the
side of publication rather than from substitutability; the third is the tenant
ownership of
[[id:5A5D6490-AF64-4AAF-8B3D-48EF3EDAF3D2][Tenant ownership and the
information flow]]. The three levels and the three ownership classes of the
row-level security model describe the same partition, and a page that states
one without the other leaves the reader to infer the other.

** The destination of each level

The level and, within the tenant level, the scope name the bundle, and the
bundle names the tenant that holds the rows. One table carries the whole map.

| Bundle | Level | Scope | Held in | Published | The values it carries |
|--------+-------+-------+---------+-----------+-----------------------|
| =system_core= | Platform | Platform | The system tenant | At database bring-up | The coding schemes, the template images, the provider, adapter and supplier catalogues, the system tenant's feeds, the ORE parameters and their value domains, the FX vol surface blueprints |
| =world= | World | World | The system tenant | At database bring-up | ISO and FpML vocabularies, calendars and their exceptions and events, currency pairs and their conventions, currencies, commodities, equity underlyings |
| =tenant_defaults= | Tenant | Tenant-wide | The tenant | When the tenant is created | The badges, the badge mappings and the code domains a tenant starts with |
| =party_essentials= | Tenant | Party | The party's tenant | When the party is onboarded | The party's instrument conventions and its CRM topology |
| =party_reporting= | Tenant | Party | The party's tenant | When the party is onboarded | The party's report definitions |
| =ore_samples= | Tenant | Party | The party's tenant | When the party is onboarded | The ORE samples: netting agreements, netting sets, CSAs, aliases, portfolios |
| =risk_management= | Tenant | Party | The party's tenant | When the party is onboarded | The business units, portfolios and books, and the ORE report definitions, of a party that runs reports |
| =crm_topology= | Tenant | Party | The party's tenant | When the party is onboarded | The CRM topology on its own |
| =lei_hierarchy= | Tenant | Tenant-wide | The tenant | When the tenant asks for it | The GLEIF hierarchy imported as the tenant's parties, and the legal-entity catalogue it derives them from |
| =lei_counterparties= | Tenant | Tenant-wide | The tenant | When the tenant asks for it | The GLEIF records published as the tenant's counterparties, and the aliases that name them |
| =crypto= | World | World | The system tenant | When the installation asks for it | The cryptocurrency slice, published beside the currencies |

A demonstration carries bundles of its own: =acme_group=, =acme_uk=, =acme_us=,
=acme_hk=, =acme_lei_import=, =northwind_uk=, =northwind_lei_import=, and the two
=marketdata.reference_vintage_*= bundles. Each lays out one demo tenant's books,
portfolios and market data. They take the same levels as the rows above — a
tenant bundle with party scope, or a tenant bundle with tenant-wide scope — and
they carry no platform row and no world row. They are left out of the table
because they name a demonstration and not a kind of reference data, and the
classification reaches them like any other dataset.

** Two properties of the classification

The first property is that no tenant holds a copy of a platform row or a world
row. The rows exist in the system tenant, and a tenant reads them where they
lie: the table's policy takes the shared-reference shape,
~tenant_id = current or tenant_id = system~, and the entity's repository
declares =:system_tenant_visible:=, whose reads admit the system tenant's rows
beside the caller's own. The policy is a select policy of its own, because a
single for-all policy that admitted the system tenant would let a tenant change
or remove a shared row. This is what allows a database to be usable before any
tenant has been provisioned, and it is what removes the class of defect in
which two copies of a fact diverge. The world catalogue carries the same read:
the calendars, the countries, the currencies, the currency pairs and their
conventions, the commodities, the equity underlyings and the FpML vocabularies
are read from the system tenant, and a tenant publishes none of them.
[[id:0EAAEDFD-7954-4649-A463-9EE377B023F7][Data read across tenants]] holds
the mechanism and the checklist for a new shared table; the write side is
governed by permission rather than by a further policy shape, because a tenant
must hold no write path into a table that carries a shared row.

The second property is that a record and the row derived from it may belong to
different levels. The GLEIF import is the case that establishes it: the LEI
record is a world fact and is held in the system tenant, whereas the party a
tenant creates from that record is the tenant's own row. The dataset that
writes parties is therefore a tenant dataset, whatever it reads.

The deployment's own registers — the services roster, the hosts and wrapper
nodes, the grid and node samples, the telemetry logs, and the applications the
grid runs — belong to the installation and not to any level of this rule. A
service writes them as it observes them, and no bundle publishes them. The
catalogue of applications is the exception a tenant reads: its rows are shared
from the system tenant by the same read every other shared row takes, because a
tenant has to know which application it may ask the grid to run. A reader
searching for the dataset that publishes a measurement is searching for
something the model does not contain.

** The bundle map

A bundle carries one level and one scope, and the two determine its target
tenant and the parameters the publication passes. The bundles that carry one
dataset therefore share its level and its scope: =party_essentials= and
=risk_management= both publish into the tenant that holds the party, so the two
may both list the report definitions and the reader cannot tell which bundle
placed a row. A dataset may not be a member of two bundles that differ in level
or in scope, and the datasets a bundle reaches through a dependency edge are
not members at all. The instrument conventions are the one dataset the map
doubles: =system_core= publishes the system party's copy and =party_essentials=
publishes each other party's, because the system tenant's derived source reads
its own party's conventions and no tenant reads another party's. A bundle is a
run and not a transaction: each step commits its own function call, and a step
that fails halts the run without undoing the steps that preceded it. The guarantee a bundle gives, and the guarantee it does
not give, is the subject of
[[id:BA57E2D2-553B-4A06-9295-E792D1E29487][Data classification by owner
count]], at B14.

** The mechanism that writes a level

The level also determines the mechanism, and the choice is not free.

- A table that a person manages — one that is previewed, republished or
  versioned — is written by a *publish function*, and its rows arrive as a
  dataset.
- A fixed vocabulary that the platform ships is written by a *populate script*,
  and every tenant reads its rows through the shared-reference read.
- A given row in a given tenant has exactly one mechanism that writes it, and a
  table may therefore carry the rows of two. One publish function may serve two
  datasets that fill one table, as the FpML and ORE asset-class datasets do;
  each row nevertheless belongs to one dataset.
- A row written by a populate script belongs to the platform and is not a
  published row, although every tenant reads it. The system party is of this
  kind, and so is the =WRLD= business centre: the platform defines it, the
  foundation layer's system party names it before any dataset can publish, and
  the world dataset publishes the other business centres beside it.

Three practices are excluded by the rule. A populate script may not write a row
that a publish function also writes, in the same tenant. A platform or world row
may not be published into a tenant. A dataset may not be a member of two bundles
that differ in level or in scope.

** The path a publication takes

#+caption: The path: from a bundle request to a published row.
#+attr_html: :width 100%
[[proj:doc/knowledge/architecture/reference_data_path.png]]

The publish function is the only mechanism that writes a published row, and it
has two callers: the bundle publication at runtime, and the bootstrap at
database bring-up. The function reads the dataset's artefact rows, which are
held in the system tenant, and writes the owning tables in the target tenant.
It returns the four counts — inserted, updated, skipped and deleted — that the
caller reads.
[[id:F19A6B98-1CAD-4206-913B-A4EAAED2DEAF][From an entity model to a published row]] holds the function's
contract, the artefact table, the artefact type and the steps by which one is
added.

#+caption: One bundle publish: dependency order, one function per dataset.
#+attr_html: :width 100%
[[proj:doc/knowledge/architecture/reference_data_sequence.png]]

The request names a bundle and not a target tenant. The bundle's level
determines the target, and a caller whose tenant the level does not admit is
refused; without that rule a tenant administrator could publish a world bundle
into their own tenant and recreate by request the second copy the model forbids.
The order of the steps is the dependency closure's and not the display order of
the bundle members, and a dataset with no target subject is dropped from the
run.

Two callers publish without the bus, and they differ in the target alone. The
bring-up publishes =system_core= and =world= into the system tenant, and the
creation of a tenant publishes =tenant_defaults= into that tenant. Both call the
same function, which takes the target tenant as an argument, and both read the
dataset from the system tenant's one copy, so no tenant needs a dataset row of
its own in order to publish. No other caller publishes without the bus, and no
tenant's creation publishes a world dataset: the tenant reads the world
catalogue where the bring-up placed it.

A world row therefore does not exist until the bring-up has published it. The
populate run stages artefact rows and publishes last, so a script that runs
earlier may not read a world table: a seed that needs one derives it from
another dataset's artefact rows, as the currency calendars derive theirs from
the currency countries, and states the dependency edge that orders the two
publishes.

** Provenance

A published row records the dataset that produced it, and it records it by name.
The audit tail is the one every reference-data entity carries, which
[[id:A19D2750-D97D-09A4-BD2B-5218D9654E56][Data quality]] states and this page
does not repeat; publication adds to it a commentary of the form =Imported from
DQ dataset: <name>= and the reason code =system.external_data_import=.

The chain proceeds from the row to the dataset by that name, from the dataset
row to its artefact rows by the dataset id, and from the artefact type to the
publish function, the target table and the subject. Because the published row
carries the name and not the id, a rename breaks the chain whereas a database
recreate does not.

** Reproducibility

A database recreate produces the schema, the system tenant, the staged
artefacts and the published platform and world catalogues, and it produces them
from the tree alone. A test therefore begins on a database that holds the
calendars, the currencies, the conventions, the commodities and the underlyings,
with no tenant provisioned and no trade booked. The procedure is the subject of
[[id:4342B077-85EA-4C6A-9BDF-D38560C80B37][How do I recreate the database?]].

Three properties are not reproduced by a recreate. The dataset identifier is
minted by =ores_dq_datasets_upsert_fn= with =gen_random_uuid()= against a
natural key of name, subject area and domain, so the same datasets return with
different identifiers. The publication history is held by no table, and
therefore cannot be reproduced. The row versions return to one, because a
recreate writes every row once.

An operator establishes the state of a catalogue with two checks. The functions
=ores_dq_bundle_datasets_list_fn= and =ores_dq_bundle_preview_fn= report whether
a bundle is complete: whether every member has a dataset and a subject, and
whether any member is missing. A re-run of the bundle then reports what it
wrote, and the counts are read against the function that produced them: a
function that compares a staged row with the row it holds returns =skipped= for
a catalogue that agrees, and one that rewrites its rows returns =updated=. The
tree holds both kinds, so a zero =inserted= count is the evidence and a zero
=updated= count is not.

Bring-up concludes with the system tenant, which holds the platform and world
catalogues. Creating a tenant publishes =tenant_defaults= into it and nothing
else; an operator continues by onboarding each party with =party_essentials=,
and a tenant that trades against GLEIF records publishes =lei_counterparties=
with the rungs it wants. Market data and trades follow, and neither is
published.
