* Summary

A system provisioned today has entities, books and portfolios but no trades.
This document argues that trade generation should be built as a *block* the
domain exposes, rather than as a seeding script, and sets out the candidate
approaches with what is known and unknown about each.

Three claims are advanced. Generation belongs behind a request carrying a
configuration, because the same block then serves provisioning, a periodic feed
and amendment without becoming three mechanisms. Sizes are presets of that
configuration rather than constants, because a load test at arbitrary volume
must be a configuration change and not a code change. And realism is a property
of the configuration rather than of the row count, because a small book differs
from a large one in what it trades and not only in how much.

The choice of generation mechanism is left open. Four candidates are described
with the evidence available; the measurements that would settle it are named,
and they have not been taken.

* Detail

** The Problem

Provisioning produces a plausible institution with no positions. Acme has 36
portfolios with owner units, aggregation currencies and a parent hierarchy, and
nothing in them.

That absence blocks work of three different shapes. An integration test
asserting on aggregation has nothing to aggregate. A demonstration of risk or
profit and loss has nothing to price. And query performance cannot be measured,
because performance questions appear only at volumes nobody produces by hand.

Those three wants differ in what they need, which is the first finding of this
document. They are not one need at three magnifications.

** What Already Exists

The scoping that preceded this document found more built than expected, and it
narrows the problem substantially.

*** Generation is already a service capability

=ores.synthetic= is a full service, and it already answers generation requests.
=generate_organisation_request= is handled on subject
=synthetic.v1.organisation.generate=, carrying a typed configuration.

Beside it sit persisted configuration entities: =feed_config=,
=market_data_generation_config=, =fx_spot_generation_config= and
=ir_curve_generation_config=. Feeds are configured, not coded.

A trade generation configuration is the missing sibling. This is the strongest
argument in the document, and it argues from precedent rather than from
principle: the mechanism the story needs is one this project already operates
for market data, and building trades any other way would create a second pattern
for the same job.

*** ORE import already exists

=ores.ore= carries =trade_mapper= with per-asset-class mappers for swap, equity,
FX, commodity, bond and credit, plus an XML importer and exporter.

The consequence is that how ORE trades get in is largely answered, and the open
question is generation and volume rather than import. It also means the importer
is available as a *check* on generated output, independently of whether it is
used to load it.

*** Seeding is Python and file-based

=projects/ores.seeder/= is Python: =src/seeder.py= with datasets beside it, each
carrying JSON and a =generate_sql.py=. The Acme dataset holds companies, books,
portfolios and LEI relationships this way.

The pattern works at the scale it currently runs. Whether it extends to trade
volumes is one of the open questions below.

** What a Valid Trade Requires

ORE's Python =TradeGenerator= is worth reading whether or not it is adopted,
because it encodes what a trade needs and where each part comes from. Its lesson
is the lookup.

A trade is built from an index such as =GBP-SONIA=. That index selects a
convention and a curve config, and those between them supply payment frequency,
payment calendars, business day conventions and spot lags. The caller supplies
only notional, maturity, fixed rate and direction.

The distinction that matters is therefore between *market structure*, which is
looked up and must be right, and *trade particulars*, which are chosen and can
be varied freely. A generator that gets the second right and the first wrong
produces trades that look plausible and do not price.

That distinction survives whichever approach is chosen, which is why it is
recorded here rather than inside one option.

** The Candidates

Four approaches, with what is known. None has been measured, and the
measurements that would separate them are named under /Open Questions/.

#+ATTR_HTML: :class hug-leading
| Approach | Mechanism | Known strength | Known risk |
|----------+-----------+----------------+------------|
| Committed dataset | Generate once, commit the SQL, seeder loads it | Reviewable; identical for everyone; no generation cost at provision | A large artefact in git, growing with the largest preset |
| Algorithmic generation | A generator writes through the trade service at provision time, sized by parameters and seeded | No large artefact; volume is a parameter; presets are data | New code, and correctness rests on it rather than on ORE |
| ORE-Python | Build trades through ORE's own API, emit XML, load through =ores.ore= | Market structure is ORE's own, so conventions are right by construction | A SWIG dependency in the seeding path; per-trade cost unmeasured |
| Archetypes multiplied | ORE fixes conventions for a small set of archetypes; multiplying across particulars is ours | ORE correctness where it matters, our speed where it does not | Two mechanisms to maintain; archetype coverage bounds instrument coverage |

They are not exclusive. A committed dataset for the small presets and
algorithmic generation for the large ones is a fifth option, and it matches the
three wants in /The Problem/ most directly, since the small presets are the ones
that benefit from being reviewable and identical for everyone.

** Sizes as Presets

A size is a named bundle of configuration parameters, not a constant.

The parameters a preset sets are the same ones a caller may supply. That single
property is what lets =t0= serve an integration test and a load test at ten
million trades be a configuration rather than a code change, and it is why the
sizes are described here as presets rather than as tiers.

#+ATTR_HTML: :class hug-leading
| Preset | Purpose | Shape |
|--------+---------+-------|
| t0 tiny | An integration test asserts against it | Few enough trades to read; one or two asset classes; a handful of counterparties |
| t1 medium | Demonstrations, and work needing breadth | Every asset class represented; realistic counterparty spread |
| t2 large | A realistic institutional book | Full breadth, and volume enough to make aggregation non-trivial |
| t3 extra large | Load and query performance | Volume chosen for the test rather than for realism of size |

The row counts are deliberately absent. They belong in the presets, where they
can be changed, rather than in a design document, where they would harden.

** Realism

Realism is the requirement most easily lost, because the obvious implementation
of smaller is to generate fewer rows of the same thing.

A small firm's book is not a large firm's book sampled. It trades fewer asset
classes, faces fewer counterparties, and has a narrower maturity spread. A tiny
population produced by uniform sampling from a large one would hold one trade in
each of a dozen asset classes, which no small firm's book looks like.

Realism is therefore a property of the *configuration*, and the criteria are per
preset. Each preset asserts a distribution across asset class, currency,
maturity, notional and counterparty, and states what would count as wrong. Those
assertions are checkable, which keeps the requirement from becoming a matter of
taste.

Portfolio assignment is the same argument applied to placement. Acme's
portfolios carry an owner unit and an aggregation currency, and a realistic
population respects both: a treasury portfolio aggregating in GBP does not
plausibly hold what an equity desk's book holds. Round-robin assignment would
satisfy the row count and fail the requirement.

** What Measurement Found

Three of the open questions are now answered, two by reading the code and one by
measuring.

*** The trade interface batches

Every instrument repository exposes both a single and a vector write. The vector
form is not a loop: it maps the whole vector to entities and hands them to
=execute_write_query=, which opens one session, begins one transaction, issues
one =sqlgen::insert=, and commits. A signature is a proxy for this, so the
implementation was read rather than assumed.

The consequence is that the service boundary costs less than feared. Generation
in =ores.synthetic= calling =ores.trading= downstream does not imply a call per
trade.

*** A per-row trigger costs a factor of four

The generated schema attaches a notify trigger to each entity table, declared
=for each row=, whose function calls =pg_notify= with a JSON payload. Per row.

=scripts/bench_trade_insert.py= measures what that costs. It builds its own
table, with and without an equivalent trigger, times =COPY= against multi-row
=INSERT= at several volumes, checks the row count after every load, and drops
what it made. It reaches postgres through =psql=, the way the rest of the
project does, so it adds no dependency.

Medians over three runs, one run at a million, against the development database:

| Trades  | Method | Trigger | Seconds | Trades/sec |
|---------+--------+---------+---------+------------|
|    1000 | COPY   | on      |   0.046 |      21584 |
|    1000 | COPY   | off     |   0.043 |      23503 |
|   10000 | COPY   | on      |   0.098 |     102432 |
|   10000 | COPY   | off     |   0.055 |     180591 |
|  100000 | COPY   | on      |   0.602 |     166179 |
|  100000 | COPY   | off     |   0.162 |     617993 |
|  100000 | INSERT | off     |   0.756 |     132209 |
| 1000000 | COPY   | on      |   6.615 |     151161 |
| 1000000 | COPY   | off     |   1.575 |     634833 |
| 1000000 | INSERT | on      |  14.868 |      67259 |
| 1000000 | INSERT | off     |   7.467 |     133918 |

Two factors of about four fall out, and they are independent. The trigger costs
4.2x on the =COPY= path at a million rows. =COPY= beats multi-row =INSERT= by
4.7x with the trigger off.

The measurement corrects the concern that prompted it. A million trades load in
1.6 seconds without the trigger and 6.6 seconds with it. At the volumes this
story targets the trigger is affordable, so *suppressing it is an optimisation
and not a precondition*. The design does not need to route bulk population
around the trigger to be viable.

*** The notify queue does not exhaust at these volumes

=pg_notify= has a queue limit, which makes exhaustion under bulk load a
reasonable thing to fear. A million trades inserted in one transaction with the
trigger on did not reach it. The limit is real and does not bite here.

One caveat the measurement does not cover: it ran with nothing listening.
Notifications are delivered at commit, and a real listener consuming a million
payloads is a different question from postgres queuing them. That is a question
about the consumer, not about the insert path this section measures.

*** What was not measured

The benchmark uses a representative flat table rather than the real bitemporal
trade schema, so it bounds the trigger and the load method rather than
predicting absolute times for a real population. The service boundary cost is
also unmeasured; the code read above shows the vector write batches, which is
what the boundary needed.

** The Service Boundary

=ores.synthetic= owns generation: the configurations, the algorithms, the seed.
=ores.trading= owns trades.

The generator therefore calls downstream rather than writing to the trade
tables. Generation gains no second path into them, and the rules governing a
trade stay in one place.

That boundary is affordable, and both halves of the question are now settled.
The downstream interface admits batching, because the vector write issues one
=sqlgen::insert= in one transaction. The multi-row =INSERT= path it uses loads a
million trades in 7.5 seconds with the trigger off and 14.9 seconds with it on.

The consequence is that *the design needs no bulk path of its own*. Generation in
=ores.synthetic= calling =ores.trading= downstream, over the interface that
already exists and through the trigger that already fires, reaches a million
trades in about fifteen seconds. =COPY= is 4.7x faster and remains available if a
preset ever outgrows that, but nothing in the target volumes requires it.

** Compositions

Three uses of one block, of which only the first is needed now.

*Provisioning* sends the request once, with a preset naming the size.

*A periodic feed* sends the same request on a schedule. =ores.synthetic= already
carries =feed_config= for market data, so this composition needs a configuration
rather than a mechanism.

*Amendment* sends the request in an update mode, so an existing population
evolves rather than being replaced. Nothing here builds it; the design is
required to admit it without change to the block.

* Open Questions

These are the measurements and decisions the analysis task owes, and this
document deliberately does not pre-empt them.

- How does cost scale with the number of trades, and where does each approach
  break? Measured at more than one volume, so the shape of the curve is known
  and not one point.
- What does bulk insert cost with and without the per-row notify trigger, and at
  what volume does the notification queue become the binding constraint?
  =scripts/bench_trade_insert.py= answers this; it needs a running environment.
- What does ORE-Python cost per trade, and what does it drag into the seeding
  path? The dependency footprint matters as much as the speed.
- Which =ores.trading= instruments can each approach populate? Measured per
  asset class, with any instrument neither side carries named.
- Can each approach produce a realistic small book, or only a truncated large
  one?
- Do the small presets benefit enough from being committed to justify
  maintaining two mechanisms?
