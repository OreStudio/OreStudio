#+title: Investigation: Trade model defects
#+description: Modelling defects in the trading domain entities, why each is wrong, and the shape that replaces it.
#+type: investigation
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :ore:trading:modelling:investigation:sprint_25:v0:
#+created: 2026-09-08
#+updated: 2026-09-08

This page documents an [[id:A7F8D9E2-3C4B-4A5D-B67C-8E9F0A1B2C3D][investigation]] into the trading domain model. It records six defects found in the entities, why each is wrong, and the shape that replaces it.

* Context

Benchmarking trade population exposed a defect the benchmark was not looking for.
A callable swap loads four times slower than a vanilla swap on the fastest write
path, although the two are identical in tables, rows and fan-out. The cause is
=call_dates_json=, a text column holding the exercise schedule.

That column is not an isolated choice. It is one instance of a pattern, and
looking for the pattern found five more. This page records what the trading
entities model badly, why each choice is wrong, and the shape that replaces it.

The performance finding is what prompted the review. It is not the main result.
Most of the defects below cost correctness or integrity rather than time, and
those are the more expensive kind.

* Methodology

The 41 entity headers under
=projects/ores.trading/core/include/ores.trading.core/repository= were parsed for
field name and declared type, and the fields grouped by pattern. Counts below are
from that scan and are reproducible against the headers.

Three of the defects were also measured. =scripts/bench_trade_shapes.py= loads
populations of each instrument shape, which separated the cost of the JSON column
from the cost of the instrument's complexity.
=scripts/load_trade_population.py= fills every column of all 41 deployed tables
and times each, which attributed the insert cost between the triggers and the
bitemporal exclusion constraint.

The loader also found defects by failing. Where the schema rejected a fully
populated row, the rejection named the rule that no declaration carries, and
defects 8 and 9 are those rejections read as findings.

Defects 2 and 3 were raised from the C++ headers and then checked against the
deployed SQL, which corrected them. Both are recorded with what the check
found.

* Defects

** Defect 1: schedules and collections held as JSON text

*Current shape.* Eight columns across six instruments hold a collection as a JSON
string: =call_dates_json=, =knock_out_dates_json=, =basket_json= in two
instruments, =option_data_json=, and =events_json=, =underlyings_json= and
=parameters_json= in =scripted_instrument=. The declared type is
=std::optional<std::string>=.

*Why it is wrong.* Four reasons, in ascending order of cost.

It is slow, and this one is measured. A callable swap population loads in 4.67
seconds against a vanilla swap's 1.14 at a hundred thousand trades, on the path
the design intends to adopt. Row width is the whole difference.

It is opaque to the database. A query for every trade with a call date in the
next quarter cannot use an index, because the dates are inside a string. The
schedule is data the system holds and cannot ask about.

It cannot be constrained. Nothing stops a malformed date, a duplicate entry or an
unsorted schedule, because the column's type is text and its content is a
convention.

It hides a shared concept. A call schedule, a knock-out schedule and a fixing
schedule are the same structure carrying different meanings. Modelling each as a
private string in its own instrument means the system has no notion of a schedule
at all, and every consumer parses its own.

*Replacement shape.* One schedule table shared by every instrument that needs
one, and a second table for its dates.

#+begin_src sql
ores_trading_schedules_tbl
  schedule_id          text primary key
  tenant_id            text not null
  instrument_id        text not null
  schedule_type_code   text not null   -- call, knock_out, fixing, payment
  business_day_convention_code  text
  calendar_code        text

ores_trading_schedule_dates_tbl
  schedule_id          text not null
  sequence_number      int  not null
  schedule_date        date not null
  primary key (schedule_id, sequence_number)
#+end_src

The discriminator is =schedule_type_code=, so one structure serves the call
schedule, the knock-out schedule and any later one, and a new schedule kind is a
reference row rather than a new column.

This trades row count for width, which the measurement says is the right
direction: the fan-out cost of extra rows is 1.4x between the widest and
narrowest shapes measured, and the width cost of one JSON column is 4x.

*The exception.* =scripted_instrument= is not the same case. Its =events_json=,
=underlyings_json= and =parameters_json= hold a user-supplied script whose shape
is not fixed by the domain, so no table can be drawn for it. The correct repair
there is narrower: hold it as =jsonb= rather than =text=, so the database can at
least validate and index it. A genuinely dynamic payload is the one place a
document column is right.

** Defect 2: dates held as text

*Current shape.* 58 date-valued fields across the instruments are declared
=std::string=, including =start_date=, =maturity_date=, =expiry_date=,
=issue_date= and =call_date=.

*Why it is wrong.* A text column admits =2026-13-45= and =next Tuesday=. It sorts
lexically, which is correct for ISO-8601 and silently wrong for any other format,
so correctness rests on every writer agreeing on a convention that nothing
enforces. Date arithmetic, the operation a trading system performs most, must
happen outside the database or through a cast on every query, and a cast defeats
the index.

The maturity-bucket queries a realistic population needs are exactly the queries
this shape makes expensive.

*Replacement shape.* The =date= type, with =timestamptz= where a time of day is
genuinely carried. Postgres stores a =date= in four bytes against ten or more for
the text, so the change also narrows every instrument row.

*Resolved against the deployed schema.* The SQL is correct. The deployed columns
are =date=, and 56 of them are. The defect is therefore narrower than the scan
suggested: the C++ declaration widens a =date= to a string on the way out of the
database, so the type information is lost at the boundary rather than in storage.

That makes this a mapper defect, not a schema defect, and it is cheaper than the
count implies.

** Defect 3: monetary amounts held as double

*Current shape.* 18 fields carrying a price, strike, premium, payout or notional
are declared =double=, among them =option_strike=, =forward_price=,
=payout_amount=, =settlement_notional= and =accumulation_amount=.

*Why it is wrong.* Binary floating point cannot represent most decimal amounts
exactly. Summing a portfolio's notionals accumulates error, and two systems
summing in different orders disagree. For a book that must reconcile, this is a
correctness defect rather than a precision preference.

*Replacement shape.* =numeric= in the database and a decimal type in the domain,
for any field denominated in a currency.

*Where double stays.* Rates, volatilities and correlations are not monetary
amounts. They are inputs to analytics that are themselves floating point, and
=numeric= would cost performance to buy a precision the mathematics does not
have. The rule is the denomination, not the field's apparent numeric nature: if
it has a currency, it is =numeric=.

*Resolved against the deployed schema.* The SQL is again correct: 72 columns are
=numeric= and only two are =double precision=. As with defect 2, the loss happens
in the C++ declaration rather than in the database, so this is a mapper defect.

** Defect 4: enumerations held as free text with no reference table

*Current shape.* Fields whose values are a closed set are declared =std::string=
with no backing table and no =_code= suffix. =long_short= appears in 14 entities,
=option_type= in 10, =exercise_type= in 5, =barrier_type= in 5 and
=settlement_type= in 3, with a further fifteen appearing once or twice.

*Why it is wrong.* Nothing constrains the value, so =Long=, =long=, =L= and =BUY=
can all be present, and every consumer must defend against all of them. The valid
set is not written down anywhere a person or a query can read it, so answering
"what exercise types do we support" means grepping the source.

The schema already establishes the remedy. =trade_type=, =party_role_type=,
=activity_type=, =trade_id_type= and =fpml_event_type= are reference tables with
foreign keys. These fields are the same kind of thing and did not get the same
treatment, so *the inconsistency is internal to the model rather than a matter of
taste*.

*Replacement shape.* A reference table per enumeration, following the tables that
already exist, and the field renamed to =_code= with a foreign key.

#+begin_src sql
ores_trading_long_short_types_tbl
  code          text primary key   -- long, short
  display_name  text not null
  description   text
#+end_src

Where an enumeration is genuinely two-valued and will not grow, a check
constraint is the cheaper answer and is still an improvement on nothing. The test
is whether the set can grow: =long_short= cannot, =option_type= and
=exercise_type= can.

** Defect 5: code fields whose reference table does not exist

*Current shape.* The =_code= suffix denotes a foreign key into a reference table.
For several fields that table was never created. =change_reason_code= carries the
convention in all 41 entities, =day_count_code= in three,
=payment_frequency_code= in three, and =currency= throughout. Reference tables
exist for only five of the codes in use.

*Why it is wrong.* This defect is worse than defect 4, because the convention
promises the constraint. A reader who sees =_code= is entitled to conclude the
value is constrained by a table, and for most of these it is not. A naming
convention that holds four times in five teaches the wrong thing.

Currency is the sharpest case. It appears throughout the model, it has an
international standard defining the valid set, and it has no table.

*Replacement shape.* The missing reference tables, populated from the standard
where one exists, and the foreign keys declared.

| Field                  | Reference table                        | Source of values |
|------------------------+----------------------------------------+------------------|
| =currency=               | =ores_trading_currencies_tbl=            | ISO 4217         |
| =day_count_code=         | =ores_trading_day_count_conventions_tbl= | ISDA definitions |
| =payment_frequency_code= | =ores_trading_payment_frequencies_tbl=   | ISDA definitions |
| =change_reason_code=     | =ores_trading_change_reasons_tbl=        | This project     |

Until a table exists, the honest repair is to drop the =_code= suffix, so the
name stops claiming a constraint the model does not enforce.

** Defect 6: one concept under two names

*Current shape.* The day count convention is =day_count_code= in three entities
and =day_count_fraction_code= in =swap_leg=. Payment frequency is
=payment_frequency_code= in three entities and =payment_frequency= in one.

*Why it is wrong.* Two names for one concept defeat the search that would find
every use, so a change to the concept misses a site. It also blocks the repair in
defect 5, because a foreign key needs one column name to point at one table.

This is the cheapest defect to fix and the one that compounds fastest, since every
new instrument copies whichever name it happened to see.

*Replacement shape.* One name per concept, chosen once and applied everywhere.
=day_count_fraction_code= is the term the ISDA definitions use, so it should be
the survivor, and =payment_frequency_code= keeps the suffix the convention
requires.

** Defect 7: referential integrity implemented in PL/pgSQL

*Current shape.* The 41 trading tables carry 866 check constraints, 41 primary
keys and *no foreign keys at all*. Referential integrity is instead enforced by
=insert= triggers that query the parent table and raise. The trigger source calls
these soft foreign keys in its own comments.

*Why it is wrong.* This is the population loader's largest finding, and it is a
performance defect rather than a correctness one.

A foreign key is enforced by an index probe the planner controls. A trigger is a
PL/pgSQL function invoked per row, which runs a query per reference and raises on
failure.

Measured on =ores_trading_vanilla_swap_instruments_tbl=:

| Configuration                                    | Rows/sec |
|--------------------------------------------------+----------|
| Deployed table                                   |     2200 |
| Deployed table, notify trigger disabled          |     2234 |
| Clone with the exclusion constraint, no triggers |    56032 |
| Clone with neither                               |   121218 |

The insert trigger accounts for a factor of about 25. The bitemporal exclusion
constraint costs 2.2x, and the notify trigger costs 1.5%, which is within the
noise of the measurement.

A second observation supports the attribution. Across the 36 tables the loader
populates, throughput is close to 2000 rows per second whatever the table, and
column counts range from 10 to 40. *Row width does not predict cost here, because
a per-row procedure dominates whatever the row contains.* This is the opposite of
what the synthetic benchmark found, where width was the largest factor, and the
difference is that the synthetic tables carried no triggers.

The database also cannot reason about a relationship it is not told about. No
cascade, no deferral, and no plan that exploits the reference.

*Replacement shape.* Declared foreign keys wherever the reference is to a table
that exists, leaving triggers only for the checks a foreign key cannot express,
such as tenancy scoping. The bulk population path is the immediate beneficiary,
since a set-based load can defer or drop constraints in a way it cannot defer a
per-row trigger.

*What must not be lost.* These triggers do more than check references. They
denormalise fields and maintain the bitemporal validity range that the exclusion
constraint enforces, so they cannot simply be removed. Separating the validation
from the versioning is the first step, and only the validation half is replaceable
by a foreign key.

** Defect 8: enumerations enforced in PL/pgSQL

*Current shape.* Loading every table surfaced 27 enumerations that no check
constraint and no foreign key declares. They live inside trigger functions, which
raise with the valid set in the message, for example =trade_type= against 112
values, =leg_type= against ten and =payment_frequency= against nine.

*Why it is wrong.* This is defects 4 and 5 in a third form, and the worst of the
three. The valid set exists, and it is written in a procedure rather than in the
schema. Nothing can query it, no tool can generate from it, and a reader
discovers it by triggering a failure.

=ores_trading_trade_types_tbl= holds 112 rows and =trades.trade_type= is =text=
with no foreign key to it. The reference table and the enforcement both exist,
and they are not connected.

*Replacement shape.* Declare the foreign key to the reference table that already
holds the values, and delete the validation from the trigger. Where no reference
table exists, defect 5's remedy applies first.

*Evidence.* =scripts/load_trade_population.py= discovers these by loading a row,
reading the rejection, and retrying with what the message named. Its output lists
the 27 it learned, which is the current inventory.

** Defect 9: one table holding two products

*Current shape.* Five equity instrument tables carry a check constraint that
splits the table into two products. The discriminator is =trade_type_code=, and
each branch names the columns that product uses and the columns belonging to the
other product, which must be null.

| Table                 | Product A            | A requires present         | A requires null            |
|-----------------------+----------------------+----------------------------+----------------------------|
| =equity_option=         | EquityCliquetOption  | =cliquet_frequency=          | nothing                    |
| =equity_accumulator=    | EquityTaRF           | =target_amount=, =target_type= | nothing                    |
| =equity_position=       | EquityOptionPosition | =option_data_json=           | nothing                    |
| =equity_digital_option= | EquityDigitalOption  | =option_type=, =strike=        | =barrier_level=, =barrier_type= |
| =equity_swap=           | EquityWorstOfBasketSwap | =basket_json=             | =underlying_name=            |

The first three are asymmetric: one product adds columns the other omits. The
last two are a true exclusive or, where each product forbids the other's columns.

*Why it is wrong.* The table's columns are not all meaningful for all its rows,
so a reader must know the discriminator before it can interpret a row, and a
query over =strike= silently means different things depending on a value in
another column.

The constraint expresses in SQL what a type would express in structure. The
schema already models 25 other instruments as a table each, so these five are
modelled differently from the rest with no stated reason. That inconsistency is
the defect more than the technique is.

*How to address it.* Three options, in ascending cost.

1. *A table per product*, matching what the schema does for every other
   instrument. This is the option consistent with the rest of the model and it
   removes the constraint entirely. It costs a migration and a repository per new
   table.
2. *Keep the table and make the grouping explicit*, by naming the optional
   column groups in the entity model so the generator emits both the constraint
   and a typed accessor that cannot read =strike= on a touch option. This keeps
   the storage and moves the discipline into the generated code.
3. *Leave it and document it*, which is the current position by default rather
   than by decision.

The first is preferable for the two true exclusive-or tables, where the columns
are genuinely disjoint and the row shapes have nothing in common. The asymmetric
three are a weaker case: an optional column that only one product uses is
ordinary, and only the constraint's phrasing makes it look like two products.

*Consequence for population.* No row in the two exclusive-or tables can carry
every column, whichever product is chosen. A loader must pick a product and honour
that product's nulls. This is not a limitation to work around; it is the model
saying the table holds two disjoint row shapes.

** Defect 10: product type names an asset class

*Current shape.* =product_type_t= is an enum on =ores_trading_trades_tbl= with
eight values: =swap=, =fx=, =bond=, =credit=, =equity=, =commodity=, =composite=
and =scripted=.

*Why it is wrong.* Those are asset classes, and the column is called product
type. The name is taken by a coarser concept than the one a desk means by
product, which blocks the term for the classification that actually needs it.

*Replacement shape.* Rename the column and the type to =asset_class=, which is
what it holds, and leave =product_type= free.

** Defect 11: staged reference data is never published

*Current shape.* The cause of the above was not missing data. Acme's parties,
business units, portfolios and books are all staged in the DQ artefact tables,
and =ores.sql= carries 30 =ores_refdata_publish_*_from_dq_fn= functions that
promote them into the refdata tables the rest of the system reads.

*Nothing called them.* Every mention of a publish function in =populate/= was a
comment. A freshly provisioned database therefore held 53 staged books and zero
published ones, which is why no trade could be booked.

*Why it is wrong.* The gap is silent and it fails far from its cause. The
provisioning reports success, and the failure appears later as a rejected trade
naming a book id, which says nothing about an unpublished dataset.

The mechanism was designed to be driven at runtime by =ores.iam.core='s Acme
provisioner over =dq.v1.bundles.publish=. That is a reasonable design, and it
leaves a database unusable until a service has run, which the SQL provisioning
does not say.

*Repair applied.* =populate/acme/acme_publish_populate.sql= calls the same
publish functions in dependency order, wired into =populate.sql= before the
trading layer. It is a second trigger of one implementation rather than a second
implementation, and it is idempotent. =fpml/fpml_populate.sql= is wired in ahead
of it, since it stages the business centres. After both run the tenant holds 250
countries, 217 business centres, 16 parties, 4 counterparties, 28 business units,
36 portfolios and 31 books, and =trades= populates.

*The order is not a preference.* It was derived by reading what each publish
function selects from, and what the target table's insert trigger validates. The
two give different edges and both matter.

| Step             | Depends on       | Established by                                  |
|------------------+------------------+-------------------------------------------------|
| Countries        | nothing          |                                                 |
| Business centres | Countries        | The publish function reads countries            |
| Parties          | Business centres | The parties insert trigger validates the centre |
| Business units   | Parties          | The publish function reads parties              |
| Portfolios       | Business units   | The publish function reads business units       |
| Books            | Portfolios       | The publish function reads portfolios           |
| Counterparties   | nothing          |                                                 |

The parties edge is the one a reader would miss. No publish function mentions a
business centre, so the dependency is invisible on the read side. It appears only
when the row is written, as a rejected party naming an unknown centre. *A
dependency enforced by a trigger does not show up in the code that appears to do
the work.*

Business unit types, book statuses and regulatory book types are prerequisites
too. They have no publish function and are loaded by direct populate scripts, so
they carry no ordering constraint here.

** Defect 12: the publish guard collapses every office onto one party

*Current shape.* Each publish function resolves a root party and skips when that
party already owns rows of the kind being published. The root party is the same
for all four Acme offices, so publishing the group's datasets first causes the
UK, US and Hong Kong datasets to skip.

*Why it is wrong.* The result is silent and wrong rather than an error. Calling
the four datasets in the obvious way publishes *2 books instead of 31*, reports
=skipped= for the other three, and leaves a database that looks provisioned.

The function does accept a =party_id= parameter, and passing each office's own
party makes all four publish. So the mechanism is correct and its default is
misleading: an omitted parameter silently narrows the result.

*Replacement shape.* The guard should key on the dataset being published rather
than on the party owning any row of that kind. A dataset already published is a
sound reason to skip; another dataset belonging to a sibling office is not.

*Worked around, not fixed.* The new populate script passes an explicit
=party_id= per office. That produces the right data and leaves the misleading
default in place for every other caller.

** Defect 13: two names for an instrument's identity

*Current shape.* Twenty-five instrument tables are keyed on =instrument_id=. Five
are keyed on =id=, together with =trades=, =swap_legs=, =composite_legs=,
=trade_identifiers= and =party_roles=.

| Keyed on      | Count | Examples                                           |
|---------------+-------+----------------------------------------------------|
| =instrument_id= |    25 | =callable_swap=, =swaption=, =fx_forward=                |
| =id=            |     5 | =bond=, =commodity=, =credit=, =composite=, =scripted=     |

*Why it is wrong.* This is defect 6 in a load-bearing position. Two names for one
concept mean a reader who learns the convention from one table is wrong about
another, and a consumer that resolves a reference by name resolves it wrongly.

*It has a demonstrated cost.* The population loader failed on
=ores_trading_composite_legs_tbl= for two sessions. Its author, having seen 25
tables keyed on =instrument_id=, mapped the child's =instrument_id= to a parent
column of the same name. The parent is keyed on =id=, so the reference resolved
to a value that did not exist, and the failure was recorded as a finding about
reference data before it was traced to the naming. *An inconsistency that costs a
consumer two sessions is not cosmetic.*

*How to address it.* Rename to one convention and apply it everywhere. The
majority is =instrument_id= and it is the more specific name, so the five
outliers should follow the twenty-five. The rename is mechanical in the entity
models and regenerates through the schema and repositories.

The child tables are the priority regardless of which name wins, because they are
where the mismatch causes a wrong reference rather than merely a surprise.

** The Equity Family

Every defect-9 table is an equity table, which is reason enough to look at the
asset class as a whole rather than at the five constraints. Doing so found a
larger problem than the constraints are.

*** Scope

Nine tables model equity. ORE defines 27 equity trade types. The nine tables can
store 16 of them.

| Table                 | Types | Which                                   |
|-----------------------+-------+-----------------------------------------|
| =equity_barrier_option= |     3 | Barrier, DoubleBarrier, EuropeanBarrier |
| =equity_accumulator=    |     2 | Accumulator, TaRF                       |
| =equity_digital_option= |     2 | Digital, Touch                          |
| =equity_option=         |     2 | Option, CliquetOption                   |
| =equity_position=       |     2 | Position, OptionPosition                |
| =equity_swap=           |     2 | Swap, WorstOfBasketSwap                 |
| =equity_asian_option=   |     1 | AsianOption                             |
| =equity_forward=        |     1 | Forward                                 |
| =equity_variance_swap=  |     1 | VarianceSwap                            |

*** Defect 14: eleven equity products cannot be stored

*Current shape.* Eleven of ORE's 27 equity trade types have no table that will
accept them: =EquityBasketOption=, =EquityBasketVarianceSwap=,
=EquityBestEntryOption=, =EquityDoubleTouchOption=, =EquityFutureOption=,
=EquityGenericBarrierOption=, =EquityOutperformanceOption=,
=EquityPairwiseVarianceSwap=, =EquityRainbowOption=,
=EquityStrikeResettableOption= and =EquityWindowBarrierOption=.

The trade type reference table names all 27, and =trades.trade_type= accepts any
of them, so a trade can be booked that no instrument table can describe.

*Why it is wrong.* This is not a backlog of tables not yet written. Nine of the
eleven are blocked by a structural decision rather than by absent work, and
adding a table per product would not fix them.

*** Defect 15: an equity underlying is an unqualified name

*Current shape.* All nine tables carry =underlying_name text=. It is not a
foreign key, no trigger validates it, and it carries no scheme, so the string
alone does not say what it identifies.

*Why it is wrong.* Two consequences, and the second is the larger.

The value is unconstrained, so one instrument can be written as =VOD.L=, =VOD LN=
and =Vodafone= in three rows with nothing relating them. Any question about
exposure to an issuer becomes a string-matching exercise. The name also omits
what pricing needs: an equity is identified by a ticker *and* a currency, and the
currency here lives in a separate column that nothing ties to the name.

More importantly, *a single column cannot hold a basket*. Five of the eleven
missing products are multi-underlying: basket options, basket variance swaps,
rainbow options, outperformance options and pairwise variance swaps. They are
absent because the column shape cannot express them. The one product that does
have a basket, =EquityWorstOfBasketSwap=, escapes through =basket_json=, a text
column holding an unqueryable list.

*Replacement shape.* This project already has the identifier this column should
carry. [[id:C3E053CA-0D4B-480B-9119-E11530160EC1][oresmd]] is a canonical, structured market data URI, implemented in
=ores.marketdata.api= as =oresmd_uri=, and an equity underlying is exactly what
it addresses:

: oresmd://equity/aapl?ccy=usd

The authority is the asset class, the path is the ticker, and the currency is a
query parameter, so the identifier carries what the name and the =currency=
column carry today and says which scheme it is in. It projects deterministically
into ORE's own quote key, =EQUITY/PRICE/AAPL/USD=, which is what the pricing
side needs.

An underlying is then a row rather than a column, so a basket is n rows:

#+begin_src sql
ores_trading_instrument_underlyings_tbl
  instrument_id     uuid not null
  tenant_id         uuid not null
  sequence_number   int  not null   -- basket order, or the leg of a pairwise product
  underlying_uri    text not null   -- an oresmd URI
  weight            numeric         -- null for a single underlying
  primary key (tenant_id, instrument_id, sequence_number)
#+end_src

A single-underlying option is one row, a basket is n, and an outperformance
option is two. =basket_json= is deleted and =underlying_name= comes out of all
nine tables.

*What this depends on.* =oresmd= is implemented as a domain type and no database
column uses it yet, so this would be its first persisted use. Two questions
follow that this page does not settle: whether the URI is stored as text with
validation in the trigger, or parsed into columns, and whether a resolved
identifier should be registered in a table so that two trades naming the same
underlying share a row rather than a string.

*** Defect 16: no observation schedule for equity

*Current shape.* Products that observe the underlying over time hold that
schedule as scalars. =equity_asian_option= has =averaging_start_date= and
=averaging_end_date=, =equity_accumulator= has =fixing_frequency=, and
=equity_option= has =cliquet_frequency=.

*Why it is wrong.* A frequency plus two dates describes a regular schedule and
nothing else. It cannot express an irregular fixing list, a schedule with
holidays removed, or an observation window that opens and closes. That is why
=EquityWindowBarrierOption=, =EquityGenericBarrierOption=,
=EquityBestEntryOption= and =EquityStrikeResettableOption= have no home.

*Replacement shape.* This is defect 1's schedule table applied to equity. The
same =ores_trading_schedules_tbl=, with a =schedule_type_code= of =fixing=,
=averaging= or =observation=, serves all four, and the scalar frequency columns
become a generated schedule rather than a special case.

*** What the repair looks like

Ordered by dependency rather than by size.

1. *The underlyings table, carrying an oresmd URI.* Everything else is easier
   afterwards, and five missing products stop being blocked. It needs no new
   master, since oresmd already identifies an equity by ticker and currency; it
   does need a decision on how a URI is stored and validated.
2. *The schedule table*, shared with defect 1 rather than built for equity. Four
   more missing products stop being blocked.
3. *Split the two exclusive-or tables*, per defect 9. Independent of the other
   two and can be done at any point.
4. *Add tables for what is then left*: the double touch and future option
   variants, which have no structural obstacle once the first two are done.

*What this is not.* None of this changes how a product is priced, and none of it
blocks the trade population story, since the 16 storable types are enough to
generate a realistic population. It is recorded because the population work
surfaced it, and because a population that silently omits eleven of a desk's
products is not the population anyone asked for.

** What the loader could not populate

When the loader was first run, five of the 41 tables rejected a fully populated
row. All 41 now load. The reasons divide into three, and only two were findings
about the model.

*** Three tables needed reference data that was never published

=trades=, =party_roles= and =composite_legs= failed because
=ores_refdata_books_tbl= was empty. That is defect 11, and it is fixed: the
publish step now runs during provisioning.

=ores_trading_trades_tbl= validates six references before it accepts a row, and
names each as a soft foreign key in its own comments.

| Reference          | Validated against               |
|--------------------+---------------------------------|
| =book_id=            | =ores_refdata_books_tbl=          |
| =portfolio_id=       | =ores_refdata_portfolios_tbl=     |
| =counterparty_id=    | =ores_refdata_counterparties_tbl= |
| =status_id=          | =ores_dq_fsm_states_tbl=          |
| =successor_trade_id= | =ores_trading_trades_tbl=         |
| =trade_type=         | Enumeration inside the trigger  |

A seventh rule is stricter than a foreign key could express: =portfolio_id= must
equal the parent portfolio of the book named by =book_id=. The two references are
not independent, so supplying a valid book and a valid portfolio is not
sufficient. They must agree.

*** One was not a defect at all

=ores_trading_composite_legs_tbl= continued to fail after the reference data was
published, and the cause was the loader rather than the model: it resolved the
parent's key by the wrong column name. That is defect 13, and the entry is kept
because the model's inconsistency is what made the mistake available.

*** Two hold two products and cannot carry every column

=equity_digital_option= and =equity_swap= are the exclusive-or cases of defect 9.
The loader now selects a product and writes nulls where that product requires
them, and both load. The defect stands: no row can carry every column.

*** A related finding: rows cannot be deleted

The tables are bitemporal, and an =ON DELETE= rule rewrites every =DELETE= into
an =UPDATE= that sets =valid_to= to the current timestamp. A delete closes a
row's validity and never removes it.

This is correct for the domain and it matters for population. A generator that
loads a population, and a test that clears one, cannot use =DELETE= to undo their
work. Removing rows requires suspending the rule, which is a privileged operation
and not something a test should do casually.

Any preset described as replaceable needs to say what replacement means here,
because the obvious implementation does not remove anything.

* Conclusions

The sixteen defects fall into four kinds, and confusing them would misdirect the
repair.

Defects 1 to 3 are *type defects*. A value is held in a type too wide for its
meaning, so the database cannot constrain it, index it, or store it compactly.
These cost performance and correctness together, and defect 1 is the only one of
the six whose cost is measured rather than argued.

Defects 4 to 6 and 13 are *integrity defects*. A relationship or a name the model
relies on is not declared consistently, so nothing the database controls enforces
it and no consumer can rely on it.

Defects 7 to 10 are *placement defects*, and they are the ones the loader found.
The rule exists and it is written in the wrong place. Referential integrity lives
in PL/pgSQL rather than in a foreign key, enumerations live in trigger bodies
rather than in the reference tables that already hold their values, a product
distinction lives in a check constraint rather than in a table, and a concept
sits under a name that means something else.

Placement is why these are expensive. A rule in a procedure cannot be queried,
planned against, deferred or generated from, and defect 7 shows the price: the
triggers cost a factor of about 25 on the insert path.

The single largest finding is that the model already knows the right answers.
Reference tables exist, the =_code= convention is established, the SQL types are
correct where the C++ ones are not, and the valid value sets are written down.
Each is applied to a minority of the places that need it, or is written somewhere
the database cannot use it. *The repair is mostly the consistent application of
decisions this model has already made*, which makes it a smaller undertaking than
the counts suggest.

Defects 14 to 16 are *expressiveness defects*, and they are the only kind that
loses information rather than discipline. A shape the domain has cannot be
written down at all: eleven equity products have no table, because an underlying
is a scalar and an observation schedule is a frequency. The others make the model
harder to trust; these make it unable to hold what a desk trades.

Two defects carry a measurement rather than an argument. Defect 1 costs 4x on the
load path and defect 7 costs about 25x. Defect 14 carries a count: 11 of 27.

* Recommendations

Repair in the order the dependencies require, not in the order of the counts.

1. Defect 6 first. It is a rename, it is cheap, and defect 5 cannot be done
   without it.
2. Defect 5 next, since it makes the =_code= convention true, and defect 4 then
   follows the same pattern for the remaining enumerations.
3. Defect 1 for the schedules, which is the change that carries the measured
   benefit and unblocks the fast population path.
4. Defects 2 and 3 last. They are the widest by field count and the most
   mechanical, and they touch the mappers rather than the shape of the model.

Two things should be settled before any of this is scheduled.

Check the deployed SQL schema against the C++ declarations. Every count here is
from the headers, and where the generated schema already declares a stronger
type, the defect is narrower than stated.

Decide whether these repairs belong to trade population at all. They were found
by this story and they are not its subject. The measured part of defect 1 blocks
the fast population path and belongs here; the rest is a change to the trading
domain model that deserves its own story, so that a population story does not
quietly become a schema migration.
