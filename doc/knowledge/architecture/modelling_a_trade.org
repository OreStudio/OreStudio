* Summary

Trades are a key concept in [[id:D04D3476-D7C5-3954-A33B-C641EBCB43F6][ORE Studio]]. It is modelled as four distinct entities
with separate responsibilities:

- *Product Type*: The class of contract that can be traded.
- *Security*: A particular tradable instance of such a class, where one exists.
- *Trade:*: An agreement struck on a product type and on a security, where
  there is one.
- *Instrument*: The economic terms of that agreement.

The relationships between these entities are as follows. A product type
classifies many securities and many trades. A security is dealt by many trades.
A trade holds exactly one set of economic terms.

Significantly, [[id:1CBDEA40-5FAE-4F04-BD21-2BB29172B5AA][ORE]] models this domain and its representation is the ground
truth. Our entities must then correspond to ORE entities, so that a portfolio
can cross between the two representations without loss.

This document describes these entities in detail, their relationships, the prior
art that was considered during their modeling and any other considerations
regarding the modeling process.

#+begin_note
The wider trading vocabulary — economic value, price, the bid, ask and traded
price, financial quotation, and the market maker — is defined in the [[https://mcraveiro.github.io/ore_studio/musings/trading.html][trading
musings]], which takes the same definitions from the same sources.

A *position* is not one of these entities. A position is an aggregate over
deals, with no identity of its own, and [[id:886AAFB5-62F3-4AA3-B325-386665AFF926][positions]] covers it.
#+end_note

* Key Entities and Relationships

The following UML diagram captures the key entities and their relationships.

[[proj:doc/knowledge/architecture/modelling_a_trade.png]]

Note that a trade names its product type directly rather than reaching it
through a security, because a product type may have no associated security
(/e.g./ in the OTC case). That is why the diagram carries an association from
product type to trade as well as the path through the security.

The next sections provide details of each entity.

** Product Type

Wikipedia defines a financial instrument as "a monetary contract between
parties", and reports the accounting definition from IAS 32.11 as "any contract
that gives rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or
equity instrument of another entity". A *product type* is a /class/ of such
contracts.

In other words, /kinds/ of contract recur, and a firm may support only a subset
of these. A trader picks the /kind of the thing/ being traded before entering
any terms, and a firm may only enable or disable the trading of a subset of
these. The risk engine can also only support a subset of the available kinds.

The entity holds the properties that belong to the class rather than to any
contract of it: a code, a description, and the asset class it groups under. It
is a closed list, so a trade whose type is absent cannot be booked. Within ORE
Studio it is modelled as [[id:2BB5F81F-836F-4A10-AF9F-E202777B880A][=trade_type=]], whose =code= column carries the
catalogue's identifier.

** Security

Wikipedia defines a security as "a tradable financial asset", and adds that in a
fungible security "all holdings of the security are treated identically and are
interchangeable". Fungibility is what makes the security an entity in its own
right: every holder of one unit holds the same thing, so the terms belong to the
security and not to any one holding of it.

That is to say, some contracts are defined once and traded many times, and every
holder holds the identical thing. For example, an issuer fixes a bond's coupon
and maturity, and two traders dealing that bond negotiate neither. Their trades
share terms that neither party chose. /Fungibility/ is the observation that all
holdings of the security are interchangeable, so the terms belong to the /thing/
held - the security - rather than to any holding of it. Without it, an issuer's
terms would be copied onto every trade in the issue, and the copies could
disagree.

A security is therefore one tradable instance of a product type: this bond
issue, this futures contract. One product type has many securities, and traders
deal the same security many times.

A *bond issue* is one such instance. An issuer sells a specific tranche of debt
under fixed terms — coupon, maturity, currency, size — and an ISIN or CUSIP
identifies it. [[id:810AE48E-E180-42C6-B4F7-FA81826F3588][Standard financial identifiers]] covers those schemes. The issuer is the
borrower: a sovereign debt office, a corporate, an agency, a supranational. A
central bank may run the auction, but it does not issue the government's debt.

Issuers re-open existing lines rather than always creating new ones. An issue
therefore reads best as /the security its ISIN identifies/ rather than as a
single sale event. [[id:9754A325-F6FB-4F44-AB56-660D980A3F11][Bond issuance]] sets out the mechanics, and [[id:6c67bf4c-ad4e-446e-a55e-d7aa43735903][=bond_issue=]] models
the entity.

** Relationship between Product Types and Securities

The relationship from product type to security is optional, and many product
types exercise that option. Two parties strike a vanilla OTC option between
themselves, and no official issue exists because nobody defines the option once
and trades it many times. A bond has an issue, a bond future has a contract, and
a swaption has neither.

We therefore model securities per product type as we reach each one, starting
with the bond issue. The absence is a fact about the product type, not missing
data for a later task to supply.

** Trade

Wikipedia defines a financial transaction as "an agreement, or communication,
between a buyer and seller to exchange goods, services, or assets for payment".
A trade is that agreement recorded. We model it as a [[id:73D2511B-0D9C-4D8B-96AC-7A7B1FD27F0D][=trade=]], and the trade id
identifies it. That id names the trade inside this firm only, and [[id:E080E32B-5E53-4AB7-903F-145D070C045C][trade
identifiers]] covers the regulatory codes two counterparties share.

The trade models the notion of an agreement being struck at a point in time
between two named parties, and it is what the firm controls. Confirmation,
authorisation, versioning, amendment and audit all attach to this agreement. The
trade's properties can be grouped into two:

- *Envelope*: the [[id:ae3884f8-f6b1-465c-bf24-ef072a70c9d7][=trade_envelope=]] models administrative properties such as the
  counterparty, the netting set, portfolios, book and desk, and any further
  fields the firm may attache. It places a trade in the organisation. These have
  limited variability by product type.
- *Economics*: The economic terms determine what the trade pays, to whom, and
  when, and vary by product type. For example, the notional, the direction, the
  payment schedule, the strike and the rate.

The two are read and may change independently: A trade may change portfolio or
book without any changes to its economic terms, and the economic terms may
change without any updates to the envelope. This categorisation is also
important with regards to [[id:74721BF2-2290-4AA7-8871-E215DA04351F][Confirmations and Deal Versioning]].

** Instrument

Within ORE Studio, the instrument concerns itself with the economic terms or
just /economics/ of a trade. The economics differ in shape, not merely in value,
between different kinds of contract: a bond's terms are a coupon, a maturity and
a redemption; a swaption's are an exercise schedule, an underlying swap and a
settlement method. No single set of columns holds both without leaving most of
them empty.

The variation is structural, which is why the economics are an entity per
product type rather than a wider =trade= table. However, the economic terms are
properties of one agreement: a notional, a direction, a schedule and a strike
all belong to that deal, and two trades on the same security carry different
ones. The relationship between trade and instrument is therefore one to one, and
the trade id identifies both.

* Prior art

There are several examples of market standard representations for trades and
related entities. The next section looks at prior art that was considered when
designing this model.

** Messaging standards

[[id:C7B36E6D-F30F-4E2C-BB5B-27B1D4516DF1][FIX]] and [[id:0FF6A2DC-EE01-4C3C-AF86-A0D08FD93925][MDDL]] meet this model only at the identifier. FIX carries an =Instrument=
component block that names a security by =SecurityID= with a =SecurityIDSource=
saying which scheme the value belongs to, and MDDL wraps any existing scheme in
a controlled vocabulary. Neither proposes a persistent model of a trade: both
describe a message on the wire. They matter here only because they confirm that
a security identifier is meaningless without the scheme that issued it, which is
the point [[id:810AE48E-E180-42C6-B4F7-FA81826F3588][standard financial identifiers]] makes.

** FpML

[[id:FCC3BBA5-DFA3-17F4-69B3-B7958C05B1F8][FPML]] covers trading, valuation, confirmation, novation, amendment, termination
and position reporting, and unlike FIX it specifies no transport: it is a data
representation only. Its relevance here is the shape rather than the content.
FpML represents a trade as a header carrying identifiers and parties, alongside
one product element chosen from many. That is the same division this model makes
between the envelope and the economic terms, and the same observation that the
economic terms vary in shape by product rather than in value alone.

Its limitation is scope. FpML addresses OTC derivatives, so it says little about
securities defined once by an issuer and traded many times, which is where our
security entity earns its place.

** The Common Domain Model

The CDM is the most directly relevant prior art, because it draws the same seam
this model draws and names it explicitly. FINOS maintains it with ISDA, ISLA and
ICMA.

CDM defines a product from three attributes: an identifier, a taxonomy that
classifies it, and =economicTerms=, described as "a parametric representation of
the future financial obligations". It then defines a tradable product as the
"definition of a financial product as ready to be traded, i.e. included in an
execution or contract, by associating a specific price and quantity", which also
binds exactly two counterparties.

The reuse mechanism is what makes the comparison sharp. CDM normalises
counterparty roles to Party1 and Party2, so that "the same product can now be
defined in a party-agnostic way and used to represent transactions between
potentially many different parties". A product definition is therefore
instantiated many times, which is the fungibility observation stated as a
modeling rule.

CDM appears at first to contradict the fourth observation, which holds that
nothing in the domain lets two trades share a set of economic terms. CDM lets
exactly that happen, by putting =economicTerms= on the reusable product. The two
agree once the seam is located precisely. CDM's economic terms are parametric
mechanics — payouts, date methodologies, termination rules — and carry no price,
quantity or party. Those arrive only at the tradable product. What this model
calls the instrument is the per-deal half: the notional, the direction, the
strike. CDM's reusable half corresponds to our product type and security, and
its tradable product corresponds to our trade. Both models therefore cut at
price and quantity, and differ only in where they draw the boundary of the word
"product".

** Vendor platforms

Calypso separates Trade from Product. A product, in its terms, is a financial
instrument that can be held or traded, defined once and traded many times, and
it manages product holdings independently of trades. Calypso's Product is the
definition — a bond, a future contract, a stock — which is the security here.

Murex builds a trade from Financial Contract Components, its elementary unit for
P&L and sensitivity, and those components cannot live independently. A Financial
Contract is the set of components dealt with one counterparty and processed as
one object. The public material does not settle how definitions are shared
between contracts, so the shape of a contract is established and its reuse is
not.

Neither platform holds an entity between the trade and the product definition.
Both descriptions come from vendor documentation rather than from a published
standard, and neither is cited to an edition or page, so they corroborate the
model without establishing it.

** ORE

[[id:1CBDEA40-5FAE-4F04-BD21-2BB29172B5AA][ORE]] is different in kind from everything above. The others are comparisons, and
this model may agree or disagree with them on its own reasoning. ORE is the
ground truth, because ORE Studio exists to work with ORE portfolios: a trade we
cannot express in ORE's representation is a trade we cannot price.

That gives the correspondence a status the other comparisons do not have. Where
FpML or CDM would merely suggest a better division, a divergence from ORE is a
defect unless it is argued for. Every entity above therefore has to map onto
something ORE expresses, and the one divergence this model carries is named as
such rather than left implicit.

*** The ORE Model

ORE names the product type with a =TradeType= element on each trade, whose
permitted values are the =oreTradeType= enumeration. Each product page in [[id:C5C95B29-2EF9-4DFC-9E04-428C41C1925D][ORE
Products by Asset Class]] states its =TradeType=, so the catalogue and the
documentation can be read against each other.

ORE models securities as =ReferenceData=, a list of =ReferenceDatum= elements.
Each element carries an =id= attribute, an optional =validFrom=, a =Type=
discriminator, and one payload chosen from a closed list: bond, bond future,
convertible bond, callable bond, CBO, bond basket, equity, equity index,
commodity index, credit index, currency-hedged equity index, credit, and
portfolio basket. A bond trade names its datum through =BondData/SecurityId=,
and a bond future names its contract through =ContractName=.

ORE divides the trade itself into the =Envelope= element and the trade-data
group, which offers one choice per =TradeType=. That division is the same one
the domain gives, which is why our model keeps it.

Both the reference datum payload and the trade-data group are choices
discriminated by type. An XML schema expresses a choice directly; a relational
model expresses it as a table per type. The two sides of the ORE model therefore
have the same shape as each other, and one modeling approach covers both.

*** The correspondence

| Our entity                         | ORE representation                                                                 |
|------------------------------------+------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| =trade_type=                       | The =oreTradeType= enumeration, and the =TradeType= element on a trade             |
| =bond_issue= and its siblings      | =ReferenceData=, as a =ReferenceDatum= with a =Type= discriminator and its payload |
| =trade=                            | The =Trade= element, identified by =Trade/@id=                                     |
| =trade_envelope=                   | The =Envelope= element                                                             |
| =bond_instrument= and its siblings | The trade-data group, one choice per =TradeType=                                   |

*** Where the two diverge

ORE carries no identity between its trade and its trade data, because the trade
data is a child element of the trade and needs none. Our =instrument_id= is
therefore a divergence from ORE rather than a reflection of it, and it is the
one place where the correspondence above does not hold.

* Additional Modeling Notes

The next sections contain assorted notes on the modeling process.

** Checking an attribute's placement

The entities and relationships come from the domain. Once they are fixed, one
test catches an attribute attached to the wrong entity: hold the entity fixed
and ask whether the value can still differ. If it can, the attribute belongs to
an entity further down.

- The *asset class* is the same for every bond trade anywhere, so it is a
  property of the product type.
- The *coupon rate* is the same for every trade on one bond issue, so it is a
  property of the security.
- The *notional* differs from one deal to the next, so it is a property of the
  trade.

A misplaced attribute fails in one of two ways. Attached too far down, it is
duplicated on every row that shares it and the copies can disagree: a coupon
rate stored per trade is rewritten on each deal in the same issue. Attached too
far up, it has one row where it needs many: a notional stored on the security
would hold only the most recent trade's size.

** Securities held without a trade

One case argues for a fifth entity, and it is worth answering directly: a
security the firm holds without a corresponding trade, for valuation, a
scenario, or a pricing exercise.

That security is a security, and nothing more. It exists whether or not anyone
has traded it, which is Calypso's "defined once, traded many times" with the
count at zero. We already model it that way: =bond_issue= exists independently
of any trade.

The genuinely hypothetical *trade* is a separate case, an exposure someone wants
priced that nobody has dealt. It has a product type, it may name a security, and
it holds economic terms of its own, so it is a trade like any other.

A hypothesis is not a product type, because it says nothing about what was
traded. A reserved product type would therefore answer a different question from
the one the hypothesis raises. The hypothesis is a property of the trade, where
=booking_nature= reads =hypothetical= rather than =actual=. Every trade then has
one shape, and the hypothesis is recorded explicitly rather than inferred from a
null value. [[id:03C12558-69AC-4925-AAE1-85FFF75B10BA][Counterparty scope and booking nature]] sets out that property.
