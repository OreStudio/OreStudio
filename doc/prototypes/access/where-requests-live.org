* Summary

The tenant's menu carries an item called *Requests*. An administrator reads it
and hears *my requests*; it is the opposite, the requests other people have
asked them to answer. A member never sees the word at all, because the door is
gated on =iam::roles:assign=, which is the permission that *decides* a role
request, so the thing they raised has no name of its own outside *My access*.

Three ways to put the word where it means what it says are drawn side by side,
for both readers. Nothing is decided yet: this is the question the journey
opened when it said the item is misleading, and it belongs to somebody looking
at the menus.

It is one file of mock HTML, CSS and JavaScript. It calls no service, needs no
build and no session, and switches variant and reader as query parameters.

* Detail

** The question

The menu is the client's structure and not a decision, so this is not about
entitlement: the server checks on every call whatever the menu offers. It is
about whether a person can tell, from one word, which of the two things they
are looking at — what they asked for, or what they are being asked to answer.

Three answers are drawn:

- *A, named for what it is*: the item stays top level and becomes *Role
  requests*. The smallest change, and it answers the complaint directly.
- *B, inside Access*: requests move under *Access*, beside *Roles*, so a request
  reads as what it is, an act on a role. The member's menu gains the same word
  for their own.
- *C, a landing page*: *Requests* becomes a place with a card per kind the
  person may act on, and the reader's own list under it.

** What it argues

| The menu today | This prototype | Why |
|----------------+----------------+-----|
| One word for two things | The word names who asked, or who answers | An administrator reading *my requests* has it backwards |
| A door only a decider has | The reader's own list has a name too | A member's requests live under no heading today |
| One queue, because one kind exists | A card per kind, or one item, chosen knowingly | The system is heading for several kinds, and the answer should be chosen rather than arrived at |
| A flat menu | A group, where the version drawn uses one | A request is an act on a role, and the menu can say so |

The third variant carries the cost of the first two honestly: with one kind of
request in the system, a landing page has one card, and a card that is alone is
worse than a menu item. Its value is that the second kind adds a card rather
than a menu item, and the question it asks is whether the landing page is worth
building before the second kind exists.

** What is real and what is not

The menus, their order and the permission that gates the door are the ones
=projects/ores.web/packages/web/src/shell/areas.ts= declares today. The counts,
the requests and the people are mock. The second and third cards of variant C
name kinds the system does not raise yet: a publish approval is designed in the
market-data journeys and is not built.

** Where the code lives

=doc/prototypes/access/where-requests-live/index.html=: one file, plain HTML,
CSS and JavaScript, no framework and no build step. The site build copies the
directory through =ores-deploy-prototypes=, beside the other prototypes.

Open it with =where-requests-live/?variant=a&who=admin=; the bar along the
bottom switches both.

- Variants :: =a= named for what it is, =b= inside Access, =c= a landing page
- Readers :: =admin=, who answers requests, and =member=, who raises them
