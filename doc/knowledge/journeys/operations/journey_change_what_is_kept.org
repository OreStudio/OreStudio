This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A system administrator turns a retention policy on or off and changes its period, and the change is recorded with the reason.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I want this store kept for a shorter period — or not swept at all — and I want
the change to be a decision somebody can look up later." The journey is done
when the policy states the new period, the system has recorded who changed it
and why, and the administrator knows when it will next take effect.

* Actor and trigger

The system administrator: an account holding the =SuperAdmin= role and acting
from the system tenant. The policy is a row of the installation, so no tenant
administrator can change one, and the screen lives in *Operations* beside the
store it bounds.

The trigger is a decision, not a symptom: a period that turns out to be longer
than a regulation allows, a store that grows faster than the disk, a report
whose outputs a team wants for a quarter instead of three months, or an
incident that makes somebody want a store swept sooner. It is also the way out
of
[[id:F7C1E75E-9F08-437B-80C0-7D50B945CA52][See what the system keeps]], where
an unbounded store is a finding with a link to here.

This journey writes. It writes one policy row, and it writes the record of the
change: who made it, when, and the reason, in the same bitemporal shape every
other entity uses, so the previous period is still readable after the change.

* Steps

1. *Choose the store.* The administrator arrives from the store list, so the
   policy is chosen rather than searched for. The screen shows the policy as
   the server holds it, and the store's size beside it, because a period is a
   decision about a quantity.
2. *Say what should change.* The system takes a period in days, a cadence in
   hours, and whether the policy is enabled. The period is the age past which
   the action applies; the cadence is how often the sweep may act. Nothing
   else about the policy is editable here: the target and the action say what
   the store *is*, and changing them is a different decision.
3. *See the change before making it.* The screen states what will change, in
   the words the record will carry: "Period 90 → 30 days; next sweep at 03:00;
   the store holds 186 MB written in the last day, so nothing is removed
   tonight." A period that removes nothing yet is a legitimate answer and the
   screen says so instead of implying the change failed.
4. *State why.* The change carries a commentary, and the system refuses a
   change that states none. The reason is what the next reader has when the
   question is "why is this kept for 30 days" and the person who decided it is
   not in the room.
5. *Record the change.* The system writes the policy with the administrator as
   =modified_by= and the commentary on the row, and the write is versioned like
   every other: the old period remains readable at its version.
6. *Read what the change did.* The screen shows the policy's next due sweep
   from the sweep's own schedule, and links to the runs of that policy so the
   administrator can come back and see the first sweep under the new period, in
   [[id:F6622109-4F52-4DE9-9C06-6B0EAA7C8D2E][Read the housekeeping log]].
7. *Turn a policy off.* Disabling states that the store is now kept for ever,
   and the screen says that in those words. The policy stays, so the decision
   can be read and reversed; nothing is deleted.
8. *Say an action cannot work.* Where a policy names an action the sweep does
   not perform, the screen refuses to present the change as effective: it
   states that the store will stay unbounded until the sweep can apply that
   action. A period that promises a deletion the platform cannot make is worse
   than no policy, because it reads as a decision that was taken.

* Screens and wireframes

One screen: the store's policy as the server holds it, the edits beside it, and
the record the save will write. The editor is not a screen of its own. The
prototype drew it as variant C, a two-pane list beside the editor, and the
review that signed the prototype off moved it into variant A: the form opens on
the store's own page, where the store, its size and its last sweep are already in
view, so the person choosing a period reads the quantity it applies to without
leaving the page.

* What is missing

- *The size beside the period.* Closed. The store's page states the size now
  above the form, and the bounds read answers it, so a period is chosen beside
  the quantity it applies to.
- *The set of actions the sweep performs.* Closed. The bounds read states the
  mechanism a policy names and whether it applies today, and a store whose bound
  the sweep cannot perform reads as unbounded with the reason, so the form never
  presents a period that would delete nothing.
- *A dry run.* Open. Step 3's answer — "nothing is removed tonight" — needs the
  server to say what its own sweep would find, and no read does. The page states
  the period, the cadence and the last sweep instead, and says nothing about
  what the next one will remove.
- *When the change takes effect.* The page states the period and the cadence and
  does not compute the next due moment: the cadence is measured from the last
  run, and the scheduler's own answer is the one to read.
