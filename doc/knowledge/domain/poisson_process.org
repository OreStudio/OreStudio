* Summary

The *Poisson process* is the point process whose
[[id:46CC91F8-3591-44EE-B19F-31CBC6D7AFA6][conditional intensity]] is a constant
$\lambda$. Events arrive at random, at a steady average rate, and what has
happened so far tells nothing about what happens next. The gap between two
events follows the exponential law with mean $1/\lambda$. It is the simplest
model of random arrivals, and it is the baseline against which clustering is
measured: a stream that is more bursty than a Poisson process of the same
rate needs a model that has memory, such as the
[[id:D72B9899-C7EC-4F27-BE17-89AE6FC18A7A][Hawkes process]].

* Layperson's mental model

Think of raindrops on a roof. The rain has an average rate, but each drop
ignores the last one. Sometimes two drops land close together and sometimes
there is a long dry gap, and that is all the variation there is. A gap that
has already lasted ten seconds is no more likely to end in the next second than
a gap that has just begun. That is the *memoryless* property.

* Detail

** The definition

With a constant intensity $\lambda$, the count $N(t)$ of events in $(0, t]$
follows the Poisson law with mean $\lambda t$:

\begin{equation}
P(N(t) = k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}\, e^{-\lambda t}
\end{equation}

Counts in separate time windows are independent. The gaps between events are
independent and follow $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$.

** The exact simulation

The exponential law has an inverse in closed form, so a gap needs one uniform
draw $U$: $\Delta = -\ln(U)/\lambda$. No rejection is needed. This is the
inversion method of the [[id:46CC91F8-3591-44EE-B19F-31CBC6D7AFA6][point process]]
note. Summing the gaps gives the event times.

** The test of dispersion

Mean and variance of the count in a window are both $\lambda t$. So the *Fano
factor*, the variance divided by the mean, is one. A real tick stream usually
shows a Fano factor above one over windows of seconds to minutes, because ticks
cluster. A measured value above one is the sign that a Poisson model is too
smooth. For a stationary
[[id:D72B9899-C7EC-4F27-BE17-89AE6FC18A7A][Hawkes process]] with branching ratio
$n$, the long-window Fano factor is $1/(1-n)^2$, which gives a way to pick $n$
from data.

** When the rate follows the clock

If $\lambda$ depends on the time of day, $\lambda(t)$, the process is
*inhomogeneous*. A busy session and a quiet night then differ in rate while
each tick still ignores the previous one. Thinning (see the point process note)
simulates it. An intraday profile for a feed is the case of this.
