* Goal

An exception belongs to a party or to the installation. The installation
publishes the calendars and their rules once, and each party adds the days it
closes: an HK party closes on days a UK party does not, in one installation and
one tenant.

* Acceptance

- An exception carries the party it belongs to, and an installation-level
  exception carries none
- A party reads the installation's exceptions beside its own, and no other
  party's
- A party's exception changes the days that party materialises, in both
  directions: it closes a day the rules open and opens one they close
- The calendar screens name the party an exception belongs to

The end-to-end test of two parties on the same date is not here. Until the
overlay lands ([[id:A898881E-B0A8-4E23-B717-DB7681AB2B18][Hold the calendar dates
once, in the system tenant]]) the dates store holds one set per tenant and names
no party, so two parties in one tenant still collide there and no assertion about
them can hold. The overlay is what makes the difference observable, and its test
is the one that proves it: a party's exception changes that party's days and
leaves another's alone.

* Analysis

Decided on
[[id:A54B0989-BFCB-45C6-80B7-279BF9843E78][Decide whether calendar dates are
stored or computed]] after the question of who owns a holiday was put plainly:
a holiday is a party's fact. Two parties in one installation trade on different
dates -- Hong Kong's calendar is not the United Kingdom's -- so an exception
counts for a party and not for a tenant, and the tree holds two levels: the
installation's, and a party's.

What exists today. =ores_refdata_calendar_exceptions_tbl= is tenant-scoped, and
the materialisation reads the caller's exceptions beside the system tenant's,
=where (tenant_id = caller or tenant_id = system)=. A tenant can therefore close
a day for every party it holds, which is the wrong granularity: the row cannot
say which of two parties it belongs to.

What the change is:
- A nullable =party_id= on the exception, null meaning the installation. The
  table's RLS keeps the tenant scope it has and adds the party scope, admitting
  the installation's rows and the party's own, as the scheduler's job-definition
  policy does for a nullable party.
- The materialisation takes the party it materialises for, so the days it writes
  apply the installation's exceptions and that party's, and no other party's.
- The screens that list an exception name its party, and the party is part of
  what the screen filters on.

The dates store is settled apart from this
([[id:A898881E-B0A8-4E23-B717-DB7681AB2B18][Hold the calendar dates once, in the
system tenant]]): the installation keeps one baseline of rule-derived days, and a
party's exceptions are applied over it at read. That task depends on this one
stating the party, because the overlay is per party.

* Result

An exception now names the party it belongs to, or names none, which is the
installation's. The row carries a nullable =party_id=, generated through the
domain, the entity, the protocol and the web protocol, and the exceptions a
party reads are the installation's and its own.

The read that states this is a custom repository method rather than the generated
finder, because the finder reads by tenant and a holiday is not a tenant's fact:
=read_for_party= takes the party and admits =party_id is null or party_id = the
party=. The materialisation calls it with the session's party, so a party session
materialises the installation's baseline with that party's closures and
openings, and a session with no party materialises the baseline alone.

Row-level security gains the party: a restrictive policy admits the rows that
carry no party, the rows of a party the session can see, and everything to the
system tenant. It narrows the generated tenant policies instead of widening them,
so a party's rows stay that party's business inside the installation.

Verified on a rebuilt database at fingerprint =6131c6d4de7f7354=: the table
carries its party column and three policies, =ores.refdata.core= passes 6247
assertions in 563 cases, and the pgTAP suite passes 109 files and 1022 tests.

What this task does not do: the dates store still holds one set per tenant and
names no party. Two parties in one tenant therefore still collide there. That is
the overlay's task, and it is the one that can assert the two parties differ.
