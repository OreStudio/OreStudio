#+title: Authorised reads
#+description: How a read decides whether to answer: every read checks its read permission unless this page allow-lists it, where a refusal travels, and why reading your own access and reading somebody else's are different subjects.
#+type: knowledge
#+level: cross
#+filetags: :knowledge:security:iam:authorisation:
#+created: 2026-09-30
#+updated: 2026-10-06

* Summary

A read is authorised by the service that answers it, per operation and per
resource, before any of the work is done. Every read checks the read permission
of the resource it answers about. A read that answers every signed-in member is
an exception: it is allowed only when the allow-list on this page names its
subject and the reason it is open. A read that is open and not on the list is a
defect. Reading your own access and reading
somebody else's are two different subjects rather than one subject with a
parameter, because a self read that cannot name another account is safer than a
self read that must compare one. A refusal is a payload: the response carries a
`result` whose outcome is `denied`, with a code and a sentence, because an empty
payload cannot be told apart from an account that holds nothing. This page states
the rule and applies it to the access reads; the
[[id:C650CBC9-FDED-4C68-981F-7902E296928E][NATS Entity Protocol]] carries the
response contract it leans on, and
[[id:3CAA394F-E84C-3434-331B-6E664F7E84D2][RBAC]] is the model underneath.

* Detail

** The contract a read obeys

Every operation answers with a `result`, and the `result` is the only place a
caller looks to learn whether the operation happened. The outcome is a closed
set — =ok=, =invalid=, =denied=, =missing=, =conflict=, =unavailable=, =failed= —
and =ok= is the only outcome under which the payload means anything. The type
exists: =ores::utility::domain::result=, which every generated entity response
already carries.

Three consequences follow, and each is easy to get wrong:

- A read that has nothing to say and a read that refuses to say it are different
  answers. An empty list is =ok= with no rows; a refusal is =denied= with no
  rows. A response without a =result= cannot express the second, so it has to
  lie.
- =denied= is not authentication. A missing, malformed or expired credential is
  refused before the operation is reached, in the envelope, and never reaches a
  payload.
- The caller is told, it does not ask. No client asks whether it may do
  something and then decides; it asks for the thing and reads the answer.

** Where the decision is made

In the service, before the work, and not in the client. A screen that hides a
control has hidden a hint, not a grant, and the database's row-level security is
the backstop rather than the guard.

There are two permission checks in the tree and they are not the same check.

| | =authorization_service::has_permission= | =ores::service::messaging::has_permission= |
|---+------------------------------------------+---------------------------------------------|
| Reads | the effective permissions from the database | the token's =roles= claim, which holds permission codes |
| Empty permission list | denies | denies |
| No permission list | — | allows: the context is the service's own, not a caller's |
| Bare =*= | allows | allows |
| Component wildcard =iam::*= | not handled | handled |
| Cost | one query | free |

The access reads use the **database-backed** check, and the reasons are the
resource rather than the cost. The token's list is a snapshot taken at sign-in,
so a role revoked during a session still passes; and the empty-list pass-through
means an account that holds no roles passes every check, which is an ordinary
state rather than an attack. A read that guards the rows a stale grant would
expose is the last place to prefer the cheaper check.

** Self reads and privileged reads

An account reading its own access is the ordinary case. An account reading
somebody else's is privileged. The two are separate operations with separate
subjects, and the difference is what the request *can say*:

- The self read takes no account id. The account comes from the authenticated
  actor, exactly as tenant and party do, so there is no field in which to name
  somebody else. Its safety does not depend on a comparison being right.
- The privileged read takes an account id, because naming the resource is its
  purpose, and it requires the read permission before it looks anything up.

This is the same rule the protocol already applies to tenancy — a privileged
operation is its own address rather than a widened ordinary one — and the same
reason: *there is nothing to override because there is no field to send*. A
single subject that allowed the caller's own account by comparison would work,
and would be one guard weaker: the day a handler forgets the comparison, the
subject reads anybody's access.

** Guarded unless allowed

A read checks the =:read= code of its resource, such as
=iam::account_contact_informations:read=, before it does any work. The check is
the default. A model, a handler or a subject does not ask for it; it is there
unless this page allows the read to be open.

A read may be open for one of two reasons only:

- *It is a self read.* The subject takes no account id, so the answer can hold
  only the caller's own rows. The account comes from the authenticated actor.
- *A member needs it before holding any role that grants it.* The member reads
  the role catalogue to choose a role to ask for, as
  [[id:DF0FBED2-59C1-43DE-945F-D8839884C38D][Ask for more access]] describes. A
  read that a member needs because of the role they hold is not open: that role
  grants its =:read= code.

So "every member needs it" is not a reason to open a read. Grant the code to
every role that needs it. An open read answers people who hold no role at all,
and it hides the read from the roles that model what each person may do.

An allowed read is allowed by its subject. Other operations on the same
resource stay guarded: allowing =iam.v1.roles.list= does not allow
=iam.v1.roles_versions.list=.

A service acting as itself carries no permission list, and the check passes.
A service that relays a caller's token is checked as the caller.

** The allow-list

These subjects answer every signed-in member. Each row says why. A subject that
is not in this table checks its read permission.

| Subject | Answers | Why it is open |
|---------+---------+----------------|
| =iam.v1.roles.mine= | The caller's roles, each with the permissions it grants and its assignment tail | Self read |
| =iam.v1.roles.list= | The tenant's roles: name, description and flags | A member chooses the role to ask for |
| =iam.v1.roles.get= | One role | A member reads the role to ask for |
| =iam.v1.roles.permissions= | The permission codes one role bundles | A member reads what a role grants before asking for it |
| =iam.v1.permissions.list= | The permission codes and their descriptions | A member reads a role's codes in words, and asks "Can I…?" |
| =inbox.v1.approval-requests.mine= | The requests the caller raised, and their decisions | Self read |
| =inbox.v1.notifications.mine= | The notifications addressed to the caller | Self read |
| =inbox.v1.notifications.unread-count= | How many of those are unread | Self read |
| =iam.v1.account_contact_informations.mine= | The caller's own contact record | Self read; the profile reads it, and =list_by_account_id= needs =iam::account_contact_informations:read= |

A row is added to this table in the same change that opens the read, with its
reason. Removing a row closes the read.

** Where the code is today

The rule above is the target. On 2026-10-06, 363 of the 367 generated handlers
that serve reads checked no read permission, because codegen guarded a read only
when its model set =:guard_reads: true=. Only =account=, =login_info=, =session=
and =tenant= set it. [[id:F061BC3F-17D5-46A4-80A7-DF05E7AE6286][Guard every read
the security documentation does not allow]] closes the gap and adds the check
that keeps it closed.

** One answer per screen

A journey that only reads wants the joined rows in one request, not a call per
panel. The first screen that needed this was the access group's, and the shape
it settled is worth stating because it recurs:

- one response per screen, carrying everything that screen renders;
- the permissions each role grants, attributed to the role rather than flattened
  into one list, so a person can see where a capability comes from;
- the assignment tail beside each role — who granted it, when, and why — which
  the junction table already stores and no subject returns.

The alternative — three thin reads that the screen joins — costs three round
trips and still cannot attribute a permission to a role, because the flat list
has thrown the attribution away.

** The access reads

| Read | Subject | Who | Needs | Answers |
|------+---------+-----+-------+---------|
| The caller's own access | =iam.v1.roles.mine= | any signed-in account | a session | the account's roles, each with the permissions it grants and its assignment tail |
| Another account's access | =iam.v1.roles.by-account= | a tenant administrator | =iam::roles:read= | the same shape, for the account named |
| One role's bundle | =iam.v1.roles.permissions= | any signed-in account | a session | the permission codes one role bundles |
| The old flat list | =iam.v1.roles.permissions-by-account= | retired | — | folded into the composed read, because a flat list is what loses the attribution |

The three that remain each carry a =result=, which the contract above requires of
every operation. The first two answer =denied= when the caller may not have what
it asked for, with the permission that was required as the code, so a caller can
tell a refusal from an empty account without reading a sentence.

One role's bundle needs no permission, and that is deliberate rather than an
omission: registration gives a person one role, asking for more is the journey
[[id:DF0FBED2-59C1-43DE-945F-D8839884C38D][Ask for more access]] runs, and a
person deciding what to ask for reads what the role grants. The tenant's roles
are listable by any member for the same reason, so gating the bundle would hide
the half of the answer that makes the ask informed. Both are on the allow-list.

** The divergence this page does not settle

The protocol says authorisation answers =denied= in the payload. Most generated
handlers do something else: they check the permission before decoding and reply
with an empty body and =X-Error: forbidden=, so the refusal travels in the
envelope and carries neither a code nor a sentence. The access reads follow the
protocol rather than the handlers, because they have to distinguish a refusal
from an empty answer and because a denied authorisation is an answer about the
resource, not a failure to reach the operation.

That leaves two mechanisms in the tree for one concept. Reconciling them —
either by moving the generated handlers onto the payload outcome or by stating
that an operation-level permission failure is an envelope failure — is its own
piece of work, and it is recorded rather than decided here.
