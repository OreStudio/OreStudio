This page documents a [[id:F44766FA-1E9C-416F-849A-E131101B2ECA][user journey]]: A tenant administrator corrects one of the tenant's own legal entities: its names and type, its identifiers, its contacts, and the countries, currencies, counterparties and business units it connects to.

A user journey describes what one person is trying to do, before anything is
built. It is a design document that outlives any sprint, so it names the
screens, the entity fields each screen shows, and the operations and messages
each step needs.

* Goal

"I want this legal entity to read correctly everywhere it is used: its name and
short code, the schemes it is known by, the people to reach for what, and the
countries, currencies, counterparties and units it connects to." The journey is
done when the party reads correctly and its history shows why each field
changed.

* Actor and trigger

The actor is the tenant administrator: an account with the =TenantAdmin= role,
acting inside their own tenant. The trigger is a legal name that changed at the
registry, an identifier that was reissued, a contact or a desk that moved, or a
wrong value found in a run or a report. The journey starts at *Parties*, or at a
link from any screen that names a party, and ends when the person leaves the
party.

The journey corrects a party that already exists. It does not create one and it
does not provision it: [[id:9AB6C90E-5338-435A-9559-4E426D612EB6][New party]] does
that. The distinction is load-bearing, because the party keeps its id, its
codename and its registration default across every correction here.

* Steps

1. *Find the party.* The person opens *Parties*. The system lists the tenant's
   legal entities: short code, full name, type, status, business center, version
   and the account that last modified the row. The person searches by name,
   short code or codename, or opens a party from a link elsewhere. The tenant's
   =System= party is not offered: =party_category= is not user-editable.
2. *Correct the party record.* The person edits the short code, the full name,
   the party type, the status, the parent party, the business center or the
   registration default. The codename and the category are shown read-only. The
   person states a change reason and a commentary, and the system writes the
   party with =must_match_version=, so an edit made meanwhile is refused instead
   of lost.
3. *Correct the identifiers.* The system lists the party's identifiers, one row
   per scheme, with the scheme's cardinality. The person records a new value
   under a scheme. Because the value is part of the row's key, the system
   retires the old value with a delete and writes the new one, and the scheme's
   =max_cardinality= decides how many values one party may hold. A change of
   legal name and a change of identifier do not read the same on screen, and
   this step says why.
4. *Correct the contacts.* The system lists one row per contact type: Legal,
   Operations, Settlement and Billing. The person opens a row and edits its
   address, phone, email and web page, and writes it with the row's own version
   as its precondition. The screen marks one row primary. See /What is missing/:
   the model carries no flag behind that mark.
5. *Set the memberships.* The system lists the countries and the currencies the
   party operates in, each with the junction read for this party. The person
   closes a membership the party no longer needs, or links another. Closing
   writes a closed version of the junction row; the country or currency
   definition is tenant-level and is untouched.
6. *Set the structure.* The system shows the counterparties the party can see
   and the business units it is made of. The person links or unlinks a
   tenant-wide counterparty, and reclassifies a business unit by choosing its
   unit type. A unit type carries a level, and the system refuses a child unit
   whose level is not strictly greater than its parent's; the refusal keeps the
   unit and its parent on screen so the person sees what broke.
7. *Review.* The system lists every change as one row: what changes, the value
   before, the value after and the operation that writes it. Nothing is written
   until the person confirms. The change reason and the commentary are written
   with every row, and the party write states the version it read.
8. *Confirm.* The system writes each operation. The party moves to a new
   version; the previous version is untouched. A child write, such as an
   identifier or a contact, bumps the party's version on its own, so a party
   write prepared against the old version is refused.
9. *Check the history.* The person opens *History*. The system reads every
   version of the party with the generic history request and shows the actor,
   the reason and the field-level difference from the version before. It also
   reads the party with its identifiers and contacts as they stood in that
   version's window, so the two kinds of change stay comparable after the fact.
10. *Revert.* The person chooses an older version. The system writes its values
    back with =refdata.v1.parties.put= as a new version, with a reason. There
    is no revert subject; history is append-only.
11. *Leave.* The person goes back. Every screen that names the party reads the
    corrected values.

No step waits on the server after its reply, and the journey keeps no state of
its own. A refused write leaves the record exactly as it was and the person can
try again. The journey is a run of screens over immediate operations, so it
needs no [[id:FDF3C079-4E18-4DB7-911F-7691AD1F5665][workflow instance]].

* Screens and wireframes

One screen, ten states. The first state is the party list, which the person
starts from and returns to. The other nine are the walk: a flat left rail with
eight steps, plus the history and the refusal as side states. Each step draws
the one view it needs. The party header, the short code, the type, the status
and the version, stays above the body so the person never loses which record is
open.

* Prototype

A clickable prototype is merged at
=doc/prototypes/parties/keep-a-partys-details-current/=, and it is the design record for
this journey. It is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript over mock data, with no
service behind it. It draws the same screens as the wireframe, and it adds the
three refusal variants the wireframe states in words:

- =list= — the party list, its search and its create action;
- =overview= — the party's own record, with the correction notice, the
  read-only codename and category, and the audit line;
- =identifiers= — the identifier rows, the scheme picker, the cardinality note,
  and the comparison of a name change against an identifier change;
- =contacts= — the contact rows, the primary mark and the full contact form;
- =memberships= — the country and currency junctions, what closing one means,
  and the gap it leaves;
- =structure= — the party hierarchy, the counterparty links, the business units
  and the unit-type level ladder;
- =review= — one row per change, with the before and after value and the
  operation, above the change reason;
- =outcome= — what was written and what changed as a side effect;
- =refused= — three refusals, selected with =?fail= =level=, =cardinality= or
  =stale=: a business-unit level broken, an identifier cardinality exceeded, and
  a stale party write;
- =history= — the version buttons, the field diff of the chosen version, the
  composite as-of read and the revert.

The design questions the prototype settles, and which this document carries:

1. *Is a correction a replacement?* No. The party keeps its id and its
   codename, and each correction appends a version. The write states the version
   it read, so a stale write is refused rather than overwriting a concurrent
   edit. That decision fixes the whole shape of the journey: there is no create
   form here and no destructive action.
2. *Do a name change and an identifier change read the same?* No. The legal name
   is one field on the party row; an identifier is a row whose value is part of
   its key, so it is retired and replaced. The identifiers step draws the two
   side by side to make the difference visible before the person acts.
3. *Is a membership an edit or a link?* A link. Closing a country or a currency
   membership hides it from this party and leaves every other party and the
   tenant-level definition alone, and it is reversible. The screen states this,
   because the action reads like a delete and is not one.
4. *How is a rule that lives in the database shown?* As a state, not a toast.
   The refusal keeps the unit, its parent and both levels on screen, so the
   person can see the record the change would have broken.
5. *Where does the party's own view of its children come from?* From the
   junction reads scoped to this party, one per panel, so a slow panel does not
   hold the others, and from the party write for the record itself.

The generic history request is the one =ores.history= defines: the subject is
derived from the entity type, and the reply carries every version's full field
render and the field-level diff from the version before. The per-entity
=refdata.v1.parties_versions.list= and =.get= also exist and return the raw
rows; the screen needs the diff, so it uses the generic request, and it uses
=refdata.v1.parties.composite_as_of= for the children as they stood.

* What is missing

Items 1 to 5 are the missing rows of the coverage table above. Item 6 is the
one =partial= row, the read the journey does not get whole. Item 7 is a gap the
coverage table cannot carry, because the operation exists and the failure does
not.

1. *A contact has no primary flag.* =party_contact_information= carries no
   =is_primary= column. The model gives one row per =contact_type=, so the rows
   are (Legal, Operations, Settlement, Billing) and nothing says which of them
   is the primary contact. The prototype marks one row primary as a client
   convention, and its review step records that choice with no operation behind
   it, so the mark is lost on reload. The candidate is an =is_primary= column
   with a one-per-party constraint, in the shape the party already has for
   =is_registration_default=. Until it exists, the screen keeps the primary mark
   in the session only and says so on the contacts step.
2. *A membership leaves no readable version.* The =party_countries= and
   =party_currencies= tables keep closed versions, but the generated protocol
   carries =list=, =.get=, =.put=, =.delete= and =.list_by_party_id= and no
   versions subject. A closed membership therefore leaves the panel with no
   on-screen record of when it closed or who closed it. The candidate is a
   versions read for the two junctions, in the shape the party's children
   already have. Until it exists, the screen shows the memberships that are open,
   marks the one being closed in this session, and gives the party's own history
   as the only trail.
3. *A refusal is the trigger's text, not a typed refusal.* When a unit type
   breaks the level rule, the business-unit insert trigger raises its own
   message, and the client can read it only as a string. It names no field and
   no rule that a screen can act on, so the person cannot be told which unit or
   identifier to correct without the message being parsed. The candidate is a
   typed refusal from the server that carries the field, the rule and the value
   it found. Until it exists, the screen shows the message with the unit and its
   parent beside it, as the refused state draws.
4. *An identifier row has no field diff of its own.* =refdata.v1.party_identifiers_versions.list=
   and =.get= return raw rows, so they show that a value existed at a version
   and not what about it changed. =refdata.v1.parties.composite_as_of= returns
   the identifiers as they stood in one version window, which is enough to
   compare two versions by hand, and that is what the history step does. The
   candidate is a diff for the child rows, in the shape the generic history
   request gives the party.
5. *There is no unit that writes a party and its children together.* Each change
   is its own operation, and the operations are independent: the party write,
   the identifier pair, the contact write and the junction writes are separate
   calls. The review step states each one, and a failure part-way leaves the
   earlier rows written. The candidate is one composite change for a party with
   its children, so a confirmed review is one transaction. Until it exists, the
   screen writes the party first and the children after it, and reports which
   calls landed.
6. *The business unit is not filtered by party.* =business_units_filter= carries
   =id_one_of= and nothing else, and the protocol has no =list_by_party_id=, so
   listing one party's units means paging the tenant's units and grouping them
   on the client. The candidate is the party filter, in the shape the three
   junctions already have.
7. *The business unit level rule has no refusal of its own.* The rule is real
   and the screen draws it, but the only way to learn it is to break it, as item
   3 says.
