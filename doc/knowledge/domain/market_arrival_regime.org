#+title: Market Arrival Regime
#+description: A market-wide factor on top of the per-series arrival processes — an exogenous calm/active state and an endogenous shared intensity that events feed — carried by one clock per run, with the rank-one result that keeps the cost linear instead of an N-by-N kernel matrix.
#+filetags: :synthetic:arrival:hawkes:regime:design:
#+created: 2026-10-25
#+updated: 2026-10-25

* Summary

A per-series Hawkes process explains why one series clusters. It cannot explain
why the whole market clusters at the same time, which is what a fast market or a
panic is. This note adds the missing layer: a market-wide factor that every
series shares.

The factor has two parts. A *state* =S(t)= says whether the market is calm or
active, and can be pinned for a stressed scenario. A *shared intensity* =C(t)=
rises when any series anywhere ticks, and decays. The state gives co-movement;
the shared intensity gives contagion. Together they make the kernel matrix
rank one, which is what keeps the cost linear.

* Why per-series clustering is not enough

Take N independent self-exciting streams. Each one clusters on its own events,
and each passes the mean-rate, Fano-factor and time-rescaling checks. Yet the
counts of two streams are uncorrelated, so nothing makes the desk panic
together. A simulator with the right per-series statistics and no cross-series
correlation cannot represent a fast market at all.

That is the trap the per-series work leaves: every test we wrote would pass a
model that cannot panic. The missing measurement is the correlation of counts
across series, not another per-series statistic.

* The intensity

For series $i$:

\begin{equation}
\lambda_i(t) = s_i \left( \mu_i S(t) + C(t) \right)
  + \sum_{t^i_k < t} \alpha_i e^{-\beta_i (t - t^i_k)}
\end{equation}

| Symbol | Meaning | Lives |
|--------+---------+-------|
| $\mu_i$ | the series' baseline rate, the long-run rate a config names | config row |
| $s_i$ | how much the market factor moves this series | config row |
| $S(t)$ | the calm/active state, a positive multiplier with long-run mean one | run |
| $C(t)$ | the shared intensity, in the same unit as a rate | run |
| $\alpha_i, \beta_i$ | the series' own jump and decay | config row |

$S(t)$ is exogenous: it depends on time alone, so a feed that reads it needs to
know nothing about the other feeds. It is a two-state Markov chain with rates
that give a chosen share of the time in the active state, or a single state
pinned by a scenario.

$C(t)$ is endogenous: every event of every series adds $\alpha_c$ to it, and it
decays at $\beta_c$. That is what makes a panic start and spread rather than
being switched on by a calendar.

* The rank-one result

Write the kernel matrix of the N series. The diagonal holds each series' own
$\alpha_i$. The off-diagonal holds the common term. Because every series feeds
and reads the same $C(t)$, every cross entry is the same process scaled by the
sensitivities, so the matrix is:

\begin{equation}
K = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_i) + \mathbf{1} c^\top
\end{equation}

It has rank one outside the diagonal. Two consequences matter:

1. *Cost.* The shared part costs one number, not $N^2$ kernels. Thinning on the
   aggregate stays O(N) per event: bound the total rate, draw a candidate from
   the bound, pick a series with probability $\lambda_i / \Lambda$, reject with
   the usual probability.
2. *Stability.* The branching matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(n_i) + \mathbf{1}
   m^\top$ with $m_i = \alpha_c s_i / \beta_c$. Its spectral radius is below one
   when every $n_i < 1$ and the common feedback $\sum_i m_i < 1$. Both are
   closed form, so the parameter rules can refuse an unstable set before
   anything is built.

An arbitrary kernel matrix, where series A excites series B but not C, is
strictly more expensive and is not needed for a market-wide regime. It stays a
separate concern for named pair-to-pair contagion.

* One clock per run

The per-series design lets every feed own a clock and sleep. That cannot carry
$C(t)$: a feed computing its own rate would need every other feed's events.

So the shared factor forces a scheduler. One clock per run holds the factors and
the per-series processes and decides which series ticks next. A feed stops being
a thread with a sleep loop and becomes a body the clock drives: =start()=
registers it, the clock calls its body, =stop()= deregisters it. The stop
semantics, the failure hook and the "one tick body" seam built for the
per-series clock survive; only the caller changes.

The alternative is to keep per-feed clocks and accept an exogenous factor only.
That is cheaper and still gives panic windows, but it cannot give contagion,
because no feed can see another's events. The choice between them is a choice
about what the simulator claims to model, not about effort.

* Where the parameters live

- Per series, on the generation config row: the mode, the branching ratio, the
  decay and the sensitivity $s_i$. One row per currency pair, per curve, per
  series, so a major pair and a curve may carry different regimes.
- Per run, on a market regime entity: the state machine and its transition
  rates, the shared intensity's jump and decay, and whether the state is pinned
  for a scenario. This is one object per simulated environment, not one per row.
- Per scenario: a pinned state and, later, a shock injected at a time, so a
  demonstration or a test can say "panic now" and every series answers.

* How to check it

- Cross-series correlation of counts over windows, which is the statistic the
  per-series tests cannot see. It must be clearly positive in the active state
  and near zero in the calm one.
- Occupancy of the state machine over a long run, against its transition rates.
- A pinned panic raises every series' mean count by its sensitivity and then
  decays back.
- The per-series checks stay: a market factor must not spoil the mean rate, the
  Fano factor or the time rescaling of a single stream.

* Sequence

1. Per-series arrival parameters: mode, branching ratio, decay on each config
   row. Needed whichever factor design follows.
2. The exogenous state and the shared clock, which is the step that changes
   ownership and makes panic representable.
3. The endogenous shared intensity, with the correlation test that proves
   contagion.
